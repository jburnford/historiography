# Indigenous history thinkers and earlier antecedents

Indigenous historical knowledge has many independent histories. The expansion of university programmes and decolonizing methodological writing in the later twentieth century is one part of that history. Earlier manuscript authors, public speakers, community narrators, genealogists and historians also interpreted the past and contested who could represent it. The project needs to show those practices and their different settings.

The earlier entry contained **15 contributors but only one separate thinker entry**, Linda Tuhiwai Smith. Its opening bibliography selected Walker, O’Brien and Smith, while “Older roots” supplied no concrete early dates. That combination made a much larger roster look like a field with one thinker and a late beginning. The local revision now has **36 roster participants, ten separate person entries and 26 approaches**. These counts include named community narrators and outside collaborators; they are not counts of people certified as Indigenous.

## Earlier historical practices

The following witnesses establish earlier historical writing and interpretation. Their dates concern particular manuscripts, editions or recordings. They do not date the beginning of the communities’ historical knowledge, and they do not establish a common line of descent into a modern academic field.

| Contributor | Selected date and witness | Historical significance and qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala | *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno*, manuscript c. 1615/1616 | The Royal Danish Library’s edition identifies an Andean historical manuscript addressing colonial government. Its Christian and royal framework needs its own historical interpretation. [Library edition](https://poma.kb.dk/). |
| David Cusick | *Sketches of Ancient History of the Six Nations*, checked second edition, 1828 | The preface describes assembling materials and translating them into English. The Tuscarora writer interprets histories that precede their printed form. “1828” identifies the examined edition, avoiding a disputed first-edition shortcut. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/06003795/). |
| William Apess | *Eulogy on King Philip*, 1836 | The address contests colonial historical judgments and Pilgrim commemoration through a Christian moral argument. It is an intervention in historical interpretation, rather than merely material for a later historian. [Selected primary text](https://voicesofdemocracy.umd.edu/apess-eulogy-speech-text/). |
| Wiremu Maihi Te Rangikāheke | Māori manuscripts written before 1854 | Genealogy and narrative connect historical knowledge to land. Grey used and altered material without acknowledging its author. Attribution changes who appears to have produced this history. [Te Ara biography](https://teara.govt.nz/en/biographies/1t66/te-rangikaheke-wiremu-maihi). |
| William W. Warren | *History of the Ojibway People*, first published 1885 | The publisher describes histories gathered from relatives and tribal leaders, translated for a white readership. It also retains the author’s nineteenth-century assumptions and limitations. [Minnesota Historical Society](https://shop.mnhs.org/products/history-ojibway-people-second-edition). |
| Chief Thunderchild and Edward Ahenakew | Narratives recorded in 1923; *Cree Trickster Tales*, 1929; *Voices of the Plains Cree*, 1973 | Oral narration, recording, literary writing and later edited publication are separate acts. Thunderchild retains his own contributor credit. The 1929 article is an actual interwar publication milestone. [Book](https://iportal.usask.ca/docs/VoicesOfThePlainsCree.pdf), [1929 issue contents](https://www.jstor.org/stable/i223502). |
| Pei Te Hurinui Jones | *Māhinārangi*, 1929; English historical manuscript by c. 1936; *Ngā iwi o Tainui*, 1995 | Interwar Tainui research preceded the posthumous volume. Te Ara describes both his Māori-language work and disputed reuse of an English manuscript by Leslie Kelly. [Te Ara biography](https://teara.govt.nz/en/biographies/4j11/jones-pei-te-hurinui). |
| John Joseph Mathews | *Wah’Kon-Tah*, 1932; *The Osages*, 1961 | An interwar work and a later history show an earlier Osage trajectory. The books have different genres; the later publisher description names oral and documentary resources. [Publisher](https://www.oupress.com/9780806117706/the-osages/), [original dates](https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=MA037). |
| Te Rangi Hīroa | *Vikings of the Sunrise*, 1938; *The Coming of the Maori*, 1949 | The existing roster’s Māori scholar now has an explicit interwar work. His ethnological and historical practice remains distinct from later decolonizing methodology. [Te Ara biography](https://teara.govt.nz/en/biographies/3b54/buck-peter-henry). |

These examples show why “the field emerged in the 1970s” needs qualification. A later academic expansion does not make earlier knowledge-keepers intellectually passive. Nor do early authors all express the politics or methods of late-century writers.

## Twentieth century historians and methodological arguments

Canada was the largest omission in the earlier selection. Several different interventions now appear together without being collapsed into one doctrine.

| Contributor or collaboration | Selected work | Contribution represented |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Olive Patricia Dickason | *The Myth of the Savage* (1984); *Canada’s First Nations* (1992) | Historical inquiry into European representations and a broad Canadian synthesis. The Métis Nation of Ontario recognizes her contribution; its obituary’s claim that she single-handedly founded the field is not adopted. The publisher’s 1997 edition date is distinguished from the original 1984 work. [MNO](https://www.metisnation.org/news/olive-dickason/), [publisher](https://ualbertapress.ca/9781772124545/the-myth-of-the-savage-and-the-beginnings-of-french-colonialism-in-the-americas/), [1992 record](https://languagemuseum.ca/books/canadas-first-nations-a-history-of-founding-peoples-from-earliest-times/). |
| Howard Adams | *Prison of Grass* (1975) | Métis historical interpretation joins colonial and class criticism. His doctorate and Saskatchewan appointment date to 1966, an exception to an academic chronology beginning only in the 1970s. [University of Saskatchewan account](https://teaching.usask.ca/indigenoussk/import/adams_howard_1921-2001.php). |
| Emma LaRocque | *Defeathering the Indian* (1975); dissertation (1999), p. 34 | Her later account explicitly relates the earlier work to 1974 research on stereotypes in history and social-studies curricula. Pedagogical criticism and historical representation are historiographical questions. [Primary retrospective account](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/bitstream/1993/1635/1/NQ45006.pdf). |
| Georges E. Sioui | *Pour une autohistoire amérindienne* (1989); author interview (1991); English translation (1992) | Sioui explains “autohistory” through Indigenous self-interpretation and transmitted traditions. His moral and philosophical account remains situated and attributed. Sheila Fischman’s translation credit and the different publication dates remain explicit. [Author interview, p. 77](https://www.erudit.org/fr/revues/qf/1991-n80-qf1224771/44778ac.pdf), [English edition](https://www.mqup.ca/for-an-amerindian-autohistory-products-9780773509504.php). |
| Blair Stonechild and Bill Waiser | *Loyal Till Death* (1997) | A Saskatchewan historical study adds regional inquiry alongside national synthesis. Both authors are credited; bibliographic inclusion does not accept every chapter’s interpretation of 1885. [Original publication record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Loyal_Till_Death.html?id=T9IUAAAAYAAJ). |

The Ahenakew example also offers a concrete method discussion. In his 1995 introduction, **Stan Cuthand** criticizes an editor’s exclusion of a story he translated. That is a specific argument about interpretation and publication authority. The graph therefore credits Cuthand separately from Ahenakew and Thunderchild. It also keeps **Old Keyam**, Ahenakew’s literary figure, out of the person registry. The checked introduction contains an inconsistent recollection about when Cuthand saw Thunderchild; that recollection supplies no accepted date.

In the United States, **D’Arcy McNickle** supplies an earlier historical synthesis, *They Came Here First* (1949), and an institutional contribution to creating the Newberry history centre in 1972. These complement the existing **Vine Deloria Jr.** and **Angela Cavender Wilson** entries. Wilson’s 1996 essay supplies a direct argument about tribal and family historians’ authority. Deloria’s existing inclusion remains bounded by publisher-description evidence; promoting his visible entry does not silently turn an unread chapter into passage-level evidence. [Newberry finding aid](https://archives.newberry.org/repositories/2/resources/333), [Wilson’s essay](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~rfrank/class_web/ES-114A/Week%203/WilsonHistoryAIQ20-1.pdf).

For Aotearoa, the existing **Ranginui Walker** and **Linda Tuhiwai Smith** remain distinct historians and methodological contributors. The new earlier Māori approaches do not create unsupported arrows into their work. **Jean M. O’Brien’s** existing Natick history also becomes visible as a separate person entry.

Hawaiʻi now has a distinct approach: **Lilikalā Kameʻeleihiwa’s** *Native Land and Foreign Desires* (1992) and **Haunani-Kay Trask’s** *From a Native Daughter* (1993; revised 1999). The publisher describes Hawaiian-language historical inquiry for the former and colonialism, sovereignty, racism and gender discrimination for the latter. Bishop Museum Press also names **David Malo, John Papa ʻĪʻī and Samuel Kamakau** as earlier Hawaiian intellectuals. They are important follow-up candidates whose individual works and dates need separate checks. [Bishop Museum Press](https://bishopmuseumpress.org/products/native-land-and-foreign-desires), [UH Press](https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/from-a-native-daughter-colonialism-and-sovereignty-in-hawaii-revised-edition/).

Australian coverage adds **Kevin Gilbert’s** interview collection *Living Black* (1977) and **Ruby Langford Ginibi’s** autobiography *Don’t Take Your Love to Town* (1988) alongside the existing Huggins collaborations and Rigney bibliography. The interviewer’s credit is distinct from the narrators’ knowledge; autobiography is distinct from transmitted oral tradition. [Government library record](https://www.vgls.vic.gov.au/client/en_AU/vgls/search/detailnonmodal?d=ent%3A%2F%2FSD_ILS%2F0%2FSD_ILS%3A554211~~0&h=8&ic=true&ps=300&qu=Aboriginal+Australians+--+Government+relations.), [ANU account](https://ia.anu.edu.au/biography/ginibi-ruby-langford-16683).

Andean coverage adds **Carlos Mamani Condori’s** *Taraqu* (1991), a catalogued historical study of Aymara land and political struggles, alongside the existing account of THOA’s collective historical work. “1866–1935” is the book’s historical subject period. This addition does not assign the workshop’s knowledge to Mamani or Rivera individually or establish a direct Guaman Poma genealogy. [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/hlas-bi96010895/).

## Qualifications to Claude’s list

The supplied list identifies substantial omissions, but it combines university careers, community history, linguistic and cultural scholarship, activism and life writing. Each contributor needs an appropriate role and work-specific reason for inclusion.

The dates for **The Indian Historian** require further resolution. Donald Grinde’s 1994 account and the Costo archive guide refer to a first issue in 1964. The guide also lists volume 1, issue 1 galleys dated winter 1967. These may describe different publication phases, but the checked evidence does not settle that explanation. Society incorporation and the start of a particular serial run should have separate records. No new founding-date arrow was added. The same guide distinguishes Rupert’s *Textbooks and the American Indian* from separately credited Jeannette editorial work, so a blanket joint-book attribution needs checking. [Grinde](https://www.historians.org/perspectives-article/teaching-american-indian-history/), [archive guide, introduction and item 012.007.001](https://static.lib.ou.edu/microformguides/Gale_793.pdf).

**Wicazo Sa Review’s 1985 foundation** is supported by its publisher. A founding role for each proposed person needs a separate institutional or primary witness before an edge is added. [Publisher history](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9781517919665/wicazo-sa-review-37-1-2/).

Nation affiliation, community participation, author credit and intellectual contribution are different assertions. The graph has added local author records and contextual contributions, with no new external identity match or binary “Indigenous author” property. Dickason’s contextual affiliation has an MNO witness. Other contested or insufficiently documented identity assertions have not been imported from the supplied list. Outside collaborators already in the field, including Cruikshank and Jacobs, retain their distinct positions.

## Remaining research priorities

The selection is substantially broader but still uneven. Canada needs Innu, Inuit and further Anishinaabe histories and French-language publication. Important candidates from the supplied list remain **Daniel Paul, Basil Johnston, Winona Wheeler, Arthur C. Parker, Charles Eastman, Muriel Wright, Joseph Medicine Crow, Rupert and Jeannette Costo, Clara Sue Kidwell, Donald Fixico, Devon Mihesuah, Veronica Velarde Tiller, Elizabeth Cook-Lynn, Mary Kawena Pukui and Gordon Briscoe**. These remain candidates, not checked production additions. The next pass should identify a particular work, verify its original date and credit, and read the passage supporting the proposed historical or methodological contribution.

More passage-level work is also needed for some existing or newly catalogued books and for Rigney’s 1999 article. Selected descriptive evidence establishes a bibliography; detailed influence and critique claims require passages. Post-2000 authors and works belong to a separate extension review. Indigenous history’s coverage remains through 2000 in this revision.

The publication chart has a different scope. **Ethnohistory is an interlocutor venue, and its counts include outside scholarship.** Its deposited records and recovered long-article candidates cannot identify Indigenous authorship or date the beginning of Indigenous historical knowledge. Missing early chart values also reflect incomplete deposits and pagination. The new written antecedents are historical selections, not fabricated additions to the publication series.
