## 2. A dated narrative, 1900s–2026

Regional strands are kept separate. US developments are not treated as the template for everyone else.

### 2.1 Before ethnohistory: salvage anthropology and Indigenous historians

In the early twentieth century, most academic writing about Indigenous pasts in North America and Australasia was anthropological. Much of it was framed as recording "vanishing" cultures. I did not re-read a source for this today; it is background knowledge, and it matches the atlas's `anthropology` entry.

Indigenous historical practice did not start with the academy:

- Te Rangi Hīroa's *The Coming of the Maori* (1949) [atlas] is a Māori scholarly trajectory that predates ethnohistory's institutional form.
- A WMQ/EAL forum abstract says Native scholars and activists "nearly two centuries ago" called for histories that emphasized Native humanity and engaged Indigenous intellectual traditions [snippet, Mt. Pleasant, Wigginton and Wisecup 2018].
- Piker and Wulf (2020) likewise place NAIS within calls for "fuller engagement" reaching back to the nineteenth century [read].

### 2.2 1946–1966: claims, the Ohio Valley conference and naming the field (US)

The American Society for Ethnohistory's own history [read, society page] gives this sequence:

- The society grew out of research for "the Indian Claims Act of 1946."
- In the mid-1950s, anthropological and historical reports prepared as evidence in claims were brought together at the first Ohio Valley Historic Indian Conference.
- That conference became the American Indian Ethnohistoric Conference, affiliated with Indiana University.
- In 1966 it became the American Society for Ethnohistory.
- *Ethnohistory* has been published quarterly since 1954. The Ohio Valley–Great Lakes archive was assembled between 1953 and 1966.

The atlas already records the Great Lakes–Ohio Valley project, begun in June 1953 with Justice Department funding [atlas].

Harkin (2010) [abstract] makes four points relevant here:

- Ethnohistory "specifically arose out of the study of American Indian communities in the era of the Indian Claims Commission."
- It was pragmatic rather than theoretical.
- Its practitioners testified "both in favor of and against claims."
- Women were unusually prominent. The field later expanded, "especially to Latin America," and to Melanesia.

Harris (New Dictionary of the History of Ideas) [read, a reference entry] gives the conference founding date differently from the ASE page. **This dating conflict is unresolved**; the ASE page is preferred for now.

**Regional variant: Mexico.** The Escuela Nacional de Antropología e Historia says that "la escuela mexicana de antropología creó en 1953 un área de especialización" to work with Mesoamerican documents against ethnographic data. That became the *Licenciatura en Etnohistoria* in 1973. Its stated aim is to clarify "el mundo del 'otro'" [read, institutional self-description]. So Mexican *etnohistoria* is a degree-granting anthropological discipline, not a litigation by-product. Its self-definition still frames Indigenous people through an "other" framing.

### 2.3 1960s–1980s: Red Power, Native studies, the "new Indian history" and settler-state claims

- **1961.** Lurie's methodological statement [atlas] argues against confining ethnohistory to supposedly exotic peoples.
- **1969.** Deloria's *Custer Died for Your Sins* criticizes social scientists and institutions [atlas; its chapters are still unread in the atlas].
- **1969, first departments.** Trent University's Indian-Eskimo Studies Program was "the first university in Canada, and only the second in North America" to establish such a department. It was co-founded by Harvey McCue of the Georgina Island First Nation and President Tom Symons [read, Trent news]. The University of Minnesota's Department of American Indian Studies was approved in June 1969 [snippet; the UMN page returned 403].
- **1972.** The Newberry Library's Center for the History of the American Indian opened, with D'Arcy McNickle as first director and an advisory council with a majority of American Indian members [snippet].
- **The "new Indian history."** The canonical markers are:
  - Axtell's "The Ethnohistory of Early America: A Review Essay" (WMQ 35.1, 1978) [metadata];
  - Merrell's "Some Thoughts on Colonial Historians and American Indians" (WMQ 46.1, 1989) [metadata].

  The label itself matters. **In early-American history, "ethnohistory" was the name historians used for the new, Indian-centred history.** In this subfield the two terms were effectively fused.
- **1994, a Native critique.** Grinde's AHA *Perspectives* piece [read] quotes Axtell: "historians need not feel unduly sensitive about their lack of personal research among contemporary tribal cultures." Grinde calls this a double standard found in no other historical field. He argues that much "American Indian history" was really the history of Indian–white relations or of bureaucracies, and he rejects the "essentialism" charge made against Native scholars.
- **1985.** *Wicazo Sa Review* is founded "to assist Indigenous peoples of the Americas in taking possession of their own intellectual and creative pursuits" [description, UMN Press]. Co-founders Elizabeth Cook-Lynn, Roger Buffalohead, Beatrice Medicine and William Willard are named in a [snippet].
- **Andes, 1970s–1980s.** Ramos (2018) [read, web full text; summary checked, not every page]:
  - *etnohistoria andina* became the customary term in the 1970s (Murra, Rostworowski, Zuidema);
  - three SSRC conferences (Sucre 1983, Madison 1984, Quito 1986) framed a "nueva etnohistoria" of Indigenous strategy and resistance;
  - Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui took part in the 1981 SSRC planning meeting. Ramos notes she gives little analysis of Indigenous scholars' role.
- **Andes, THOA.** Rivera's 1987 account of THOA's Aymara-directed oral history [atlas] is a parallel Indigenous-led practice.
- **Aotearoa New Zealand.** The Waitangi Tribunal was created in 1975. The 1985 amendment backdated its jurisdiction to 1840, which "provided unprecedented opportunities for professional historians outside the academy." Historians work for Crown, iwi and Tribunal alike [read, Crocker 2014, pp. 106–108]. This is a claims-driven historiography like the ICC's, but it was not called "ethnohistory." Oliver and Byrnes (2004) criticized its "presentist," juristic history [snippet]. Johnson (2023) [abstract] reads the Tribunal as producing "an alternative historiography" of partnership, and research ethics as having become "governmentality."
- **Australia.** The founding statement of *Aboriginal History* (vol. 1, 1977) [read, primary] "aims to present articles and information in the field of **Australian ethnohistory**, particularly the post-contact history of the Aborigines and Torres Strait Islanders." Charles Perkins sat on the 1977 national committee. The journal now describes itself as "a flagship of the field of Australian Aboriginal history" [read, ANU]. **This shows directly that the label shifted from ethnohistory to Aboriginal/Indigenous history within one venue.** When the shift happened is still to be established.

### 2.4 1990s: Indigenous historians' critiques and the courtroom

- **1991.** Krech's "The State of Ethnohistory" [metadata; quoted only through Harris, a reference entry] warns against applying "a special name to the history of the culturally distant Other."
- **1991–1993, Andes.** Starn's "Missing the Revolution" (1991) and Molinié (1993) attacked the essentialism of "lo andino." Molinié's words were "más soñada que demostrada." Ramos treats this reflexivity as part of the field's development, not its decline [read, Ramos 2018].
- **1996.** The *American Indian Quarterly* special issue on writing about American Indians. Wilson asks historians to take collected accounts back to knowledgeable elders and to respect authority over what may be shared [atlas, pp. 3–5]. Fixico says Native history "has mainly been researched and written from a non-Indian... perspective" [snippet of the abstract].
- **1997, Canada.** In *Delgamuukw*, the Supreme Court of Canada held that oral histories must be "placed on an equal footing" with documentary evidence (para. 87). This reversed the trial judge's treatment of Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en oral narratives as the products of "primitive" societies [read, B. G. Miller 2011, pp. 3–4, 9].
  - **Critical for the relationship:** a Crown expert, "drawing heavily on the work of Africanist historians, especially Jan Vansina," used the "floating gap" to argue that oral histories are unreliable. Oral narratives are "routinely said by the Crown to be weak evidence" [read, Miller pp. 10–11].
  - The same comparative oral-tradition method the atlas links to ethnohistory (`edge_751`) was thus used *against* Indigenous claimants. Ray, a historian and veteran expert witness, describes adversarial cross-examination of experts [read, Miller p. 7].
- **1999.** Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* [atlas, intro pp. 1–5]. Rigney's Indigenist methodology [atlas, bibliographic only].

### 2.5 2000s–2026: Indigenous studies, relationality, sovereignty and a "new ethnohistory"

- **NAISA.** It began with exploratory meetings at Oklahoma (2007) and Georgia (2008) and was incorporated in 2009 [read, NAISA].
  - A contemporary account by Scott Richard Lyons dates the association's official coming into existence to 11 April 2008 at Athens. It names a steering committee of Warrior, Weaver, O'Brien, Lomawaima, Hernández-Ávila and Kauanui [read, list message].
  - **Figures conflict:** NAISA says 350 attended in 2007; Lyons says "Three hundred showed up."
  - NAISA calls itself "international" and emphasizes "Indigenous community-driven scholarship" [read].
- **Relational and land-based paradigms.**
  - Shawn Wilson (Opaskwayak Cree), *Research Is Ceremony* (2008): "Relationships don't just shape Indigenous reality, they are our reality." Fernwood describes the paradigm as shared by Indigenous scholars in Canada and Australia [description].
  - Kovach, *Indigenous Methodologies* (2009) [snippet].
  - Coulthard, *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014), and "grounded normativity" [snippet].
- **"Writing back."** Susan A. Miller (Seminole), "Native America Writes Back: The Origin of the Indigenous Paradigm in Historiography," *Wicazo Sa Review* 23.2 (2008) [metadata]. Miller and Riding In, eds., *Native Historians Write Back* (2011): Native scholars on their own nations' histories, grounded in "ancestral wisdom" [description].
- **US contest.** The 2018 WMQ/EAL forum asked early Americanists to "complete the turn" to NAIS materials and methods [metadata/snippet]. The 2020 *AHR* exchange began when Silverman challenged NAIS's alleged credulity toward Indigenous sources in Brooks's and DeLucia's books; four scholars replied [abstract, OUP]. The respondents, reportedly DeLucia, Philip Deloria, Mt. Pleasant and O'Brien, come from a [snippet].
  - **This is the live form of the old question.** Is Indigenous history judged by the source criticism of ethnohistory and the discipline, or by community-engaged NAIS protocols? The atlas should present it as a debate, not a settled succession.
- **Canada: ethnohistory reconstituted.** *Towards a New Ethnohistory* (2018) grew out of the Stó:lō Ethnohistory Field School, held biennially since 1998. Its introduction is titled "Decolonizing Ethnohistory" (pp. 1–38) [metadata]. The press says community members "identify the research questions, host the researchers... and participate meaningfully in the analysis" [description]. The Sims review [read] reports Gaudry's epilogue argument that outside researchers should enable communities to do their own research.
- **Indigenous-centred infrastructure.** Shekon Neechie is a site for Indigenous historians. It says it exists because history sites' boards and contributors reflect "the relative homogeneity and whiteness of the historical profession" [read]. Its founding date was not found.
- **Ethnohistory now.**
  - The journal describes its scope as anthropological and historical approaches "around the world, but with a particular emphasis on the Americas," on "indigenous, diasporic, and minority peoples" [read, Duke/ASE].
  - Riehm et al. (2019), sampling 1954–2013, find North America dominant, with growth in Latin America and beyond [abstract].
  - The 2023 forum (*Ethnohistory* 70.2) places the field's "birth" at the intersection of "Indigenous rights activism and state law." It includes Oaxaca customary law (Yannakakis), Colombian land titles researched as lawyer-historian (Lopera-Mesa), Aotearoa (Johnson) and African human-rights epistemologies (Ibhawoh) [abstracts via Crossref].
  - Litigation remains the field's defining setting, now worldwide.
- **Wider audience.** Blackhawk (Te-Moak Western Shoshone), *The Rediscovery of America* (Yale, 2023), won the National Book Award [snippet]. It is a synthesis arguing that Indigenous peoples are central to US history.
- **Recent (last 24 months).** *The Raymond D. Fogelson Papers* (2026) reprints "The Ethnohistory of Events and Nonevents" [metadata]. Its "ethno-ethnohistory" idea, taking Indigenous conceptions of history seriously, is a bridge concept worth reading.

## 3. The relationship between ethnohistory and Indigenous history

The evidence supports **"all of these"**: predecessor, method used within, contested tradition, and continuing parallel field. Which one holds depends on region and date. It supports none of them as a single genealogy.

1. **Method versus field.** Ethnohistory is used both ways:
   - as a **method**: documentary criticism informed by ethnology, plus oral tradition, archaeology and language (Lurie; Harris quoting Carmack's caution about the label);
   - as a **field or discipline**: a society (1966), a journal (1954), a Mexican degree (1973) and international congresses (Buenos Aires, 1989).

   Indigenous historians use the method without joining the field.
2. **Origins in settler legal and academic practice.** In the US it was born of claims litigation, with experts on both sides (Harkin). In Canada the Crown used comparative oral-tradition method against claimants (Miller). This is not a neutral ancestry. The atlas's claims-archives strand should say explicitly that expertise ran both ways.
3. **Predecessor, but only as a label, and only in some places.** In Australia, "Australian ethnohistory" (1977) became "Aboriginal history." In early-American history, "ethnohistory" was the label for what became Native/Indigenous history. In both cases, **ethnohistory is a historiographical predecessor of the academic *field name*, not of Indigenous historical knowledge.** The latter has independent roots (Te Rangi Hīroa; THOA; tribal and family historians).
4. **Contested tradition.** Indigenous scholars' critiques target three things:
   - "outsider" interpretation cut off from community authority (Wilson 1996);
   - disciplinary double standards (Grinde 1994, on Axtell);
   - the othering implicit in the prefix "ethno-". Krech concedes this point; the ENAH self-description ("el mundo del 'otro'") embodies it.
5. **Continuities.** Both value oral tradition, interdisciplinarity and a long time-depth across contact. Both have been shaped by litigation. Collaboration such as Cruikshank's with Sidney, Smith and Ned, and the Stó:lō field school sits squarely in both, which is why the atlas already lists these people in both entries.
6. **Reform from inside.** "New ethnohistory" (Stó:lō, 2018) and the 2023 forum show ethnohistory taking up community-engaged and decolonizing protocols. It is a field reshaped by Indigenous studies, not only superseded by it.
7. **The meaning varies by region.**
   - **US:** claims-born interdisciplinary field.
   - **Mexico:** a degree-granting anthropological discipline centred on Mesoamerican documents.
   - **Andes:** *etnohistoria andina*, with its own internal critique of "lo andino."
   - **Australia:** an early name for Aboriginal history.
   - **Aotearoa:** claims historiography, not called ethnohistory.
   - **Africa:** a contested methodological label (Vansina; a 1970s debate over positivism is noted but unread).

## 4. Recommendations for editorial review

### 4a. Atlas entries and edges

1. **Keep `edge_749` (comparison) but rewrite it** so the comparison names shared methods (oral tradition, interdisciplinarity, litigation) as well as the authority question. Add `wilson_indian_history` and `grinde` (new source) to its sources. Proposed map label: "Shared methods, contested authority."
2. **Add a directed critique `indigenous → ethnohistory`.** Relationship: "Native historians challenge ethnohistorians' outsider authority and disciplinary double standards." Sources: Grinde 1994 [read] (a direct response to an ethnohistorian's statement) and Wilson 1996 [atlas]. Evidence note: the critique targets specific practices, not every ethnohistorian. Grinde and Wilson do not speak for all Indigenous historians.
3. **Do not add an `influence` or "predecessor" edge `ethnohistory → indigenous`.** It would contradict the `indigenous` scope note and flatten point 3 above. Put the label predecessorship in scope notes and strands instead:
   - **`ethnohistory`, `claims_archives` strand:** add "experts testified for and against claims (Harkin 2010); in Canadian litigation the Crown used Vansina-derived oral-tradition criticism against claimants (Miller 2011, pp. 10–11)."
   - **`ethnohistory` scope note:** add the regional meanings (§3.7), the 1977 *Aboriginal History* usage and the ENAH 1953/1973 dates. Suggested date_label additions: "Mexico ENAH specialisation 1953 · ASE name 1966 · Mexican licenciatura 1973."
   - **`indigenous` scope note or new strands:** a Native studies institutional strand (Trent and Minnesota 1969; *Wicazo Sa Review* 1985) and a Canadian claims/oral-evidence strand (*Delgamuukw* 1997). Suggested date_label: "Older roots · Native studies departments from 1969 · 1970s–90s growth · coverage through 2000."
4. **Consider one bounded contribution `ethnohistory → indigenous`**, but only if it names a documented practice. Example: "Collaborative life-history method (Cruikshank with Sidney, Smith and Ned)." Even this may be better modelled as the existing shared strand than as a new edge.
5. **For the 2001–2026 extension layer** (not the 1920–2000 graph): a NAISA 2007–09 milestone, Wilson 2008/Kovach 2009/Coulthard 2014 as relational and land-based methods, Stó:lō *Towards a New Ethnohistory* 2018 as `indigenous → ethnohistory` contribution candidate, and the 2020 *AHR* exchange as a named debate.

1. **Journal *Ethnohistory*: map to both, with ethnohistory primary.**

## 5. Open questions

- **When did *Aboriginal History* stop using "ethnohistory"?** Read editorials from the 1980s–2000s. The same question applies to the "Native American history" versus "ethnohistory" labels in WMQ.
- **Deloria 1969:** the "Anthropologists and Other Friends" chapter is still unread. The atlas already flags this.
- **The founding date of the ASE conference** is 1954 on the society page and differs in Harris. The society page is preferred until checked against Harkin's full text.
- **Africa:** the use of "ethnohistory" in African history and the 1970s *Cahiers d'Études Africaines* debate are unread [snippet only]. Do not generalize.
- **French and Spanish traditions:** Wachtel's *La vision des vaincus*; Mexican *etnohistoria*'s own critiques (the ENAH mentions a "crisis de crecimiento"). Portuguese-language Brazil and Nordic Sámi history are unexamined.
- **Indigenous-language historiography** (for example te reo Māori scholarship, Aymara THOA publications) may be thinly digitized, not absent.
- **Should the atlas label "Indigenous history & decolonizing methods" be split** from Native/Indigenous studies as an institutional field? That question is beyond this brief.
