## 1. Patrick Wolfe, "Settler colonialism and the elimination of the native", *Journal of Genocide Research* 8:4 (December 2006): 387–409

**Citation.** DOI 10.1080/14623520601056240 (Crossref: issued 2006-12, pages 387–409, 4,510 Crossref citations; OpenAlex counts 6,460 citing works and marks it "bronze" open access on the publisher's site). **Read status: read in full** in the Wayback capture of the publisher's full-text page (notes 1–80 included; no page breaks in the capture). Page numbers below are "as given by Kauanui 2016" (notes 3–6: pp. 387, 388, 390, 403), "as given by Tuck and Yang 2012" (p. 387) or "as given by Snelgrove et al. 2014" (p. 388); otherwise the article's own section breaks (five asterisked sections after the opening) are used.

**The argument, in his words.**
- Opening: "The question of genocide is never far from discussions of settler colonialism. Land is life—or, at least, land is necessary for life." "In this article, I shall begin to explore, in comparative fashion, the relationship between genocide and the settler-colonial tendency that I term the logic of elimination. I contend that, though the two have converged … they should be distinguished. Settler colonialism is inherently eliminatory but not invariably genocidal." (opening; p. 387 per Kauanui.) Note 1 cites his own "Nation and miscegeNation" (*Social Analysis* 36, 1994, pp. 93–152) and the 1999 book for the term.
- Race made in the targeting: "Indians and Black people in the US have been racialized in opposing ways that reflect their antithetical roles in the formation of US society. Black people's enslavement produced an inclusive taxonomy … the 'one-drop rule' … For Indians, in stark contrast, non-Indian ancestry compromised their indigeneity, producing 'half-breeds,' a regime that persists in the form of blood quantum regulations." (p. 387, as quoted by Tuck and Yang.) "a race cannot be taken as given. It is made in the targeting." "the primary motive for elimination is not race (or religion, ethnicity, grade of civilization, etc.) but access to territory. Territoriality is settler colonialism's specific, irreducible element."
- The structure sentence: "The logic of elimination not only refers to the summary liquidation of Indigenous people, though it includes that. In common with genocide as Raphaël Lemkin characterized it, settler colonialism has both negative and positive dimensions. Negatively, it strives for the dissolution of native societies. Positively, it erects a new colonial society on the expropriated land base—as I put it, settler colonizers come to stay: invasion is a structure not an event." (p. 388 per Kauanui and Snelgrove et al.; note 7 refers the formula back to the 1999 book, p. 2, and to "Nation and miscegeNation", p. 96.) "In its positive aspect, elimination is an organizing principal of settler-colonial society rather than a one-off (and superseded) occurrence. The positive outcomes of the logic of elimination can include officially encouraged miscegenation, the breaking-down of native title into alienable individual freeholds, native citizenship, child abduction, religious conversion, resocialization in total institutions such as missions or boarding schools, and a whole range of cognate biocultural assimilations."
- "Settler colonialism destroys to replace." (Herzl's *Altneuland*; Benvenisti; Tony Birch on Gariwerd.) "It is both as complex social formation and as continuity through time that I term settler colonization a structure rather than an event, and it is on this basis that I shall consider its relationship to genocide." (end of the first section; p. 390 per Kauanui for "elimination refers to more than the summary liquidation of Indigenous people, though it includes that.")
- Second section (discovery, preemption, the Cherokee removal): "the agency which reduced Indian peoples to this abjection was not some state instrumentality but irregular, greed-crazed invaders"; "Rather than something separate from or running counter to the colonial state, the murderous activities of the frontier rabble constitute its principal means of expansion"; "settler colonialism is an inclusive, land-centred project that coordinates a comprehensive range of agencies, from the metropolitan centre to the frontier encampment, with a view to eliminating Indigenous societies."
- Third section (modernity): "Settler colonialism was foundational to modernity." He quotes **Cole Harris** 2004 (note 36, p. 179): "Combine capital's interest in uncluttered access to land and settlers' interest in land as livelihood, and the principal momentum of settler colonialism comes into focus" — a direct citation of the Canadian geographer whom the project's `settler_colonialism` entry carries as a historian writing "beside" the paradigm (see Corrections).
- Fourth section (agriculture, the Five Civilized Tribes, assimilation): "Agriculture not only supports the other sectors. It is inherently sedentary and, therefore, permanent." "Indians were the original communist menace." "Here, in essence, is assimilation's Faustian bargain—have our settler world, but lose your Indigenous soul. Beyond any doubt, this is a kind of death." Pratt's "Kill the Indian in him and save the man" (1892).
- Fifth section (genocide as the killing of a *genos*): "Richard Pratt and Phillip Sheridan were both practitioners of genocide. The question of degree is not the definitional issue." Against "cultural genocide": "it confuses definition with degree."
- Sixth section (allotment, blood quantum, termination; Israel's Law of Return): "A major difference between this and the generality of non-colonial genocides is its sustained duration." "assimilation is one of a range of strategies of elimination that become favoured in particular historical circumstances."
- Seventh section ("Why, then, logic of elimination rather than genocide?"): "settler colonialism is relatively impervious to regime change. The genocide of American Indians or of Aboriginal people in Australia has not been subject to election results." Against hyphenated genocides ("never quite the real thing"); "the Holocaust, the non-paradigmatic paradigm". "When invasion is recognized as a structure rather than an event, its history does not stop—or, more to the point, become relatively trivial—when it moves on from the era of frontier homicide." "I suggest that the term 'structural genocide' avoids the questions of degree—and, therefore, of hierarchy among victims—that are entailed in qualified genocides, while retaining settler colonialism's structural induration" (p. 403 per Kauanui).
- Last section (prediction and prevention): "settler colonialism is an indicator"; "Since settler colonialism persists over extended periods of time, structural genocide should be easier to interrupt than short-term genocides"; Israel's "progressive dispensing with its reliance on Palestinian labour would seem to present an ominous case in point"; Colin Tatz's apartheid argument; "Gaza and the West Bank become less and less like Bantustans and more and more like reservations".
- Self-positioning: "Unpalatable though it is (to speak as a member of a settler society)". Note 20 announces a planned critique of US Indian sovereignty judgments. Note 62 quotes his own 2001 AHR essay (p. 894): "Difference … is not simply given. It is the outcome of differentiation, which is an intensely conflictual process."

**Readers he cites who matter to the project**: Harris 2004; Hoxie 1989 (*A Final Promise*); Limerick 1987; Deloria and Wilkins; Moses ed., *Genocide and Settler Society* (2005, notes 3, 34, 72); Lemkin 1944; Rodinson 1973 ("the collective mother country", note 35); Tony Birch 2003.

## 2. J. Kēhaulani Kauanui, "'A Structure, Not an Event': Settler Colonialism and Enduring Indigeneity", *Lateral* 5:1 (Spring 2016)

**Citation.** DOI 10.25158/L5.1.7; open access (CC BY 4.0) at https://csalateral.org/issue/5-1/forum-alt-humanities-settler-colonialism-enduring-indigeneity-kauanui/. **Read status: read in full** (web text, unpaginated; located by paragraph; notes 1–17). Based on a 2014 ASA roundtable (note 1); "The last edits of this essay were completed in the immediate wake of Patrick Wolfe's death. He was not only a colleague; he was a friend" (acknowledgements). Responses by Beenash Jafri and Melissa Gniadek with a reply by Kauanui in *Lateral* 6:1 (2017) (editors' note).

**Positions.**
- Two senses of "enduring indigeneity" (para. 1): "that the operative logic of settler colonialism may be to 'eliminate the native,' as the late English scholar Patrick Wolfe brilliantly theorized, but that indigenous peoples exist, resist, and persist; and second, that settler colonialism is a structure that endures indigeneity, as it holds out against it." (She calls him "English"; Wikidata and the VU record say Australian; see Proposals.)
- On citation: Wolfe's 2006 essay "is often cited as the principal work representing the concept and theory of the settler colonial analytic. And although Wolfe insisted on making it clear time and again that he did not create the field of settler colonial studies—that Native scholars did—within the field of American Studies (as just one example), he tends to be most frequently cited as if he had" (para. 2). - **`description` (1,170 characters):** "An Australian historian of settler colonialism, race and the history of anthropology (1949–2016). His first book read Victorian and Australian anthropology through 'the elimination of native societies': 'the colonizers come to stay—invasion is a structure not an event' (1999). The 2006 essay made the formula a programme: settler colonialism 'destroys to replace'; it is 'inherently eliminatory but not invariably genocidal'; 'territoriality is settler colonialism's specific, irreducible element'; after the frontier, elimination 'turned inwards' through allotment, blood quantum and assimilation, so the form stays 'relatively impervious to regime change'; 'structural genocide' names its convergence with genocide without 'hierarchy among victims'. Race, he argued, 'is made in the targeting' (2001, 2006; Traces of History, 2016). Veracini called him the field's founder 'in a fit of absentmindedness'; Kauanui records that he 'insisted … that he did not create the field … that Native scholars did'. Critics find in his structure a 'colonial fatalism' (Macoun and Strakosch) and ask whether exploitation, not elimination, defines settler colonies (Englert)."
