### 2a. The *Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde* debate on the book (165:4, 2009, "Debate", pp. 552–567?)

A four-part exchange: Frances Gouda (University of Amsterdam), "A race horse among work horses in Dutch archival pastures", pp. 552–556; Remco Raben (Utrecht), "Ambiguities of reading and writing", from p. 556 (only its first page read, at the foot of Gouda's PDF); Henk Schulte Nordholt (KITLV, Leiden), "How colonial is this effort to establish a new standard for an ethnography of the archive?", pp. 560–562; Ann Laura Stoler (The New School for Social Research), "Thinking through colonial ontologies", from p. 562 (only its first paragraph read, at the foot of Schulte Nordholt's PDF). Sources: https://hdl.handle.net/11245/1.310957 (Gouda) and https://hdl.handle.net/11245/1.311281 (Schulte Nordholt); the journal's own host (kitlv-journals.nl) has lapsed; Brill's issue page returned 403.

**Gouda (read in full).** What she takes the book to do: "Stoler first trains her analytic gaze on the Dutch archival record, not simply using it as a privileged source of historical information. Instead, she treats the archives as a subject in and of itself demanding painstaking scholarly attention" (p. 552). Stoler's words as Gouda quotes them, with Gouda's page references to the book: a "racialized common sense about people and places" (p. 24); "imperial dispositions" (p. 3); "epistemic uncertainty and clarity" (p. 43); the archive as a "repository of good taste and bad faith" (p. 41); "the habits of the [colonial] heart" (p. 53); "the affective knowledge that was at the core of political rationality" (p. 98); "the social relations of power" (p. 35); a "logos and pathos of empire" (p. 278); "non-events" (p. 5). The method, in Gouda's words: "Stoler underscores the necessity to read along the grain before trying to read against it, as many postcolonial scholars in recent years have advocated. She encourages historians to enter the archives and surrender to them as a 'force field' in which a shifting vocabulary of political authority, affective knowledge and racial anxiety gives documentary voice to 'the habits of the [colonial] heart' (p. 53)" (pp. 552–553). On the shift from her earlier work: "In contrast to earlier statements about a generic 'colonial project' in, among others, *Race and the education of desire* in 1995 … this time around she offers variations on the same theme that are solidly grounded in the particularity of the Dutch colonial record" (p. 553); she "now seems to contest the notion that colonial societies … constituted political vanguards or 'laboratories of modernity'" (p. 553). On her standing: "In the thriving academic cottage industry of postcolonial studies, paying homage to Ann Stoler's work has become de rigueur … almost on a par with acknowledging the genealogical influences of such classics as Frantz Fanon's *Peau noire, masques blancs* (1952) or Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978)" (pp. 553–554). The case studies: the 1848 protest of the *inlandsche kinderen*; the Pauper Commissions of 1874 and 1901; Frans Carl Valck and the Deli murders of 19 October 1876 (p. 554). Criticisms: "so little engagement with the work of contemporary Dutch historians" (p. 555); "she nonetheless skips over most of the corpus of Dutch colonial historiography" (p. 555); the question of extrapolating from the Dutch case "to a general understanding of the nature of European colonialism in Asia appears more urgent than before" (p. 554). Praise: the book "is resplendent with questions and insights" (p. 552); "a provocative addition to the field" (p. 556).

**Raben (first page only, p. 556).** "There was a time when colonial historiography was performed by going to the archives, reading what colonial administrators had to say about the topic, and writing it down. Later, there was a time when perusing those same archives was an extremely suspect, if inevitable, activity … Times have changed again. In line with the view that sources tell something primarily about themselves and their makers, Ann Laura Stoler has written a captivating account about reading the colonial archives." The rest (pp. 557–560, on chapters 3–5) is unread.

**Schulte Nordholt (read in full).** The summary: "Archives are no longer seen as simple repositories of historical data but are also approached as objects of research" (p. 560); "we can no longer speak of 'the colonial project' as a single and coherent phenomenon, as Stoler did in her earlier work" (p. 560); "Good historians … are accustomed to encountering contestations and contradictions during archival research, but they are less often accustomed to making archives into an object of ethnographic research. *Along the archival grain* is an eloquent plea to explore this approach more seriously" (p. 560). Stoler's words as he quotes them: archives "not just as repositories of state power, but as unquiet movements in a field of force, as restless realignments and readjustments of people and the beliefs to which they were tethered, as spaces in which the senses and the affective course through the seeming abstractions of political rationalities" (book pp. 32–33); her ambition to "look for the pulse of the archive in the quiescence and quickened pace of its own production, in the steady and feverish rhythms of repeated incantations, formulae, and frames" (book p. 35); the aim "to set a new standard for an ethnography of the archives (p. 45)" as against "doing an ethnography in the archives" (review pp. 561–562); the "politics of disregard" (ch. 7). His doubts: "The power of Stoler's rhetoric is also a bit confusing, and I am not quite sure how to identify these 'pulses'" (p. 560); "the case studies are examples of good fieldwork in the archives, and have not yet set a new standard" (p. 562); "Why were archives in Indonesia ignored in this ethnography? … no Indonesian scholars are mentioned in the opening pages of the book … *Along the archival grain* has, therefore, despite its innovative ambitions, an unexpected colonial flavour" (p. 562). The footnotes' references to "Heidegger, Kant, Nietzsche, Nussbaum, Rorty" (p. 560).

**Stoler's reply (first paragraph only, p. 562).** "There were times in working on *Along the archival grain* over the last some 20 years when I thought I could hear Jorge Luis Borges laughing at me … attempting to trace a map of the Dutch colonial archives as dense and extensive as they were themselves." The reply's answers to the three reviewers (pp. 563–567?) are unread.

## 3. Joan M. Schwartz and Terry Cook, "Archives, records, and power: the making of modern memory", *Archival Science* 2:1–2 (March 2002): 1–19

DOI 10.1007/BF02435628. **Read status: abstract, keywords and reference list only** (paywalled). The abstract, as displayed: it introduces "the first of two thematic issues of *Archival Science* on 'archives, records, and power'"; archives are often treated as neutral, but are established by the powerful and shape which stories are remembered; "Archives, then, are not passive storehouses of old stuff, but active sites where social power is negotiated"; archivists' choices in appraisal, description, preservation and access shape collective memory and identity, and the authors call for these powers to be openly debated and held accountable (the last two clauses paraphrased by the fetch). Schwartz (National Archives of Canada / Queen's) and Cook (University of Manitoba / Clio Consulting) are the Canadian archival-science side of the turn; the "archives, records, and power" issues frame the field's own reckoning with Derrida through Harris (South Africa). ## 4. Michel-Rolph Trouillot: life, works and identity

### 4a. University of Chicago News, "Michel-Rolph Trouillot, scholar of Caribbean history, 1949–2012" (William Harms, 10 July 2012)
URL https://news.uchicago.edu/story/michel-rolph-trouillot-scholar-caribbean-history-1949-2012. **Read in full (fetch summary).** Died 5 July 2012, aged 62. "Grew up in Haiti"; came to the United States to join the Haitian exile community in New York "during the Duvalier years of the late 1960s"; a songwriter and activist against the Duvalier dictatorship and against US treatment of undocumented Haitian immigrants. BA in Caribbean history and culture, City University of New York, 1978; PhD in anthropology, Johns Hopkins, 1985. Before Chicago, "Krieger/Eisenhower Distinguished Professor of Anthropology" and director of the Institute for Global Studies in Culture, Power and History at Johns Hopkins; joined the Chicago faculty in 1998 as professor of anthropology. Works named with dates: *Ti Dife Boule sou Istoua Ayiti* (1977), "a Haitian Creole history of the Haitian Revolution of 1791–1804"; *Peasants and Capital: Dominica in the World Economy* (1988); *Haiti: State against Nation: The Origins and Legacy of Duvalierism* (1990); *Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History* (1995); *Open the Social Sciences* (1996; the Gulbenkian Commission report, of which he was a member); *Global Transformations: Anthropology and the Modern World* (2003). Award: the Caribbean Philosophical Association's 2011 Frantz Fanon Lifetime Achievement Award "for the originality of his interrogations in the human sciences". Colleagues quoted: Greg Beckett ("Rolph asked difficult questions, and he insisted others do the same"); Jean Comaroff; Yarimar Bonilla ("Rolph's work is the gold-standard for me intellectually"). A service in Port-au-Prince; a Chicago memorial planned for the autumn. Not given: birth date, Duke post, the 2002 aneurysm (which other obituaries mention; not read here).

## 5. Terry Cook, "Fashionable Nonsense or Professional Rebirth: Postmodernism and the Practice of Archives", *Archivaria* 51 (Spring 2001): 14–35
URL https://archivaria.ca/index.php/archivaria/article/view/12792 (open access; PDF read, pp. 14–35, text layer; **read in part: the opening, note *, pp. 21 and 31–32 in full, the rest skimmed by keyword**). Silencing the Past (1995) asked how power enters 'the production of history': silences enter at four moments, in the making of sources, archives, narratives and 'history in the final instance'; 'in history, power begins at the source' (as quoted by Davidson); the Haitian Revolution was 'unthinkable' as it happened, and the 'historical guild' relegated 'racism, slavery and colonialism' to 'an historical backburner' (p. 98, as quoted by Craipain). 'Anthropology and the Savage Slot' (1991) and Global Transformations (2003) turned the same question on anthropology's 'North Atlantic universals'. Craipain: three decades on, Silencing the Past 'remains an unavoidable reference' (2024); Daut asked readers to go 'beyond Trouillot' (2021).
