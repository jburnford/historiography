## 1. Sources read

### 1a. R. Bin Wong, *China Transformed: Historical Change and the Limits of European Experience* (Cornell, 1997)

The existing source `wong_china_primary` records chapter openings only (pp. 1, 9, 13, 33, 53, 71, 73, 105, 127, 153, 179, 207, 209, 231). Nothing new of the book was read this session. Three readers state what it argued and how it was used:

- **Pomeranz's acknowledgement** (2000, pp. 7–8, read in the later-edition preview): a regional approach "anticipated in important ways by my colleague R. Bin Wong" (p. 7, n. 11 "Wong 1997"); "Here, too, I am following a procedure outlined in Wong's recent China Transformed … viewing both sides of the comparison as 'deviations' when seen through the expectations of the other, rather than leaving one as always the norm" (p. 8); "my concrete application of this reciprocal comparative method has some significant differences from Wong's" (p. 8), namely "greater stress than Wong does on global conjunctures and reciprocal influences … much more about some he does not treat extensively, such as environmental change" (n. 13); "I largely agree with Wong" on similarities in "agricultural, commercial, and proto-industrial … development among various parts of Eurasia as late as 1750" (p. 8).
- **Mokyr** (2002, p. 2, read; quoting Wong pp. 31 and 41): "Wong's argument is stark in its simplicity and yet frustrating in its inability to be resolved. Before 1800, he maintains, the economic dynamic of Europe and China were quite similar: Smithian expansion, rural industry, population expansion, a growth in consumer demand, the threat of environmental degradation"; Wong: the "supply-driven Industrial Revolution that followed the demand-led Industrious Revolution in Europe did not happen in China" (p. 31); "Europeans … escaped the damaging dynamics of rural industrialization in the nineteenth century. Not so the Chinese" (p. 41). Mokyr's objection (p. 3): "Bin Wong does not cite any book by Needham."
- **Parthasarathi** (2011, p. 5, publisher excerpt): "In his China Transformed, R. Bin Wong argues that early-modern China, as did Europe, experienced Smithian growth … Kenneth Pomeranz builds upon this work".
- **Frank** (2001, §1d): Wong rejects "anti- historical Euroocentric nonsense", but "still confines his analysis to a comparative method that seeks out allegedly crucial differences".

### 1b. Jack A. Goldstone, "Efflorescences and Economic Growth in World History: Rethinking the 'Rise of the West' and the Industrial Revolution," *Journal of World History* 13:2 (2002), 323–389. **Abstract only** (OpenAlex; MUSE blocked)

- "The 'Rise of the West' has been treated by economic historians as stemming from the onset of rapid economic growth … In contrast, all premodern and non-Western economies have been treated as showing only slow or no growth … This is an error. Examined closely, many premodern and non-Western economies show spurts or efflorescences of economic growth … Medieval Europe, Golden Age Holland, and Qing China, among other cases".
- "The distinctive feature of Western economies since 1800 has not been growth per se, but growth based on a specific set of elements: engines to extract motive power from fossil fuels …; the application of empirical science …; and the marriage of empirically oriented science to a national culture of educated craftsmen and entrepreneurs … This combination developed from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries only in Britain, and was unlikely to have developed anywhere else in world history."
- **Mediated** (Mokyr 2002, p. 1 n. 1): Goldstone (2002, p. 330) "feels that to even repeat such beliefs that have been 'abandoned by virtually all historians and sociologists' … is 'embarrassing or seemingly absurd.'" Mokyr's bracketed gloss mocks it ("with the minor exception of such obscure figures as David Landes and Jared Diamond").
- **Use.** Goldstone is a member of the "California School" by every list read (§1h) yet locates the breakthrough in British science, engines and craft culture. Vries (2009, p. 48) singles him out: "Californians would be well-advised to broaden their approach, as some, e.g. Jack Goldstone, have already done." *Why Europe? The Rise of the West in World History, 1500–1850* (2008) is quoted by Vries (2013, p. 17) at p. viii; unread here.

### 1c. David S. Landes, *The Wealth and Poverty of Nations: Why Some Are So Rich and Some So Poor* (Norton, 1998)

- **Chapter 1, "Nature's Inequalities": read in full** in the publisher-supplied text on the *New York Times* "first chapter" page (1998; no print pages). Its argument is geographical, not the cultural polemic: "geography is discredited, if not discreditable, by its nature. It tells an unpleasant truth, namely, that nature like life is unfair, unequal in its favors"; "The world has never been a level playing field, and everything costs." The introduction, where his remarks on Eurocentrism are usually found, was **not** read; do not quote it.
- **Readers placing Landes as the Eurocentric counterpoint** (read):
  - The Northeastern University debate notice (World History Seminar, debate on **2 December 1998**; notice posted to the World-Systems Network list on 13 Nov. 1998 by the seminar's co-ordinator; read in full): "LANDES maintains that Max Weber was right about the RISE OF THE WEST … For Landes and them, the key to the West's success has been the exceptionalism of its values and institutions, that were and still are lacking in the Rest"; "FRANK retorts that … Received social theory … is vitiated by the ingrained EUROCENTRISM of historiography"; "LANDES retorts that Frank and his echoes are a magnet for fallacies and fantasies [and] the invention of folklore. Bad history." The notice is written from Frank's side; the Landes words are as the notice quotes them.
  - Frank (2001, §1d): "David Landes [1997] still trots out the same tired old shibboleths about alleged cultural differences".
  - Vries (2013, read): Landes is among those of whom "If they are not Eurocentrists, who is? Yet none of them ever writes in terms of 'inevitability'" (p. 54); n. 135 quotes Landes (1998, p. 29): "Europe was lucky…" and the probability of European dominance around 1000 "somewhere around zero"; "Landes in 1998 still describes Qing China as 'despotic' or even 'totalitarian', and as an empire of 'stasis and retreat'" (p. 59, citing Landes pp. 56–57 and chapters 21, 24).
  - Mokyr (2012, §1f): Europe's success "was not quite as predetermined and inexorable as it was made out to be by scholars from Max Weber to David Landes."
- **Use.** Landes is the debate's Eurocentric counterpoint in the readers' own words, with Vries's qualification that he never claimed inevitability.

### 1d. Andre Gunder Frank: *ReOrient* (1998) and his review of Pomeranz

- ***ReOrient: Global Economy in the Asian Age*** (University of California Press, 1998): **mediated** only. Vries (2013, pp. 15–16) quotes it (pp. 75, 259, 277, 333): Europe "remained a marginal player in the world economy with a perpetual deficit"; "the Europeans did not do anything – let alone 'modernize' – by themselves"; Europe rose by "climbing up on Asian shoulders"; and calls the first "a quite silly statement" (n. 14). Pomeranz cites "Abu-Lughod 1989; Frank 1998" in a note on p. 11 (2000; read only as a citation).
- **Frank, review of Pomeranz, *Journal of Asian Studies* 60:1 (Feb. 2001), 180–182.** Read in full in Frank's own posted manuscript ("Review for the JOURNAL OF ASIAN STUDIES"; the printed wording may differ): "This book makes, bar none, the biggest and most important contribution to our new understanding of the causes and mechanisms that brought about THE GREAT DIVERGENCE"; Pomeranz "becomes the most important, accomplished and hopefully the most influential member of what Jack Goldstone has called the new 'California School' of historians writing on China and the world, including Bin Wong, von Glahn …, Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giraldez …, Robert Marks … with honourary membership generously extended to me". On colonies: Pomeranz's argument "is far more sophisticated than one of simple exploitation and capital drain a la Marx, Eric Williams or the Wallerstein/Frank world systemises." Frank also writes that Samuel Huntington "coined this phrase" (the great divergence); **unverified**, record only as Frank's claim.

### 1e. Prasannan Parthasarathi, *Why Europe Grew Rich and Asia Did Not: Global Economic Divergence, 1600–1850* (Cambridge, 2011). **Publisher excerpt**, chapter 1 pp. 1–5

- Method (p. 1): previous explanations "all identify something that made Europe different, to which Europe's divergent path is then attributed. This book takes a different approach … it rejects claims for European difference."
- Thesis (p. 2): "Britain diverged from Asia, as well as other parts of Europe, not because it possessed rationality, science, markets, capitalism or anything else in greater abundance, but because the pressures and needs it faced – in combination with its state policies – produced a revolutionary response. Two pressures were critical in generating British divergence. The first was the competitive challenge of Indian cotton textiles … The second was shortages of wood, a consequence of deforestation."
- **Against Brenner** (pp. 3–5): "The most influential is the work of Robert Brenner, who argues that the British path of development emerged from an exceptional system of property rights" (p. 3); Bengal, Gujarat and South India "exported grain … which points to the limitations of Brenner's arguments for the exceptionalism of English agriculture" (p. 5). **For the Brenner packet:** this is a read critique of Brenner from the divergence side.
- **Against Weber and the Enlightenment explanation** (p. 4): "the exceptionalism of European forms of rationality continues to have its adherents. The source of this exceptional reason is no longer Protestantism but the Enlightenment" (aimed at Mokyr's kind of argument; Mokyr is not named on the pages read).
- On Wong and Pomeranz (p. 5): quoted in §1a.

**Joel Mokyr, review of Parthasarathi, EH.Net (January 2012). Read in full** (HTML; no pages):
- "One could call it perhaps 'the small divergence.'"
- "Above all, this book is an argument that the 'California hypothesis' applies to India as well. The California School, most notably embodied in Kenneth Pomeranz's Great Divergence and Andre Gunder Frank's Re-Orient, argue that the Chinese economy rather than falling behind from Ming times actually performed admirably until the end of the eighteenth century".
- "By blaming the Raj squarely for everything that went wrong with India's nineteenth century development, Parthasarathi offers us a warmed-up old nationalist chestnut"; "his cherry-picking of examples … simply does not persuade"; "Part of the book's weakness is the author's very limited and minimalist concept of the Industrial Revolution, which he sees as purely a revolution in cotton and coal."
- Concession: "There is no question that the initial differences between Asia and Europe on the eve of the Industrial Revolution have been overdrawn, and that Europe's success was not quite as predetermined and inexorable as it was made out to be by scholars from Max Weber to David Landes."
- Close: "a provocative and eloquent book that will modify our views of the Great Divergence, if not nearly as much as its author would hope for."

### 1f. Jean-Laurent Rosenthal and R. Bin Wong, *Before and Beyond Divergence: The Politics of Economic Change in China and Europe* (Harvard, 2011). **Mediated**: Debin Ma, EH.Net review (February 2012), **read in full** (HTML)

- "Distinguished from the Pomeranz thesis, which emphasizes resource endowments, they stake their claim on differences in political structure, namely the dichotomy between a unified and centralized imperial China versus a politically fragmented Europe".
- The mechanism, as Ma summarises chapter 4: warfare in fragmented Europe produced "a greater degree of concentration of trade and manufacturing in urban locations … With higher urban wages … technological innovation in Europe, as in the good old Habakkuk thesis (recently resurrected by Robert Allen), would be more biased towards saving labor than in China."
- Chapter 5: "the authors challenge the accepted thesis of China being a high interest rate and capital scarce country."
- Ma's criticisms: "This stimulating and striking hypothesis is supported by fairly meager evidence in the chapter"; "The evidence presented to support the claim that China provided higher levels of public goods is even shakier"; the claim (p. 195) that "representation was as unique to Europe as good governance was to China" "seems way overdrawn".
- Verdict: "a good contribution in the Great Divergence debate as it addresses some of the key issues beyond what has been done recently by Pomeranz, Allen and others … far less so when it comes to providing credible support for its own thesis."
- **Use.** Ma links component 4 to component 3 (Allen's labour-saving bias). Ma is also a co-author of Allen et al. (2011) on edge_1050; record that when citing him.

### 1g. Peer Vries

**"A World of Surprising Resemblances?", *Leidschrift* conference publication "The Role of the State in the Great Divergence" (2009), pp. 35–49. Read in part** (pp. 35–39, 47–49; Leiden repository PDF):
- (p. 35) "I would have loved to have written The Great Divergence. It changed the way people look at a central problem in historiography … However, I think that there are problems in the approach of Pomeranz and more in general the so-called California School".
- (p. 35) "The main thesis of the California School is that the Great Divergence took place rather late in history, during the late eighteenth and the beginning of the nineteenth centuries, and that it was contingent."
- (p. 35 n. 1) "The best-known amongst these scholars are Kenneth Pomeranz, Roy Bin Wong, Jack Goldstone, James Lee, Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez, Robert Marks, Peter Perdue, and the late Andre Gunder Frank. They are often called 'the California School' because many of them worked at universities in California. The term was introduced by Jack Goldstone."
- (pp. 37–38) On agriculture, farm size, livestock and wage labour in Britain and China: "I would not call that a 'surprising resemblance'"; "An old-fashioned Marxist would say that Britain in its agriculture had an altogether different mode of production from what we see in China. Californians are not keen on thinking in terms of such modes of production."
- (p. 48) "Coal and colonies definitely were fundamental in the Rise of the West. But reference to them cannot suffice to explain it … It has deep, firm and extended roots in Western history"; "Californians would be well-advised to broaden their approach, as some, e.g. Jack Goldstone, have already done."
- (pp. 48–49) On the state: "you cannot understand the functioning of an economy if you ignore it. The tendency to do so, by the way, might be one of the less fortunate effects of the current focus on 'the global' in historiography"; Britain's mercantilism was "to a certain extent" a good thing.

***Escaping Poverty: The Origins of Modern Economic Growth*** (Vienna University Press / V&R unipress, 2013; open-access copy). **Read in part**: pp. 13–17, 53–54, 59 and notes 6–23, 133–136, 155–158.
- (p. 14) "The debate on the Great Divergence clearly is not a recent fad … It has become quite vigorous again because a lot of the assumptions it used to take for granted, have become highly contested with the emergence of the so-called 'California School', a group of scholars so called because most of them at one time or another were based in California." Core named: Pomeranz, Wong, Rosenthal, Goldstone, Flynn and Giráldez, von Glahn, Lee and Wang Feng, Marks; "Quite similar ideas were defended by" Frank, Blaut, Hobson, Goody, Perdue and Li Bozhong.
- (p. 14 n. 8) The Global Economic History Network "was created by Patrick O'Brien, Kenneth Pomeranz, Kaoru Sugihara and myself."
- (pp. 14–15) "The Californians, moreover, experiment with a new kind of comparative reciprocal history that suits global historians who not only want to juxtapose but also connect. The emphasis on ecology and environment as we see it in the work of, for example, Pomeranz is not only very much en vogue, it also allows politically correct historians to be non-judgemental in their claims about what causes wealth and poverty" (n. 9 cites Jan de Vries, "The Great Divergence after ten years: justly celebrated yet hard to believe", p. 13).
- (p. 15) "The California revisionist thesis, in a nutshell, can best be described in terms of 'surprising resemblances' and 'Eurasian similarities'."
- (p. 17) "the most important Californian, Kenneth Pomeranz, very recently admitted that he probably overstated the lateness and suddenness of the Great Divergence and that economic parity between the two extremes of Eurasia probably had already disappeared in 1750 if not in 1700" (n. 23: de Vries, "after ten years", and Pomeranz, "Ten years after"). **Mediated**: Pomeranz's 2011 text is unread; the concession is Vries's report.
- (p. 54) on Landes, quoted in §1c.

## 2. Proposed component strand for `capitalismdebate`

**Primary strand: `great_divergence`.**
- **title:** "The Great Divergence and the so-called California School, 1997–2021"
- **focus (draft):** "Wong (1997) and Pomeranz (2000) compared Europe's and China's most advanced regions reciprocally and dated divergence late, to coal and colonies (Pomeranz) or to the escape from Smithian dynamics (Wong). Readers attribute the label 'California School' to Goldstone, whose 'efflorescences' (2002) put pre-modern growth outside Europe too; Frank's ReOrient (1998) and its public debate with Landes (1998) marked the polemical poles. Critics answered from property relations (Brenner and Isett 2002), from knowledge and technology (Mokyr 2002, 2012), from wages and national accounts (Broadberry and Gupta 2006; Allen et al. 2011; Broadberry, Guan and Li 2021) and from the state (Vries 2009, 2013).
