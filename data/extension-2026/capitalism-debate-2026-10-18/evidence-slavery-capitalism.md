## 1. Sources read

### 1a. Williams and the "Williams thesis": readers of the debate

Williams's own words are the existing reading (`williams_capitalism_primary`); none were re-read. What is new is how the debate's later participants frame him.

- **Pomeranz** (2000, p. 3 n. 2, read in the later-edition preview): "Note, for instance, the generally negative current mainstream verdicts on the arguments of Eric Williams (1944), Andre Gunder Frank (1969), Samir Amin (1974), etc. A good general critique of the overseas extraction thesis is DeVries 1976". His contents list a section "Overseas Extraction and Capital Accumulation: The Williams Thesis Revisited" (p. 186; unread).
- **Burnard and Riello** (2020, read; *JGH* 15:2, 225–244):
  - (p. 226) "By the early 2000s, Barbara Solow reflected a strong orthodoxy among economic historians about the importance of slavery in European capitalism. She declared that 'slavery did not cause the Industrial Revolution but played an active role in its pattern and timing'" (n. 7: Solow, in Solow and Engerman, eds, 1987, p. 72). "David Eltis and Stanley Engerman, by contrast, reflected that consensus by concluding that slavery and the slave trade were of marginal importance in the main currents of economic growth, even in Britain" (n. 8: *JEH* 60:1, 2000, p. 138).
  - (p. 227) "Williams' book advanced a breath-taking number of assertions about the role of slavery in British life … Four of his arguments are critical to the NHC project: that slavery was central to the Industrial Revolution; that slave wealth was important to the history of eighteenth-century Britain; that the West Indian slave economy went into decline from 1783; and that West Indian slave-owners moved from being a progressive force within mercantilism to being a reactionary force within industrial capitalism." (Compare the *Legacies* introduction's "four connected arguments", edge_1064.)
  - (p. 227) "His contemporary defenders, however, use Williams for historical perspectives he never considered. Williams devotes very little attention in his book either to America or to cotton".
  - (p. 233) "The historiographical pendulum is swinging away from a view that Williams was thought-provoking more than persuasive, which held sway among scholars in the 1980s and 1990s, to a movement among economic historians that accepts a modified version of the Williams thesis. Key works in this re-evaluation are Pomeranz's The Great Divergence and Inikori's Africans and the Industrial Revolution in England." This closely echoes Hall (2014, p. 24) on "a modified version of Williams's thesis amongst economic historians", naming the same two authors.
- **Hilt** (2017, p. 516, read): "Like Eric Williams in Capitalism and Slavery (1944), Beckert argues that the fortunes made in the slave trade and on sugar plantations in the Caribbean created a source of finance for the innovations in cotton textile production"; n. 6: "Beckert does not feature the work of Eric Williams prominently. He does, however, draw on Inikori (2002), the strongest recent defense of the Williams hypothesis."
- **Regele** (2023, pp. 6–7, read): the new works on slavery "draw from a long history of scholarship on the relationship between slavery and capitalism, most notably Eric Williams's Capitalism and Slavery. Many of their claims about the profitability, modernity, and brutality of the institution of slavery have either already been made or have been refuted by economic historians."
- **Frank** (2001; `evidence-great-divergence.md` §1d): Pomeranz's colonial argument "is far more sophisticated than one of simple exploitation and capital drain a la Marx, Eric Williams or the Wallerstein/Frank world systemises."

### 1d. Joseph E. Inikori, *Africans and the Industrial Revolution in England: A Study in International Trade and Economic Development* (Cambridge, 2002). **Mediated**: Kevin H. O'Rourke, EH.Net review (October 2003), **read in part** (the first three-quarters; HTML)

- "Joseph Inikori … has written a provocative, occasionally frustrating, and ultimately convincing monograph on one of the classic issues in our field, the causes of the British Industrial Revolution. His argument is that international trade was crucial to the success of that revolution, in particular trade with the Atlantic Basin (Africa, the West Indies, and the Americas); and that Africans on both sides of the Atlantic were central to this process, both as consumers and producers."
- "it comes as a welcome contrast to the domestically-focused 'west is best' arguments that are so common in the literature nowadays".
- Chapter 3 "contains a survey of the historiography of the Industrial Revolution, from Toynbee to today … the literature … has moved from being largely focused on external trade (before the Second World War), to more internalist explanations after 1945, and that the pendulum may now be swinging back again." (A reader's link to component 3.)
- Reservation: "a lot of cliometricians will probably conclude that Inikori's argument is not as yet proven. The model of endogenous technical change central to his argument that trade drove invention is never precisely spelled out".
- Other readers: Burnard and Riello call Inikori "Williams' best modern-day defender" (p. 227); Hilt calls the book "the strongest recent defense of the Williams hypothesis" (p. 516 n. 6). A roundtable with Inikori's response is in *International Journal of Maritime History* 15:2 (2003), 279–329 (metadata).

### 1e. The "new history of capitalism": name, publicity and programme

- **The 2013 publicity.** Jennifer Schuessler, "In History Departments, It's Up With Capitalism," *New York Times*, 7 April 2013. **Mediated** (the article itself could not be opened): Columbia University Press's blog (8 April 2013, via WebFetch) quotes it: "a new generation of scholars is increasingly turning to what, strangely, risked becoming the most marginalized group of all"; "'And to understand capitalism,' said Jonathan Levy, 'you've got to understand capitalists'"; "'I like to call it "history from below, all the way to the top,"' said Louis Hyman"; "a loosely linked group of scholars who came of age after the end of the cold war cleared some ideological ground"; "Instead of searching for working-class radicalism, they looked at office clerks and entrepreneurs." The blog names the Columbia series "Columbia Studies in the History of U.S. Capitalism" (editors Hyman, Moreton and Ott). Regele (2023, abstract): "Since the new history of capitalism (NHOC) trend received national publicity in 2013". **Whether the 2013 article itself used the phrase "new history of capitalism" is not established.**
- **Regele, "A Brief History of the History of Capitalism, and a New American Variety," *Enterprise & Society* 25:1 (2024; online 25 Oct. 2023), 2–26. Read in part** (abstract; pp. 1–3, 6–7 with notes 7–12):
  - (p. 6) "This dissatisfaction with the explanatory limitations of labor history, combined with the recognition of the failures of capitalism during the financial crisis of 2007–2009, culminated in the emergence of the NHOC. This 'new' approach to capitalism is especially attuned to power, inequality, and the existence of slavery and other forms of coerced labor. (The scholarly trend to which this name applies predated the crisis: Harvard launched a seminar on the political economy of modern capitalism in 2005, and works by such historians as Seth Rockman, Louis Hyman, Julia Ott, Bethany Moreton and Stephen Mihm, and Jonathan Levy either began or were published before the crisis.)"
  - (p. 6) "Slavery has become central to the field, highlighted by the 2016 publication of Slavery's Capitalism".
  - (p. 7) "The newest thing about the field is the label and the attention it has received, both publicly and from other historical fields."
- **Beckert and Desan (eds), *American Capitalism: New Histories* (Columbia, 2018)**: metadata only (cited by Burnard and Riello n. 4 and by Regele). **Beckert and Rockman (eds), *Slavery's Capitalism* (Pennsylvania, 2016)**: mediated; Burnard and Riello (p. 232) quote the introduction (p. 1): "A scholarly revolution over the past two decades, which brought mainstream historical accounts into line with long-standing positions in Africana and Black Studies, has recognized slavery as the foundational American institution".
- **Hilt on the name** (2017, p. 512, read): "an early overview of the field by one its foremost practitioners, Sven Beckert of Harvard University, defined it in this way (Beckert 2011). Yet this definition is so broad that it includes most of the fields of labor, political, social, and business history"; "historians of capitalism aspire to something closer to social criticism … an 'activist impulse' seems to animate their work (Rosenthal 2016)"; "The name 'history of capitalism' calls attention to the presence of capitalism, but it should mainly be seen as signifying critical social analysis by historians." On influences (pp. 513–514): "The first is Karl Polanyi's The Great Transformation … The second major influence is the recent financial crisis and Great Recession."

### 1f. Sven Beckert, *Empire of Cotton: A Global History* (Knopf, 2014). **Authorised excerpt** (*The National*, three parts; "copyright © 2014 by Sven Beckert. Published by arrangement with Alfred A Knopf"; no pages)

- "Slavery, the expropriation of indigenous peoples, imperial expansion, armed trade, and the assertion of sovereignty over people and land by entrepreneurs were at its core. I call this system war capitalism."
- "We usually think of capitalism … as emerging around 1780 with the Industrial Revolution. But war capitalism, which began to develop in the 16th century, came long before machines and factories … resting on the violent expropriation of land and labour in Africa and the Americas."
- "Many historians have called this the age of 'merchant' or 'mercantile' capitalism, but 'war capitalism' better expresses its rawness and violence as well as its intimate connection to European imperial expansion."
- "War capitalism, then, was the foundation from which evolved the more familiar industrial capitalism … At first, industrial capitalism remained tightly linked to slavery and expropriated lands".
- *The National*'s headnote: "a finalist for the 2015 Pulitzer Prize in history and won the Bancroft Prize" (newspaper's claim; verify with the Bancroft list before display).

### 1g. Edward Baptist, *The Half Has Never Been Told* (Basic Books, 2014), and Walter Johnson, *River of Dark Dreams* (Harvard, 2013): **mediated only**

- **Hilt** (2017, read):
  - Baptist (p. 518): "Baptist makes the astonishing claim that all of the increase in productivity in cotton picking observed in the antebellum era was due to increases in the whipping and torturing of slaves. This claim enables him to make the central argument of the book, that the 'ultimate cause' of the Industrial Revolution was the 'systematized torture' of American slaves (pp. 135, 141; see also p. 413) … Olmstead and Rhode (p. 26–31) show that the increase in productivity they observe was due primarily to biological innovations"; the 1836 GDP calculation is "a disastrously mishandled undertaking"; the book "is perhaps best understood as 'history as rhetoric' rather than 'history as scholarship.'"
  - Johnson (p. 519): "River of Dark Dreams is, at its core, an ethnography of the Cotton South"; on "bales per hand per acre", "as Gavin Wright (2014, p. 878) notes 'makes neither mathematical nor economic sense.'" Hilt notes (p. 513 n.) that Johnson does not consider himself a historian of capitalism.
- ***The Economist*'s review and apology** (September 2014; **mediated** by MPR News, 5 Sept. 2014, via WebFetch): the review said "Mr Baptist has not written an objective history of slavery. Almost all the blacks in his book are victims, almost all the whites villains." The magazine's apology: "There has been widespread criticism of this, and rightly so. Slavery was an evil system, in which the great majority of victims were blacks, and the great majority of whites involved in slavery were willing participants and beneficiaries of that evil … We regret having published this and apologise for having done so." A dated public episode in the book's reception; the review itself is not an argument of the debate.

### 1h. Economic historians' critiques of the new history of capitalism

**Alan L. Olmstead and Paul W. Rhode**
- **AHA paper, "The 'New History of Capitalism,' Cotton, and Slavery,"** 131st Annual Meeting, Denver, session "Perspectives on the New History of Capitalism", **8 January 2017**. **Abstract read in full** (AHA programme page): "The 'New History of Capitalism' grounds the rise of industrial capitalism on the production of raw cotton by American slaves. Recent works include Walter Johnson's River of Dark Dreams, Sven Beckert's Empire of Cotton, and Edward Baptist's The Half Has Never Been Told … We show that all make fundamental errors in economic reasoning that affect their major interpretations … Baptist argues that the calibrated use of torture on the American cotton frontier was the key innovation leading to the global industrial revolution … However, Baptist's account is at variance with the ex-slave narratives that he cites and with evidence drawn from plantation records."
- **"Cotton, Slavery, and the New History of Capitalism," *Explorations in Economic History* 67 (2018), 1–17** (doi:10.1016/j.eeh.2017.12.002): **metadata only** (Elsevier, closed).

**Eric Hilt, "Economic History, Historical Analysis, and the 'New History of Capitalism'," *JEH* 77:2 (2017), 511–536. Read in part** (pp. 511–519, OCR; open access):
- Abstract (p. 511): "This article presents a critical survey of ten books from the history of capitalism, a newly emerging subfield of history … Yet specious arguments and failures of analytical reasoning sometimes undermine these books' effectiveness as social criticism."
- (p. 515) "None of the books makes any attempt to falsify major elements of their arguments."
- Beckert (p. 516): "Beckert argues that the colonial empires of the European powers … constituted an early phase of capitalism, which he denotes 'war capitalism' … Beckert presents a theory of the Industrial Revolution that emphasizes its origins in 'slavery, colonial domination, militarized trade, and land expropriations' (p. 60)"; "Research by economic historians casts doubt on some of these arguments and lends support to others. David Eltis and Stanley Engerman (2000) concluded that relative to other British industries … the total value added and growth-inducing linkages of the British slave trade and Caribbean slave plantations were not particularly large."
- Across components (p. 517): "the steam engine appears rather awkwardly out of nowhere in Beckert's cotton-focused account, whereas its emergence is coherently explained by research that emphasizes British labor and energy costs, or the scientific advances associated with the Enlightenment"; n. 8 names "Allen (2009)" and "Mokyr (2010)", and Beckert's dismissal of "'Enlightenment traditions' (p. xiv)". Also (p. 517): "Like Kenneth Pomerantz's [sic, as printed] The Great Divergence, Beckert's book emphasizes the importance of colonial land holdings in enabling the British economy to expand beyond its ecological limits."
- Hilt n. 4 (p. 516): "Economic historians have already begun to critique these works (Olmstead and Rhode 2016; Majewski 2015), and the Journal of Economic History presented a series of critical reviews of The Half Has Never Been Told (Murray et al. 2015). These works have also been critiqued from within the field of history (Oakes 2016)."

**Trevor Burnard and Giorgio Riello, "Slavery and the New History of Capitalism," *JGH* 15:2 (2020), 225–244. Read in part** (abstract; pp. 225–228, 232–233; open access, CC-BY):
- Abstract: "the NHC overemphasizes the importance of coercion to economic growth in the eighteenth century … in linking slavery to industrialization, the NHC proposes a misleading chronology. Cotton produced in large quantities in the United States came too late to cause an Industrial Revolution … During early industrialization, sugar, not cotton, was the main plantation crop in the Americas … the NHC … underplays the 'power of consumption'".
- (p. 226) Beckert's book is "the most important book to emerge from the NHC movement to date"; "the NHC is a movement that comes primarily out of the historiography of the nineteenth-century USA and is heavily America-centric".
- **Joining the components** (p. 226 n. 6): "For an earlier debate over the origins of capitalism, involving competing theories by Fernand Braudel, Immanuel Wallerstein, Maurice Dobbs [sic], and Robert Brenner, see … The Brenner debate". (p. 227) "One NHC achievement is that it reinserts politics and power into Kenneth Pomeranz's influential thesis on the Great Divergence … A second important element borrowed from the Great Divergence debate is the linking of capitalism and industrialization." (p. 228 n.) "Beckert refers to world-systems theory only in passing but is heavily influenced by its axioms."
- (p. 232) on *Slavery's Capitalism*: "their claims are sweeping, polemical, and rooted in present-day politics, seeking to wrest the study of capitalism from economic historians"; "The new interest in slavery is probably more an evolution of historiographical attention than a 'scholarly revolution'". On Britain: compensation of "£20 million to slave-owners … a sum equivalent to one-fifth of government expenditure in 1834" (n. 43 Draper 2010); n. 44 cites Hall et al., *Legacies*.
- (p. 232) "The NHC agrees with Pomeranz that slavery and plantations were key factors in the transformation of the early modern world economy. But it differs from Pomeranz on two substantial points. First, the NHC sees slavery as more important than industrialization in the shaping of modern capitalism. Second … advocates of NHC believe that Williams was not only right … but also that his insights understate the importance of slavery".
- **Note on Burnard.** Burnard reviewed Hall et al.'s *Legacies* (`w1_burnard_legacies_2016`, opening only). He writes here as a historian of Caribbean slavery, not as an economist; his and Riello's critique is from global and Atlantic history, distinct from Hilt's and Olmstead and Rhode's.

## 2. Proposed component strands for `capitalismdebate` (component 5)

**Strand 5a: `williams_thesis`.**
- **title:** "Capitalism and Slavery and its critics: the Williams thesis, 1944–2020"
- **focus (draft):** "Williams (1944) linked slave-trade and plantation wealth to British industrial capitalism and explained abolition by changing economic interests. Drescher's Econocide (1977) contested the decline of the West Indies; Solow and Engerman's conference volume (1987) sifted the 'legacy'; Solow concluded that slavery 'did not cause the Industrial Revolution but played an active role in its pattern and timing', while Eltis and Engerman (2000) judged its contribution marginal (both as quoted by Burnard and Riello). Inikori (2002) reasserted the thesis through Atlantic trade and African producers and consumers; Pomeranz's coal-and-colonies argument and the Legacies of British Slave-ownership project are read as part of 'a modified version of the Williams thesis' (Hall 2014; Burnard and Riello 2020)."
- **person_ids:** eric_williams, seymour_drescher, barbara_solow (new), stanley_engerman, david_eltis (new), joseph_inikori (new), kenneth_pomeranz, catherine_hall
- **works:** "Williams, Capitalism and Slavery (1944); Drescher, Econocide (1977); Solow and Engerman (eds), British Capitalism and Caribbean Slavery (1987); Drescher, 'Capitalism and Slavery after Fifty Years' (1997); Eltis and Engerman (JEH 2000); Inikori, Africans and the Industrial Revolution in England (2002); Hall et al., Legacies of British Slave-ownership (2014)"
- **source_ids:** williams_capitalism_primary, williams, drescher_econocide, cd_drescher_fifty_1997, cd_solow_engerman_1987, cd_orourke_inikori_2003, cd_burnard_riello_2020, w1_hall_gendering_property_2014, w1_hall_legacies_2014, pomeranz_divergence

**Strand 5b: `new_history_of_capitalism`.**
- **title:** "Slavery's capitalism and its economic-historian critics, 2013–2020"
- **focus (draft):** "A loosely joined group of mainly Americanist historians, publicised as historians of capitalism in 2013, put slavery at the centre of American and global capitalism: Johnson's ethnography of the Cotton Kingdom (2013), Baptist's argument that torture drove cotton productivity (2014), Beckert's 'war capitalism' (2014) and Beckert and Rockman's Slavery's Capitalism (2016). Economic historians answered that the books 'make fundamental errors in economic reasoning' (Olmstead and Rhode, 2017) and contain 'specious arguments' (Hilt, 2017); Burnard and Riello (2020) objected that US cotton came 'too late to cause an Industrial Revolution'. Readers trace the movement to Polanyi, the 2008 crisis and a turn away from labour history (Hilt; Regele), and to Williams and Pomeranz (Burnard and Riello)."
- **person_ids:** sven_beckert (new), edward_baptist (new), walter_johnson (new), seth_rockman (new), alan_olmstead (new), paul_rhode (new), eric_hilt (new), trevor_burnard (new), giorgio_riello (new), karl_polanyi (as reported influence)

- **description (draft, about 170 words):** "A set of linked arguments about why capitalism, industrialisation and Western wealth arose where and when they did. Marxist historians disputed whether trade or class relations ended feudalism (Dobb and Sweezy, 1950), and Brenner's property-relations thesis (1976) opened a second round. Economic historians slowed the British Industrial Revolution while reasserting 'an important discontinuity' (Crafts and Harley, 1992), and its causes multiplied: Wrigley's escape from an 'organic economy', de Vries's 'industrious revolution', Mokyr's 'Industrial Enlightenment', Allen's high wages and cheap energy. Wong and Pomeranz compared Europe's and China's advanced regions and dated divergence late, to coal and colonies; critics answered from property relations, knowledge, wages and the state. Williams's Capitalism and Slavery (1944) returned through Inikori, the Legacies of British Slave-ownership project and an American 'history of capitalism' whose economic-historian critics charged 'specious arguments'. Readers join these debates themselves: Burnard and Riello set the new history of capitalism beside the Brenner debate and Pomeranz."
- **scope_note (draft):** "Five components, each with its own strands: the transition debate; the Brenner debate and political Marxism; explaining industrialisation; the Great Divergence; slavery and capitalism.
