## 1. Sources read

### 1a. Arnold Toynbee, *Lectures on the Industrial Revolution in England* (posthumous, 1884). **Re-read in part** (existing source `toynbee_industrial`; Saylor transcription, no print pagination; cite by lecture)

- Lecture I, opening: "The subject of these lectures is the industrial and Agrarian Revolution at the end of the eighteenth and beginning of the nineteenth centuries … It will describe England on the eve of the Industrial Revolution, and the system of regulation and protection of industry as it existed in 1760."
- Lecture VIII, "The Chief Features of the Revolution": "The essence of the industrial Revolution is the substitution of competition for the medieval regulations which had previously controlled the production and distribution of wealth. On this account it is not only one of the most important facts of English history, but Europe owes to it the growth of two great systems of thought - Economic Science, and its antithesis, Socialism."
- Lecture VIII, on consequences: "The effects of the industrial Revolution prove that free competition may produce wealth without producing well-being."
- **Use.** A dated precursor and a definition by institutions (competition replacing regulation), not by output growth. **Do not say Toynbee coined the term**: nothing read here establishes first use, and the atlas has no source for coinage. Jan de Vries (1994, p. 249, §1f) calls the concept "By far the most important such monument in economic history", which is the reader's warrant for treating Toynbee's lectures as the debate's starting point.

### 1b. Gradualism: N. F. R. Crafts and C. K. Harley

**Crafts and Harley, "Output Growth and the British Industrial Revolution: A Restatement of the Crafts–Harley View," *Economic History Review* 45:4 (1992), 703–730. Abstract only** (University of Warwick repository record, read in full):
- "This paper reviews the debate concerning British industrial output growth between 1760 and 1830. The earlier indices by Crafts and Harley are revised in accordance with new data … The main results show that the general picture provided by the authors ten years ago is still acceptable-insofar as any adjustment is required the effect is slightly to lower the estimated growth rate, contrary to recent claims. The idea of the industrial revolution as an important discontinuity is reasserted."
- **Caution for the atlas.** The restatement does **not** abolish the Industrial Revolution; it reasserts a discontinuity at a slower aggregate pace. "Gradualist" is the opponents' label (Berg and Hudson, §1c), and the atlas should say so.

**Crafts, *British Economic Growth during the Industrial Revolution* (1985). Unread; mediated** by Berg and Hudson (1990, working-paper pp. 5–6), who quote it: "not only was the triumph of ingenuity slow to come to fruition but it does not seem appropriate to regard innovativeness as pervasive", and report his estimate that total factor productivity in manufacturing grew "only around 0.2% between 1760 and 1801 and 0.4% 1801–1831", with cotton as "a modern sector floating in a sea of tradition" (their paraphrase of his view).

### 1c. Maxine Berg and Pat Hudson, "Rehabilitating the Industrial Revolution"

**Working paper**: University of Warwick Economic Research Paper 351 (May 1990), about 40 pages plus notes (EconPapers lists it as 13 pages, which is wrong for the scan). **Read in part**: pp. 1–6 (introduction and the critique of Crafts), the demography section opening, pp. 38–39 (conclusion), notes 1 and 5–6. Published version: *Economic History Review* 45:1 (1992), 24–50 (**abstract only**, via OpenAlex). The two texts are not identical; quote the published abstract for the 1992 claim and the working paper only as "an earlier version".

- **Their target** (WP p. 1): "It is only in the last decade that the gradualist perspective has appeared to triumph. In economic history it has done so because of a growing preoccupation with growth accounting at the expense of more broadly based conceptualisations of economic change." (p. 2): "Thus the notion of industrial revolution has been dethroned altogether leaving instead only a long process of structural change in employment from agrarian to non agrarian occupations."
- **Their thesis** (WP pp. 4–5): "We argue that a much more radical transformation took place in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries than is conveyed by recent quantitative assessments … In other words N. F. R. Crafts and others may not be able to find the industrial revolution because, in addition to using problematic data, they have mis-specified their subject in identifying it largely with marked discontinuity in trend rates of growth."
- **Four areas** (WP p. 5): "technical and organisational innovation, the deployment of female and child labour, regional specialisation and demographic development".
- **Against Wrigley and Schofield** (demography section): "The work of Wrigley and Schofield dominates the population history of this period and illustrates some of the more general problems of causal analysis based on aggregative studies."
- **Conclusion** (WP pp. 38–39): "It is time to move on from the macro accounting framework and to pull aside the veil of continuity and gradualism which this has imposed. We need to adopt a broader concept of innovation, to insist on a greater awareness of female and child labour and to rebuild the national picture of economic and social change from new research at regional and local level. If this is done, a rehabilitation of the industrial revolution will not be long delayed."
- **Acknowledgement** (note 1): "We are very grateful to N.F.R. Crafts for detailed [comments on an] earlier version" (OCR damaged; bracketed words are not legible in the scan).
- **1992 abstract** (published version): "Gradualist perspectives now dominate economic and social histories of the industrial revolution … This article challenges these perspectives. Currently accepted economic indicators and recent social history underplay the extent and uniqueness of economic and social transformation. The article emphasizes change in technology, the use of a female and child labour force, regional specialization, demographic behaviour, and political change."
- **Berg and Hudson's 1994 comment** replying to Crafts and Harley (*EHR* 47:1, 147–149): metadata only (search result).

### 1d. E. A. Wrigley: organic and mineral-based economies

**Wrigley, *Continuity, Chance and Change: The Character of the Industrial Revolution in England* (Cambridge, 1988). Publisher excerpt**: Introduction pp. 1–6 and chapter 1 pp. 7–8 (Cambridge's free sample of the paperback, ISBN 978-0-521-39657-8; page images OCRed locally).
- Origin (p. 1): "This book began as the Ellen McArthur lectures delivered in the University of Cambridge in the Lent Term 1987." He had "returned increasingly to my first main research interest, the better understanding of the industrial revolution".
- The field's state (p. 2): "By an astounding irony of modern historiography, the industrial revolution … has become a dull subject that slips into focus and out again, uncomfortably peripheral to the vision of many historians … Hamlet is often performed without the Prince of Denmark."
- His aim (p. 3): "My main aim in the lectures was to question the appropriateness of the view that the industrial revolution was a cumulative, progressive, unitary phenomenon."
- The "chance" of the title (p. 4): "The paradox disappears … if, instead of regarding the industrial revolution as a unitary process in the traditional fashion, the growth taking place in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries is seen as the product of two different sets of forces having only an accidental relationship to one another in the early stages of their overlap in time."
- The organic economy (p. 5): the classical economists' writings "remain authoritative for the analysis of growth within the confines of a traditional economy, an economy bounded by the productivity of the land, what I shall term an organic economy. It escaped their notice, however, that a different economic base was emerging whose character contrasted sharply with that of any organic economy." The new regime "escaped from the problem of the fixed supply of land and of its organic products by using mineral raw materials".
- Energy (p. 6): when coal supplied heat and, through the steam engine, mechanical energy, "the prospects for growth both in aggregate output and in output per head were entirely transformed from those which had always previously obtained."
- Stance (p. 6): "The book remains, however, an essay and not a treatise. My aim is not to try to establish a new orthodoxy."
- Chapter 1 (p. 7): "The industrial revolution is the centrepiece of world history over recent centuries, and a fortiori of the country in which it began."

**Wrigley, "Energy and the English Industrial Revolution," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A* 371 (2013), 20110568. Abstract only** (Europe PMC): "Societies before the Industrial Revolution were dependent on the annual cycle of plant photosynthesis for both heat and mechanical energy … In the Industrial Revolution, energy usage increased massively and output rose accordingly. The energy source continued to be plant photosynthesis, but accumulated over a geological age in the form of coal. This poses a problem for the future."

**Wrigley, *The Path to Sustained Growth: England's Transition from an Organic Economy to an Industrial Revolution* (Cambridge, 2016). Publisher excerpt**, Introduction pp. 1–4:
- (p. 1) "The underlying constraint that prevented sustained growth in organic economies arose from the nature of its energy sources."
- (p. 3) "The possibility of bringing about an industrial revolution depended on gaining access to a different source of energy. Mining coal provided the solution to this problem." It "shares an underlying theme with an earlier publication, Energy and the English industrial revolution" (2010, unread).

### 1f. Jan de Vries: the industrious revolution

**"The Industrial Revolution and the Industrious Revolution," *JEH* 54:2 (1994), 249–270** (existing `de_vries_industrious`; **re-read pp. 249–251** this session from the same scan, OCRed):
- Abstract (p. 249): "The Industrial Revolution as a historical concept has many shortcomings. A new concept—the 'industrious revolution'—is proposed to place the Industrial Revolution in a broader historical setting. The industrious revolution was a process of household-based resource reallocation that increased both the supply of marketed commodities and labor and the demand for market-supplied goods … that preceded the Industrial Revolution, a supply-side phenomenon."
- The concept as monument (pp. 249–250): "By far the most important such monument in economic history is the Industrial Revolution—the British Industrial Revolution"; Clapham "had to acknowledge that this thrice-squeezed orange still had an astonishing amount of juice left in it".
- The crisis of the concept (p. 250): "Our cherished Industrial Revolution now faces this same fate"; note 5 lists "Berg and Hudson, 'Rehabilitating the Industrial Revolution'; Cannadine, 'The Past and the Present'" among recent works.
- On the revisionists (pp. 250–251): "the revisionist macroeconomic research, leading to a sharp downward revision of the rate of growth of the British economy during the Industrial Revolution, that has been carried out by Peter Lindert and Jeffrey Williamson, Knick Harley, and Nick Crafts. Yet, unless I have badly misread their work, none of these scholars was engaged in a nihilistic project … They were interested in cleansing the temple of the 'false god of the take off,' not in pulling down the pillars."
- **Use.** de Vries places his concept explicitly as a response to the Crafts–Harley revision, and he reads Berg and Hudson. This is the read bridge between the "concept and revisions" strand and the household-demand strand.

**de Vries, *The Industrious Revolution: Consumer Behavior and the Household Economy, 1650 to the Present* (Cambridge, 2008). ### 1g. Joel Mokyr: the Industrial Enlightenment

### 1h. Robert C. Allen: high wages and cheap energy, and its critics

**Allen, *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (Cambridge, 2009). Publisher excerpt**, chapter 1 pp. 1–6:
- The question (p. 1): "This book is about a historical problem: why did the Industrial Revolution happen in Britain, in the eighteenth century?"
- The thesis (p. 2): eighteenth-century Britain had "a unique structure of wages and prices … Wages were remarkably high, and energy was remarkably cheap … The Industrial Revolution, in short, was invented in Britain in the eighteenth century because it paid to invent it there, while it would not have been profitable in other times and places … the Industrial Revolution can be seen as the sequel to that first phase of globalization."
- Its end (pp. 2–3): diffusion came when British engineers made the techniques "globally appropriate technologies"; "Global diffusion marked the end of the Industrial Revolution".
- On rival explanations (p. 3): "explaining the Industrial Revolution has been a long-standing problem in social science and has generated all manner of theories (Hartwell 1967, Jones 1981, Blaut 1993, Goldstone 2002, Bruland 2004). Most approaches fall under the headings of social structure, constitution and property rights, science, and culture."

Via WebFetch extraction**: Allen argues that "the combination of high wages and cheap fuel deserves a central place in our analyses of industrialisation"; "Britain's cheap and abundant coal supplies created an energy situation unique in the world."
