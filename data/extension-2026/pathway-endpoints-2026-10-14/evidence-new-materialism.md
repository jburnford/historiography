## 2. The coinage: "neo-materialism" / "new materialism" (late 1990s)

### 2a. DeLanda's own words (1996, 1997)

- DeLanda, "The Geology of Morals: A Neo-Materialist Interpretation" **[read]**. The subtitle
  carries the coinage. Section on abstract machines: "This conception of very specific abstract
  machines governing a variety of structure-generating processes not only blurs the distinction
  between the natural and the artificial, but also that between the living and the inert. It
  indeed points towards a new form of materialist philosophy in which raw matter-energy through
  a variety of self-organizing processes and an intense power of morphogenesis, generates all
  the structures that surround us." Closing paragraph: "I must end now this brief exploration of
  what a Neo-Materialist interpretation of the philosophy of Deleuze and Guattari would be like.
  […] It nevertheless retains its basic geological spirit, a philosophical stance which rejects
  ideas of progress not only in human history but in natural history as well. Living creatures,
  according to this stance, are in no way 'better' than rocks." (No page numbers: web text.
  The essay's sources are Simon, Braudel, Mirowski, and Deleuze and Guattari.)
- DeLanda, *A Thousand Years of Nonlinear History* (1997) **[read]**:
  - p. 15: "much as history has infiltrated physics, we must now allow physics to infiltrate
    human history."
  - p. 16: "all these forms of spontaneous structural generation suggest that inorganic matter
    is much more variable and creative than we ever imagined. And this insight into matter's
    inherent creativity needs to be fully incorporated into our new materialist philosophies."
  - p. 19: "I take as my point of departure a view shared by several materialist historians
    (principally Braudel and McNeill)". p. 20: "I refer to this historical narrative as
    'geological' because it concerns itself exclusively with dynamical elements (energy flow,
    nonlinear causality) that we have in common with rocks and mountains and other nonliving
    historical structures." Chapter Two, p. 20: colonial enterprises are "the means by which the
    genes of many nonhuman species have invaded and conquered alien ecosystems."
  - p. 152 (ch. 2) uses Crosby directly: "Crosby argues that, in order for European cities to
    replicate themselves, […] a whole array of species (humans and their domesticates) had to
    migrate together, had to colonize the new land as a team. The end result is that the
    temperate areas of these five countries became what he calls 'Neo-Europes.'"
  - Note for the atlas: DeLanda is a philosopher writing a history, and his named historians are
    Braudel, McNeill and Crosby. The book is a pre-2000, in-period bridge from Annales and
    environmental history *into* new materialism. It is not evidence that historians took
    DeLanda up.
- DeLanda, interview in DvdT (2012) **[read]**, p. 39: "Any materialist philosophy must take as
  its point of departure the existence of a material world that is independent of our minds."
  p. 43: "So yes, neo-materialism is based on the idea that matter has morphogenetic capacities
  of its own and does not need to be commanded into generating form. But we should not attempt
  to build such a philosophy by 'rejecting dualisms' or following any other meta-recipe."

### 2b. Braidotti's own words: available only at second hand or retrospectively

- The 1991 and 1994 phrases are quoted by Dolphijn and van der Tuin, p. 20 **[secondary]**,
  from Braidotti, *Patterns of Dissonance* (1991) pp. 263–266: "a general direction of thought
  is emerging in feminist theory that situates the embodied nature of the subject […] at the
  heart of matter. […] This leads to a radical re-reading of materialism, away from its strictly
  Marxist definition. […] The neo-materialism of Foucault, the new materiality proposed by
  Deleuze are […] a point of no return for feminist theory". And from *Nomadic Subjects* (1994)
  p. 154: "What emerges in poststructuralist feminist reaffirmations of difference is […] a new
  materialist theory of the text and of textual practice". I did not read either book, so
  these are DvdT's transcriptions.
- DvdT p. 19 **[read]** say she "coined the term 'neo-materialism'" in her chapter in Buchanan
  and Colebrook, eds., *Deleuze and Feminist Theory* (2000) (= "Teratologies"; their cite is
  Braidotti 2000, 159).
- Braidotti's retrospective account, interview in DvdT, pp. 20–21 **[read]**: "throughout the
  1980's, a text such as Althusser's 'Pour un materialisme aléatoire' had established a
  consensus across the whole spectrum of his students—Foucault, Deleuze, Balibar. […] the
  post-'68 thinkers had to be simultaneously loyal to the Marxist legacy, but also critical and
  creative in adapting it". p. 21: "by the mid-1990's the differences among the various strands
  and branches of the post-structuralist project were becoming more explicit. The hegemonic
  position acquired by the linguistic branch […] intensified the need for clearer terms of
  demarcation and of theoretical definition. Thus 'neo-materialism' emerges as a method, a
  conceptual frame and a political stand, which refuses the linguistic paradigm, stressing
  instead the concrete yet complex materiality of bodies immersed in social relations of
  power." p. 22 names "a specific brand of situated epistemology (Haraway 1988)".
- The coinage claim itself: DvdT, ch. 5, p. 93 **[read]**: "Manuel DeLanda and Rosi
  Braidotti—independently of one another—first started using 'neo-materialism' or 'new
  materialism' in the second half of the 1990s, for a cultural theory that does not privilege
  the side of culture, but focuses on what Donna Haraway (2003) would call 'naturecultures' or
  what Bruno Latour simply referred to as 'collectives' ([1991] 1993)." The same claim opens the
  Barad interview, p. 48.
- Judgement. DeLanda's 1996 subtitle and 1997 p. 16 are first-hand, in-period uses. Braidotti's
  pre-2000 use rests on DvdT's quotations (1991, 1994). "Coined in the late 1990s by DeLanda
  and Braidotti" is DvdT's 2012 genealogy. The atlas should attribute it to them ("Dolphijn and
  van der Tuin date the term…"), not state it as fact.

## 3. Pre-2000 roots in Latour and Haraway

### 3a. Latour: nonhumans, generalized symmetry, the Parliament of Things (in-period)

- *We Have Never Been Modern* (1993) p. 96 **[read]**: "Callon calls the principle of
  generalized symmetry: the anthropologist has to position himself at the median point where he
  can follow the attribution of both nonhuman and human properties (Callon, 1986). He is not
  allowed to use external reality to explain society, or to use power games to account for what
  shapes external reality." Same page: "quasi-objects, quasi-subjects, proliferate in it. No
  longer unthinkable, it becomes the terrain of all the empirical studies carried out on the
  networks."
- p. 103: "It uses [the] same terms to explain truths and errors (this is the first principle of
  symmetry); it studies the production of humans and nonhumans simultaneously (this is the
  principle of generalized symmetry)".
- p. 144: "Natures are present, but with their representatives, scientists who speak in their
  name. Societies are present, but with the objects that have been serving as their ballast from
  time immemorial. […] it is around them that the Parliament of Things gathers henceforth."
- p. 47 cites Haraway: "Now hybrids, monsters—what Donna [Haraway] calls [cyborgs] and
  [tricksters] (Haraway, [1991]) whose explanation it abandons—are just about everything". The
  OCR dropped the italicised words; the bracketed words are my reconstruction and should be
  checked against a clean copy.
- Note: ANT's own label is pre-2000 (Callon 1986, Law 1986, both already on `sts`
  `translation_association`). I did not read a Latour text that uses "actor-network theory" by
  name; WHNBM speaks of "networks" and "generalized symmetry".

### 3b. Haraway: cyborgs (1985/1991) and objects as actors (1988)

- "A Cyborg Manifesto" (1991 text) **[read]**:
  - p. 150: "By the late twentieth century, our time, a mythic time, we are all chimeras,
    theorized and fabricated hybrids of machine and organism; in short, we are cyborgs. The
    cyborg is our ontology; it gives us our politics. The cyborg is a condensed image of both
    imagination and material reality".
  - p. 151: "Nature and culture are reworked; the one can no longer be the resource for
    appropriation or incorporation by the other." Also p. 151: "three crucial boundary
    breakdowns": human/animal, then (p. 152) "animal-human (organism) and machine", then
    (p. 153) "the boundary between physical and non-physical is very imprecise for us."
  - p. 152: "Late twentieth-century machines have made thoroughly ambiguous the difference
    between natural and artificial, mind and body, self-developing and externally designed […]
    Our machines are disturbingly lively, and we ourselves frighteningly inert."
  - p. 153: "Both chimpanzees and artefacts have politics, so why shouldn't we (de Waal, 1982;
    Winner, 1980)".
- "Situated Knowledges" (1988) **[read]**, section "Objects as Actors: The Apparatus of Bodily
  Production" (p. 591):
  - p. 592: "Situated knowledges require that the object of knowledge be pictured as an actor
    and agent, not as a screen or a ground or a resource, never finally as slave to the master
    that closes off the dialectic in his unique agency and his authorship of 'objective'
    knowledge."
  - p. 593: "granting the status of agent/actor to the 'objects' of the world. Actors come in
    many and wonderful forms." Also: "the world encountered in knowledge projects is an active
    entity. […] The approach I am recommending is not a version of 'realism,' which has proved a
    rather poor way of engaging with the world's active agency." Also: "Ecofeminists have
    perhaps been most insistent on some version of the world as active subject". And: "richly
    evocative figures to promote feminist visualizations of the world as witty agent."
  - p. 595: "a cumbersome entity called a 'material-semiotic actor.' This unwieldy term is
    intended to portray the object of knowledge as an active, meaning-generating part of apparatus
    of bodily production […] 'objects' do not preexist as such. Objects are boundary projects."
  - n. 1, p. 596: Latour's *Les microbes* / *Pasteurization* is cited as the key science-studies
    text: "Latour is not otherwise a notable feminist theorist, but he might be made into one by
    readings as perverse as those he makes of the laboratory".
- So in-period, Haraway and Latour cite each other (SK n. 1, 1988; WHNBM p. 47, 1993). Haraway
  links object agency to feminism and ecofeminism. That is the route DvdT (p. 93) and Braidotti
  (p. 22) later trace.

## 4. Core post-2000 texts

### 4a. Jane Bennett, *Vibrant Matter: A Political Ecology of Things* (2010) **[read]**

- p. vii: "The philosophical project is to think slowly an idea that runs fast through modern
  heads: the idea of matter as passive stuff, as raw, brute, or inert."
- p. viii: "By 'vitality' I mean the capacity of things—edibles, commodities, storms, metals—not
  only to impede or block the will and designs of humans but also to act as quasi agents or
  forces with trajectories, propensities, or tendencies of their own." Then: "The term is Bruno
  Latour's: an actant is a source of action that can be either human or nonhuman". p. ix:
  "Latour strategically elides what is commonly taken as distinctive or even unique about
  humans, and so will I."
- p. x: "In the 'Treatise on Nomadology,' Deleuze and Félix Guattari experiment with the idea of
  a 'material vitalism,' according to which vitality is immanent in matter-energy. That project
  has helped inspire mine."
- p. xiii (distinction from historical materialism): "I pursue a materialism in the tradition of
  Democritus-Epicurus-Spinoza-Diderot-Deleuze more than Hegel-Marx-Adorno. It is important to
  follow the trail of human power to expose social hegemonies (as historical materialists do).
  But my contention is that there is also public value in following the scent of a nonhuman,
  thingly power, the material agency of natural bodies and technological artifacts."
- p. xiv: "Karl Marx sought to demystify commodities and prevent their fetishization by showing
  them to be invested with an agency that belongs to humans". p. xvi: "How did Marx's notion of
  materiality—as economic structures and exchanges that provoke many other events—come to stand
  for the materialist perspective per se?" Ch. 1 n. 51 (p. 129): "Historical materialism would
  not focus on matter but on human power-laden socioeconomic structures."
- pp. 9–10 (the "flat" claim): "Agentic capacity is now seen as differentially distributed across
  a wider range of ontological types." Then: "I agree with John Frow that these differences need
  'to be flattened, read horizontally as a juxtaposition rather than vertically as a hierarchy
  of being.'"
- p. 11 draws on DeLanda 1997 for bones as mineralization: "In the long and slow time of
  evolution, then, mineral material appears as the mover and shaker".
- p. 17 (constructivism): "This impulse toward cultural, linguistic, or historical
  constructivism, which interprets any expression of thing-power as an effect of culture and the
  play of human powers, politicizes moralistic and oppressive appeals to 'nature.' And that is a
  good thing. But the constructivist response to the world also tends to obscure from view
  whatever thing-power there may be." p. 19, quoting Bill Brown's "Thing Theory" (2001) via
  n. 52, which glosses Appadurai's *Social Life of Things*: these tales "disavow … the human
  production of materiality". This is the material-culture line that Bennett says she departs
  from.


### 4b. Karen Barad, "Posthumanist Performativity: Toward an Understanding of How Matter Comes to Matter", *Signs* 28.3 (2003) **[read]**

- p. 801: "Language has been granted too much power. The linguistic turn, the semiotic turn, the
  interpretative turn, the cultural turn: it seems that at every turn lately every 'thing'—even
  materiality—is turned into a matter of language or some other form of cultural representation.
  […] There is an important sense in which the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is
  matter." Also: "Why are language and culture granted their own agency and historicity while
  matter is figured as passive and immutable".
- p. 802 n. 1 (the pre-2000 lineage in her own notes): "Dissatisfaction surfaces in the
  literature in the 1980s. See, e.g., Donna Haraway's 'Gender for a Marxist Dictionary' […] and
  'Situated Knowledges' (originally published 1988)".
- p. 807: "while Andrew Pickering has been one of the very few science studies scholars to take
  ownership of this term [performativity], there is surely a sense in which science studies
  theorists such as Donna Haraway, Bruno Latour, and Joseph Rouse also propound performative
  understandings of the nature of scientific practices." p. 808: "Donna Haraway's scholarly
  opus—from primates to cyborgs to companion species—epitomizes this point." In n. 9 (p. 808) she
  distances herself from Pickering's "posthumanist space".
- p. 809, reading Foucault (the question historians would ask): "To what degree does the matter
  of bodies have its own historicity? Are social forces the only ones susceptible to change? Are
  not biological forces in some sense always already historical ones?"
- p. 810: "My contribution toward the development of such an understanding is based on a
  philosophical account that I have been calling 'agential realism.'" It "takes feminist,
  antiracist, poststructuralist, queer, Marxist, science studies, and scientific insights
  seriously". (A note on p. 814 cites "Barad 1996, forthcoming" for the Bohr argument; DvdT p. 48 date agential realism "in print since 1996".)
- p. 815: "The notion of intra-action (in contrast to the usual 'interaction,' which presumes the
  prior existence of independent entities/relata) represents a profound conceptual shift."
  p. 817: "Agential intra-actions are specific causal material enactments that may or may not
  involve 'humans.'"
- pp. 826–827: "Matter plays an active, indeed agential, role in its iterative materialization"
  (p. 826); "On an agential realist account, agency is cut loose from its traditional humanist
  orbit. […] Agency is a matter of intra-acting; it is an enactment, not something that someone
  or something has. Agency cannot be designated as an attribute of 'subjects' or 'objects' (as
  they do not preexist as such)." (pp. 826–827).
- For the atlas: Barad's agency is not Bennett's thing-power. For Barad, agency is a relation
  ("doing"), not a property of things. Critics of "object agency" (section 6) often run the two
  together.

### 4c. Diana Coole and Samantha Frost, "Introducing the New Materialisms" (2010) **[read]**

- p. 2: "Everywhere we look, it seems to us, we are witnessing scattered but insistent demands
  for more materialist modes of analysis and for new ways of thinking about matter and processes
  of materialization." The examples are "material culture, geopolitical space, critical realism,
  critical international political economy, globalization, and environmentalism". p. 2–3:
  "the more textual approaches associated with the so-called cultural turn are increasingly being
  deemed inadequate for understanding contemporary society".
- p. 3: "materialism's demise since the 1970s has been an effect of the dominance of analytical
  and normative political theory on the one hand and of radical constructivism on the other."
- pp. 6–7, the three themes: "First among them is an ontological reorientation that is resonant
  with, and to some extent informed by, developments in natural science: an orientation that is
  posthumanist in the sense that it conceives of matter itself as lively or as exhibiting agency.
  The second theme entails consideration of a raft of biopolitical and bioethical issues
  concerning the status of life and of the human. Third, new materialist scholarship testifies to
  a critical and nondogmatic reengagement with political economy". p. 7: "In distinction from
  some recent examples of constructivism, new materialists emphasize the productivity and
  resilience of matter."
- p. 9: "Jane Bennett has provocatively labeled this an 'enchanted materialism,' ascribing agency
  to inorganic phenomena such as the electricity grid, food, and trash". Also: "new materialists
  are rediscovering a materiality that materializes".
- p. 25 (on historical materialism): what is new is "its practitioners' reinvention of materialism
  in response to criticisms that radical constructivists and deconstructionists rightly made of
  earlier critical materialisms and realisms, Marxism in particular".
- Latour appears only in a note (n. 19, p. 40: *Politics of Nature*). Haraway's cyborg appears at
  p. 17 (n. 21). Coole and Frost's line runs through political theory, phenomenology, Marxism and
  biopolitics; the Bennett/Barad line runs through STS. The proposed entry should say that "new
  materialisms" is plural and the strands differ.

### 4d. Dolphijn and van der Tuin, *New Materialism: Interviews & Cartographies* (2012) **[read]**

- p. 13: "'New materialism' or 'neo-materialism' is such a new metaphysics. A plethora of
  contemporary scholars from heterogeneous backgrounds has, since the late 1990s up until now,
  been producing (re-)readings that together work towards its actualization."
- p. 93 (genealogy): see 2b. It names Haraway's "naturecultures" and Latour's "collectives".
- Barad interview, p. 48: "The idea behind 'agential realism,' in print since 1996".
- Note the book's series: "New Metaphysics", edited by Graham Harman and Bruno Latour (series page in
  the front matter; acknowledgements thank both). That is an institutional tie between Latour and the 2012 consolidation of the
  label.


## 5. Historians' uptake: the "material turn" in history (post-2000)

### 5a. Frank Trentmann (2009): the clearest historian's statement, and a critique **[read]**

- p. 283: "Things are back. After the turn to discourse and signs in the late twentieth century,
  there is a new fascination with the material stuff of life." Also: "Unlike the linguistic turn,
  however, with its roots in poststructural thought, the material turn has multiple sources." He
  cites Haraway's "A Cyborg Manifesto" (1991 text) in n. 1.
- p. 284: "Most radically, greater humility toward things has led to calls for a more
  object-oriented democracy in which subaltern things will be liberated from the humanist rule of
  subject-centered discourse." (n. 6: Latour, "From Realpolitik to Dingpolitik"; Olsen;
  Domańska 2006.) "Like words in the postmodern 1980s, things today are shaking our fundamental
  understandings of subjectivity, agency, emotions, and the relations between humans and
  nonhumans."
- p. 285 (old material culture versus the new questions): "Arguably, the very success of
  eighteenth-century material culture studies has had a conservative side effect. Most scholars
  have tended to take as given what material culture is and how to study it. […] It has resulted
  in a widening gulf between material culture, focused on identities and representations, and
  material politics and political economy." n. 7 lists McKendrick, Brewer and Plumb; Brewer and
  Porter; Weatherill (all on the atlas's `consumption` entry). He takes his title from Appadurai,
  who in 2005 said the commodity-biography work "now seems quaint".
- p. 290: "The most influential (if controversial) platform for exploring the 'agency' of things
  in the past couple of decades has been actor-network theory (ANT). Spearheaded by Bruno Latour,
  Michel Callon, John Law, and others, ANT tries to transcend multiple dualities". Then n. 33
  (p. 290): "We should also note the difficulty of integrating historical formations of power and
  culture in an analysis primarily concerned with reconstructing a network of actors and actants
  at a specific moment in time."
- p. 291 (a historian's objection to ANT's history): "It is perhaps ironic that a theory like ANT,
  so concerned with resisting grand narratives and abstractions (like 'society'), draws on such a
  flat view of modern history. […] It is hard to see a general turning away from things, as
  presumed by Latour and others." p. 301: "this rather monochromatic view of modernity supposedly
  so divorced from things". Also: "Before simply rejecting Latour as problematic history, however,
  we might historicize his concerns into questions for research."
- pp. 301–302 on Patrick Joyce (*The Rule of Freedom*, 2003), Otter, Crook and Vernon: "the
  scholars who have especially engaged with these questions have done so coming from a late
  Foucauldian background". Then: "the problem of this theory-conscious literature is perhaps not
  that it goes too far but that it does not go far enough. Its interest in materiality remains
  tied to a late Foucauldian interest in the disciplining force of technology. The material world
  appears a fairly pliant instrument of rule."
- p. 307 (his settlement on "agency"): "this point has become linked with the more controversial
  claim that things have 'agency.' Whatever we think about this stronger claim, it should not
  distract from the more easily acceptable proposition that things and humans are inseparably
  interwoven in mutually constitutive relationships." And: "The material world has too much
  history in it to leave it to the social sciences."
- Note: Trentmann does **not** use "new materialism". He writes of the "material turn" and of ANT.
  He is a contributor to the turn and a qualified critic of object agency, not an adherent.

### 5b. Timothy LeCain (2015): an environmental historian who names himself "neo-materialist" **[read]**

- p. 1, title and abstract: "Against the Anthropocene: A Neo-Materialist Perspective". The article
  "draws on emerging neo-materialist theory to suggest a radically different approach that
  emphasizes the ways in which humans and their cultures have been created by and with a powerful
  material environment."
- p. 2: "a vein of post-humanist thinking referred to as 'new materialism' or 'neo-materialism'.
  […] neo-materialist theory proposes that humans and their cultures are best understood as the
  products of their material environment, not its masters."
- p. 12 links the turn to Chakrabarty (relevant to the Chakrabarty 1.132 packet): "It was a sign
  of the times when the well-known postcolonialist Dipesh Chakrabarty surprised many with his 2009
  manifesto, 'The Climate of History' […]. Other scholars have begun to organize under the broad
  banner of what they identify as a 'new materialism'". Also: "Matter is back, and it's back in a
  big way – perhaps even as big as the cultural turn it in some ways seeks to replace."
- p. 14 cites Coole and Frost and Bennett. p. 15 says: "many will have likely spotted the telltale
  fingerprints of Bruno Latour and Actor Network Theory (ANT), which has indeed escaped its
  balkanization in Science and Technology Studies to influence a much wider scholarly audience."
- p. 16 (the environment link, in a historian's words): "new materialists have much they can
  learn from the work of environmental historians, a field that surprisingly few new materialists
  have incorporated into their theories and methods. As one of the founders of the discipline,
  Donald Worster, was already insisting in 2003, the 'unexamined cultural determinism which
  underlies mainstream historiography is just as problematic' as any form of environmental or
  materialist determinism." The Worster quotation is taken from Foltz 2003 (n. 36). Also:
  "Joined by some intrepid historians of technology, environmental historians have been mounting
  indirect attacks on the conventional boundaries between culture and matter". He names Edmund
  Russell's "evolutionary history" (p. 16) and Timothy Mitchell's *Carbon Democracy* (p. 17).
- LeCain's book *The Matter of History: How Things Create the Past* (Cambridge, 2017) is
  **[metadata]** only.

### 5c. ANT read into a historian's book: Callon on Timothy Mitchell (2006/2010) **[read]**

- Callon, *Gérer et Comprendre* 100 (2010), p. 82 (standfirst): "Mosquitos are more dangerous than
  tanks […] we must decompartmentalize the social sciences and draw the natural sphere closer to
  the social one, and technology closer to politics". p. 83, on Mitchell's ch. 1 "Can the Mosquito
  Speak?": "This sociotechnical complexity no longer surprises social scientists. We now know that
  humans and nonhumans weave relations with each other and that we cannot, therefore, a priori
  separate social forces or causes from natural ones." The epigraph quotes Mitchell, *Rule of
  Experts*, p. 303 **[secondary]**: "Capitalism has no singular logic, no essence. It survives
  parasitically … taking up residence in human bodies and minds, or in sugar cane or private
  property".
- Mitchell's own chapter was **not read** (no open copy).

### 5d. Other historians' uptake (abstract or secondary only; do not quote as read)

- AHR Conversation (2009) **[abstract]**, editor's framing: "As a field, the historical study of
  material culture is no longer new." Among its questions: "Is there a meaningful distinction
  between what is human and what is material?" Participants: Leora Auslander, Amy Bentley, Leor
  Halevi, H. Otto Sibum, Christopher Witmore.
- Domańska (2006) **[abstract]**: "the recent call in the human sciences for a 'return to things.'
  This renewed interest in things signals a rejection of constructivism and textualism". She
  refers "to bruno latour's idea of the agency of things and object-oriented democracy".
- Steinberg (2002) **[secondary]**: the AHA notice says he "contends that taking environmental
  history seriously means that historians must recognize that nature is more than a mere
  backdrop" and that he "insists that historians should begin to recognize the environment as a
  critical factor affecting human agency." This is a pre-new-materialist, in-field version of the
  agency-of-nature argument (cf. Worster, Cronon).
- Bennett and Joyce, *Material Powers* (2010) **[abstract]**: "This edited collection is a major
  contribution to the current development of a 'material turn' in the social sciences and
  humanities. It does so by exploring new understandings of how power is made up and exercised by
  examining the role of material infrastructures in the organisation of state power and the role
  of material cultural practices in the organisation of colonial forms of governance." The cases
  include "territorial engineering projects in seventeenth-century France" (Mukerji's case). The
  concerns "are connected to major critical re-examination of the limits of the earlier
  formulations of cultural materialism and the logic of the 'cultural turn'." Trentmann 2009 (5a)
  places Joyce in a "late Foucauldian" line: governmentality and technopolitics rather than
  vital matter.
- Mukerji (2020) **[abstract]**: "I argue that the material turn in my work and the study of
  logistics as a form of power became important to me because of my archival experience." Her
  in-period work is *Territorial Ambitions and the Gardens of Versailles* (1997) **[metadata]**:
  reviewed in *AHR* 104.1 (1999) by Konvitz. It is a pre-2000 historical-sociological study of
  material power, but I have none of her own words on Latour or new materialism.
- Nash (2005): **not read**. A web summary attributes to her (p. 67) the view that nature "may
  resist and complicate human actions" but has neither intentionality nor choice. Unverified; do
  not use until read.

## 6. Critics

### 6a. Ahmed (2008): the "founding gestures" critique (feminist) — **[secondary]** via Bruining 2016

- Bruining, p. 22 **[read]**, quoting Ahmed 2008, p. 25: sentences in which new materialists
  characterise cultural theory's past "are repeated without illustration or contextualization",
  which results in "the 'routinization' of the gesture of pointing towards the anti-biologism or
  constructionism of others".
- Bruining, p. 34 **[read]**, quoting Ahmed p. 26: feminist work on biology "disappears in the very
  argument that we must return to the biological"; and Ahmed p. 24: "by constructing feminism as
  'prohibiting' an attention to the biological and other matters, this new work is often
  represented as a gift to feminism in its very refusal to be prohibited by feminism's
  prohibitions".
- Bruining's own thesis, p. 21 **[read]**: texts relying on these gestures "are not only founded
  on a misrepresentation of postmodern and poststructuralist thought, but are also guilty of
  repeating the perceived mistakes of which they are critical, such as upholding the
  language/matter dichotomy." He names replies in the same debate: van der Tuin, "Deflationary
  Logic" (*EJWS* 15.4, 2008), and Davis (*EJWS* 16.1, 2009) (p. 31 n. 49) **[metadata]**.
- The objection lands on Barad 2003's opening (4b: "Language has been granted too much power"),
  on Coole and Frost p. 3 ("radical constructivism"), and on Bennett's "constructivist response"
  (p. 17). For a teaching entry this is the critique to pair with the founding claims.

### 6b. Historians and others on object agency and "flatness"

- Trentmann 2009 (5a) **[read]**: (i) ANT "draws on such a flat view of modern history" (p. 291);
  (ii) ANT has difficulty "integrating historical formations of power and culture" (p. 290 n. 33);
  (iii) on "agency", the "more controversial claim" should not displace "the more easily acceptable
  proposition that things and humans are inseparably interwoven" (p. 307). This is the best
  historian-authored, read evidence for a critique edge.
- Hornborg (*EJST* 20.1) **[abstract]**, framed as environmental history ("Toward a critical theory of global
  environmental history"): the article "challenges the urge within Actor-Network Theory,
  posthumanism, and the ontological turn in sociology and anthropology to dissolve analytical
  distinctions between subject and object, society and nature, and human and non-human. It argues
  that only by acknowledging such distinctions and applying a realist ontology can exploitative
  and unsustainable global power relations be exposed." Also: "The incompatibility of posthumanist
  and Marxist approaches to the Anthropocene and the question of agency".
- Ingold 2007 **[abstract]**, from anthropology/archaeology: "things are active not because they are
  imbued with agency but because of ways in which they are caught up in these currents of the
  lifeworld."
- Malm, *The Progress of This Storm* (2018): **not read**. Skiveren 2023 **[abstract]** reports
  "a reinvigoration of eco-Marxist thinking, which not only offers new focus points but also
  launches philosophical polemics against the field's longstanding turn to matter", staged as Malm
  versus Bennett.
- Devellennes and Dillet 2018 **[abstract]**: "Building a critique of the work of Diana Coole,
  Samantha Frost, and Jane Bennett". The new materialisms "are plural".
- In-period background (already in the atlas's science-studies evidence): Bloor, "Anti-Latour"
  (1999), objects to Latour's treatment of nonhumans and society. That is an SSK critique of the
  ANT root, not of new materialism, and it predates the label.
- Self-limits inside the texts **[read]**: DeLanda (DvdT p. 43) warns against building the
  philosophy "by 'rejecting dualisms' or following any other meta-recipe". Barad 2003, pp. 826–827,
  denies that agency is "an attribute" of things. Bennett, p. ix, says she brackets subjectivity
  only "for a while and up to a point". Critics who treat "object agency" as one doctrine miss
  these differences.

## 7. Distinctions the entry must keep

1. **From historical materialism (`karl`).** Bennett p. xiii: "a materialism in the tradition of
   Democritus-Epicurus-Spinoza-Diderot-Deleuze more than Hegel-Marx-Adorno". Her ch. 1 n. 51
   (p. 129): "Historical materialism would not focus on matter but on human power-laden
   socioeconomic structures." Braidotti (DvdT p. 20): "a radical re-reading of materialism, away
   from its strictly Marxist definition" (1991, as quoted). But Coole and Frost's third theme is "a
   critical and nondogmatic reengagement with political economy" (p. 7), and they treat their
   materialism as a reinvention after constructivist criticisms of "Marxism in particular" (p. 25).
   Critics such as Hornborg (abstract) and Malm (not read) argue from historical materialism. Suggested wording:
   "Distinct from historical materialism, which explains through human social relations of
   production; some new materialists reject it, others try to rework it."
2. **From material-culture / consumption history (`consumption`).** Trentmann p. 285 contrasts
   eighteenth-century material culture studies "focused on identities and representations" with
   new questions of agency and material politics. Bennett ch. 1 n. 52 marks her distance from the
   Appadurai *Social Life of Things* line via Bill Brown. Consumption history asks what goods meant
   and how they circulated. New materialism asks what matter does and whether "agency" belongs
   only to humans. The two meet in "thing" studies (Appadurai, Brown) and should be linked by a
   comparison, not merged.
3. **From the linguistic turn (`language`).** Every core text defines itself against "the cultural
   turn" or "linguistic paradigm" (Barad p. 801; Coole and Frost pp. 2–3; Braidotti in DvdT p. 21;
   LeCain p. 12). Ahmed's critique (6a) is that this contrast is a caricature. A critique or
   comparison edge to `language` is supportable, but the Ahmed objection must sit beside it.
4. **From STS/ANT (`sts`, `latour`).** ANT is a method in the sociology of science and technology
   that follows associations of humans and nonhumans (WHNBM pp. 96, 103). New materialism is a
   broader metaphysical and political programme that draws on ANT, Deleuze and feminist science
   studies. Bennett borrows "actant" (p. viii); LeCain sees "the telltale fingerprints of Bruno
   Latour" (p. 15); Barad keeps her distance (pp. 807–808).


## 8. Proposal (evidence for a later revision batch; not a graph edit)

### 8a. Entry

- **id**: `new_materialism`. **label**: "New materialisms / the material turn". Use the plural,
  following Coole and Frost p. 7 and Devellennes and Dillet. Pair with "material turn", the
  historians' term (Trentmann p. 283; *Material Powers*; LeCain p. 12).
- **date_label**: "Roots in actor-network theory and feminist science studies, 1980s–90s ·
  'neo-materialism' coined late 1990s · consolidation 2003–2012". Pre-2000 anchors: Haraway 1985
  and 1988, Callon 1986 and Latour 1993, DeLanda 1996 and 1997, Braidotti 1991 and 1994 (as quoted
  by DvdT). Post-2000 anchors: Barad 2003 and 2007, Bennett 2010, Coole and Frost 2010, DvdT 2012.
  Historians: Trentmann 2009, Bennett and Joyce 2010, LeCain 2015.
- **layer**: `historiographical_developments`. **period**: none of period_0–3 fits. Let the
  open-frame revision (1.133) decide; otherwise period_3 with coverage marked post-2000.
- **entry_kind**: group; entry_type "School or movement". Better: a "cluster of approaches",
  since the texts disagree (Barad's relational agency versus Bennett's thing-power; Coole and
  Frost's political economy).
- **description sketch** (about 90 words): "Treats matter as active rather than inert. Some
  approaches ask what bodies, things, energy and other organisms do, and how agency is distributed
  or enacted across human and nonhuman relations. The roots are actor-network theory's nonhumans
  and generalized symmetry, Haraway's cyborg and her 'objects as actors', and DeLanda's
  'neo-materialist' history of matter-energy. After 2000, Barad, Bennett, and Coole and Frost
  set the agenda against the 'cultural turn'. Historians took up a 'material turn' unevenly.
  Trentmann historicised ANT's account of modernity; LeCain wrote environmental history as
  neo-materialist. Ahmed contested its picture of feminism's past. The entry is distinct from
  historical materialism and from the history of consumption and material culture."
- **scope_note**: "Not historical materialism (explanation by social relations of production), and
  not consumption or material-culture history (what goods meant and how they moved). It overlaps
  with both: Coole and Frost re-engage political economy, and thing studies descend from
  Appadurai."
