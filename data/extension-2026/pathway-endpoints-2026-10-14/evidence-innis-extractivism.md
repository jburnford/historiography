# PART A. Innis → dependency through Canadian political economy

## A1. Texts read (in-period, full text unless noted)

All CJPST items are open PDFs on the journal's UVic OJS archive (Canadian Journal of Political
and Social Theory, now CTheory); page numbers are the printed ones, mapped page by page.

1. **Melville H. Watkins, "A Staple Theory of Economic Growth," CJEPS 29:2 (1963): 141–158.**
   [read: opening paragraphs only, Crossref full-text deposit of the first page, p. 141.
   Body of the article NOT read; JSTOR/De Gruyter paywalled.]
   - "The staple approach to the study of economic history is primarily a Canadian innovation;
     indeed, it is Canada's most distinctive contribution to political economy. It is
     undeveloped in any explicit form in most countries where the export sector of the economy
     is or was dominant." (p. 141)
   - Innis's approach "was more technological history writ large than a theory of economic
     growth in the conventional sense." (p. 141)
   - Note: the 1963 article is a *growth* theory, not a dependency argument. Rotstein's 2014
     retrospective (A2 below) presents it as Canada's counter-case to Prebisch's pessimism.

2. **Mel Watkins, "The Staple Theory Revisited," JCS 12:5 (1977): 83–95.** [metadata only:
   Crossref record, no abstract; UTP Journals paywall. NOT read.] Watkins 1982 (n. 11) cites
   it at p. 85 and records its reprint in Melody, Salter and Heyer, eds., *Culture,
   Communication and Dependency: The Tradition of H. A. Innis* (Norwood, NJ, 1981) — the
   title of that volume is itself evidence that by 1981 Innis was being read under the sign
   of "dependency" [metadata as cited by Watkins 1982, n. 11].

3. **Mel Watkins, "The Innis Tradition in Canadian Political Economy," CJPST 6:1–2
   (1982): 12–34.** [read in full; earlier versions given 1978–81, n. 1]
   - Drache "has argued that there are (even within the liberal paradigm) two versions of the
     staple theory: Innis' dependency model and W.A. Mackintosh's growth-model" (p. 16).
   - Postwar Keynesianism meant for Canada "a continuing if not increasing commitment to the
     export of staple products developed by and for foreign capital, that is, economic growth
     at the expense of deepening dependency" (p. 16).
   - A modernized staple theory "would have confronted the economic historian with Canada's
     role as a resource hinterland within the American empire, that is, with Canada's
     dependency ... For the orthodox paradigm, however, what could not be risked was the
     discovery of neo-colonialism" (p. 18).
   - Work on foreign ownership "by, for example, Aitken himself, the early Stephen Hymer and
     Kari Levitt" (p. 19).
   - "What was above all at risk was the discovery of dependency" (p. 19).
   - Drache's Innisian theory: "In Drache's terms, 'rigidities' result in 'incomplete
     development' or dependency" (p. 20).
   - "In Canada, because of the central importance of dependency, these developments have been
     animated by a powerful strand of nationalism"; "Innis became, by default, the property of
     the left. This is admittedly ironic given Innis' own unwillingness to have any truck or
     trade with the intellectual left" (p. 26).
   - Political economy "has been influenced to some degree (and in some cases - such as my own
     - decisively so) by Innis and his school" (p. 27); he names McNally's critique (p. 27).
   - Watkins never names Frank, Wallerstein, Amin or Latin American dependency theory in this
     essay; his vocabulary is "dependency", "hinterland", "satellitic", "neo-colonialism".

4. **Daniel Drache, "Harold Innis and Canadian Capitalist Development," CJPST 6:1–2 (1982):
   35–60.** [read: opening pp. 35–36 and section "The World System: The Origin of Rigidities",
   pp. 49–50]
   - Innis's research "produced ample evidence that staple-led growth leads to an
     ever-deepening arc of dependency of the hinterland on the metropole" (p. 35).
   - "Innis' pioneering examination of rigidities should be considered a natural bridge between
     neoclassical economic theory and Marxist theory of dependency and capital accumulation at
     the periphery"; rigidities "were finally responsible for Canada's semi-peripheral economic
     status" (p. 36).
   - "Although not as systematic in generalizing the laws of capitalist development as for
     instance-and more recently-Samir Amin, there is a striking similarity between Innis' theory
     of rigidities and Amin's theory of extroversion. Even though one must tread cautiously and
     avoid superficial comparisons between developed dependency and stark underdevelopment, Innis
     and Amin share a common perspective ..." (p. 49; Amin, *Unequal Development*, 1976, n. 50).
   - Innis "placed particular importance in examining the evolution of capitalism as a world
     system" (p. 49).
   - This is the clearest in-period text that *explicitly* joins Innis to a named dependency /
     world-system theorist (Amin), and it is a Canadian reader making the join, not Amin.

5. **Daniel Drache and Arthur Kroker, "The Labyrinth of Dependency" (editorial), CJPST 7:3
   (1983): 5–24.** [read pp. 5–13]
   - The special issue "is intended to deepen and intensify the tradition of dependency theory"
     (p. 5).
   - Political economists are "abandoning the indigenous tradition of dependency theory as
     represented most formidably by the communications theory and political economy of Harold
     Innis in favour of a Eurocentric class analysis" (p. 7).
   - Dependency theory can absorb "systemic analyses of the structural origins of world economic
     crisis (Wallerstein)" (p. 10).
   - "Kari Levitt's pioneering study, Silent Surrender. The Multinational Corporation in Canada
     was part of the intellectual tradition of dependency theory, but marginal to it for the
     reason that Canada was part of the system of advanced industrial societies and not a member
     of the third world"; Canada was "simultaneously rich and underdeveloped", an "advanced
     dependent" society (p. 11).
   - They quote Levitt's opening paragraph ("This book presents a sketch of Canada's slide into a
     position of economic, political and cultural dependence on the United States") and her "new
     mercantilism of corporate empires" passage (pp. 11–12). [secondary quotation of Levitt
     1970; Levitt's own page numbers not given and not checked]

6. **Daniel Drache, "The Crisis of Canadian Political Economy: Dependency Theory Versus the New
   Orthodoxy," CJPST 7:3 (1983): 25–49** (printed pagination; OJS metadata says 26–49; printed
   p. 40 is missing from the scan). [read in full apart from p. 40]
   - Canada as a "semi-peripheral social and economic formation", like Australia and New
     Zealand (p. 25).
   - "The Innisian theory is based on an extraverted model of development, while for
     Mackintosh, development is auto-centric" (p. 27).
   - "what I call a dependent resource commercial economy" (p. 35).
   - Canada "falls between social formations, having the social relations of advanced capitalism
     and the economic structures of dependency" (p. 36).
   - Heterodoxy: "The principal contributions of Pentland, Ryerson and Clement are solidly in
     this camp, as are more obviously Naylor, Levitt and Watkins" (p. 37).
   - Self-critique of the school: Clement's study of elites "suffers from a similar ambiguity"
     (the "unequal" alliance vs. an autonomous Canadian corporate elite) (p. 38); of Levitt,
     "there was no silent surrender: the surrender occured with the active participation of the
     state and the Canadian elites" (p. 39).
   - "The charge is often made by more orthodox marxists that it is the dependency theorists
     such as Naylor, Clement, Levitt who in focusing on externalities have ignored or minimized
     the importance of 'internal' developments!" (p. 42).
   - "it is the Innisian tradition that despite its liberal origins, has the potential for giving
     Marxism a new resonance" (p. 43).

7. **Wallace Clement, "Macrosociological Approaches Toward a 'Canadian Sociology',"
   *Alternate Routes* 1 (1977): 1–37.** [read pp. 13–18; open PDF, alternateroutes.ca]
   - Introduces "frameworks, developed mainly for Latin America's relationship with advanced
     capitalist nations, based on regional power relationships" — Frank, Galtung, Sunkel
     (p. 13), quoting Frank's "within this world-embracing metropolis-satellite structure the
     metropoles tend to develop and the satellites to underdevelop" (p. 14, citing Frank 1969).
   - Canadian work so far "is at the level of the Frank 'development of underdevelopment'
     thesis"; "Mel Watkins, for instance, suggests using a 'center-margin framework' similar to
     Frank's model", while "arguing that in the international framework, 'Canada would lie above
     the line as one of the exploiting nations rather than an exploited nation' (40)" (p. 17;
     Watkins, "The Branch Plant Condition," in A. K. Davis, ed., *Canadian Confrontations:
     Hinterland vs. Metropolis*, 1970 — secondary quotation, Watkins unread).
   - "These frameworks have a particular promise for Canadian social science as models because
     they reflect a centralist tradition important in Canada's development ... as exemplified in
     Innis's staple theory and the Lower/Creighton 'Laurentian thesis'. Each has been built on an
     analysis of structural power links binding them into a whole founded on geography,
     regionalism, and resource extraction" (p. 18).
   - This is the in-period text in which a Canadian dependency writer explicitly lays Frank
     beside Innis.

## A2. Critics of the staples–dependency fusion (in their own words)

8. **Leo Panitch, "Dependency and Class in Canadian Political Economy," *Studies in Political
   Economy* 6 (1981): 7–33.** [read pp. 7–10 and notes; SPE open archive, via Wayback copy of
   spe.library.utoronto.ca]
   - Joins and criticises at once: "The early dependency theories bore a certain similarity to
     the long standing and home-grown staples thesis. While the former had a Marxian vocabulary
     and inspiration and the latter was in the tradition of classical political economy, the two
     converged in their concern with unequal exchange between societies" (p. 7).
   - "the new political economy rather uncritically borrowed formulations on dependency and
     development from Latin America and the Caribbean with little regard to their degree of
     compatibility with the Marxian class analysis which it was also developing" (p. 8).
   - "The greatest shortcoming of the new political economy in Canada is its tendency to view
     dependency in neo-mercantilist terms. Ever since Kari Levitt used the notion of the 'new
     mercantilism' as the centrepiece of her influential work ..." (p. 9).
   - On Frank: "Whereas Gunder Frank sought to differentiate between North American (including
     Canadian) and Latin American development ... by arguing that the former had the blessing of
     being 'ignored' by the metropole ..., the new Canadian political economy argued that the
     Latin American and Canadian case were fundamentally the same" (p. 10; Frank, *Lumpen
     Bourgeoisie: Lumpen Development*, 1972, p. 19, n. 5). Then quotes Clement, *Continental
     Corporate Power* (1977), p. 129: "The experiences of Canada and Latin America, when
     compared, are similar in the underdevelopment of industrial sectors ..." (p. 10).
   - Levitt quoted by Panitch (n. 3, p. 31; Levitt, *Silent Surrender*, pp. 23–4): "The central
     thesis of our argument is that the subsidiaries and branch plants of large American-based
     multinational corporations have replaced the operations of the earlier European-based
     mercantile venture companies in extracting the staple and organizing the supply of
     manufactured goods." [secondary quotation; Levitt's book not read]
   - Note: the only engagement by Frank with *Canada* found here is Panitch's report of Frank's
     1972 contrast, which runs *against* a Canada-as-Latin-America reading. Frank's engagement
     with *Innis* is not found.

9. **David McNally, "Staple Theory as Commodity Fetishism: Marx, Innis and Canadian Political
   Economy," *Studies in Political Economy* 6 (1981): 35–63.** [read pp. 35–37, 41 and notes]
   - "Frequently, contemporary neo-Marxian theories of economic dependency have been considered
     to provide the framework for this fusion of Innisian and Marxian analysis ... This article
     constitutes a challenge to this prevailing orthodoxy in radical Canadian political economy"
     (p. 35).
   - "The first work to attempt a radical appropriation of Innis was Kari Levitt's Silent
     Surrender" (p. 35); in her ch. 6 "Metropolis and Hinterland" the "central thesis of modern
     dependency theory ... is formulated clearly", and "Levitt takes over a now-famous passage
     from Innis' The Fur Trade in Canada and inserts it into the general framework of dependency
     theory" (p. 36; n. 2: Levitt quotes Innis at *Silent Surrender* p. 47).
   - Watkins's essay "Resources and Underdevelopment" (in Robert Laxer, ed., *(Canada) Ltd.: The
     Political Economy of Dependency*, 1973, pp. 115–16) "linked Innis' staple theory with the
     work of the Latin American dependency school" (p. 37, n. 7). McNally quotes Watkins 1977
     ("Staple Theory Revisited", p. 88) that Naylor's model "is a Marxist version of the
     Innisian version" (p. 37, n. 6). [secondary quotation of Watkins 1977 with its page]
   - The objection: "For versions of the staple theory, as for modern dependency theory, '...
     capitalism is very broadly conceptualized in terms of exchange based on trade and
     investment' ... these theorists fetishize the sphere of commodity circulation" (p. 41).

10. **Jorge Niosi, "The Canadian Bourgeoisie: Towards a Synthetical Approach," CJPST 7:3
    (1983): 128–149.** [read pp. 128, 138, 141]
    - "Theoretically left wing Nationalism was nourished by Latin American dependency perspectives
      either reformist (of the R. Prebisch and C. Furtado variety) or Marxist (in the P. Baran,
      P. Sweezy or A.G. Frank tradition)" (p. 128).
    - Quotes Pratt and Richards: "The 'Left nationalist' version of dependency theory ... could
      not account for the emergence in the 1970's of an ambitions arriviste bourgeoisie in a
      province such as Alberta" (p. 138). [secondary quotation]
    - "the core-periphery dichotomy which pervades the Socialist debate, mainly in the
      Nationalist approach, is in my view an overly simplistic scheme"; Canada's "intermediate
      position in the modern world system" (p. 141).

11. **Gordon Laxer, *Open for Business: The Roots of Foreign Ownership in Canada* (1989), and
    "Foreign Ownership and Myths about Canadian Development," CRSA 22:3 (1985): 311–345.**
    [NOT read: book access-restricted on the Internet Archive; article paywalled. Laxer's words
    below are as quoted in David McNally's review essay, "Political Economy Without the Working
    Class?", *Labour/Le Travail* 25 (1990): 217–226, read in full.]
    - Laxer's starting point is "sharp rejection of both traditional cornerstones of
      Left-nationalist political economy: staples theory, and the Naylor-Clement thesis"; he
      argues that "staple traps do not inevitably follow from external and geographic factors"
      (McNally p. 221, citing Laxer pp. 29, 27).
    - Canada's divergence from Sweden is "overwhelmingly internal not external" (McNally's
      paraphrase, p. 222); "the reason the Canadian state did not defend domestic ownership of
      Canadian industry is ironic: the capitalists were too powerful" (McNally p. 222, citing
      Laxer p. 151).
    - McNally (1990) on the field: Levitt's *Silent Surrender* is "the founding document of the
      new Canadian political economy" (p. 217); in Clement and Williams, eds., *The New Canadian
      Political Economy* (1989), "dozens upon dozens of references to Harold Innis eclipse the
      mere handful of citations from Marx" (p. 218); Clement "defends the notion of a political
      economy which 'adds' a theory of class exploitation to the staple theory of inequality
      among nations, while suggesting that this involves 'reversing Innis's logic' (39,40)"
      (p. 219; secondary quotation of Clement 1989).
    - Laxer's later voice (2014, read): in *The Staple Theory @ 50* he writes on "Alberta's
      Sands, Staples and Traps" (pp. 53–57) — not mined here.

12. **Historians: Naylor's dependency business history and Michael Bliss.**
    - Gerald Tulchinsky, "Recent Controversies in Canadian Business History," *Acadiensis* 8:1
      (1978): 133–139 [read in full]: summarises Naylor's claim that "Canada was still in the
      'staple trap' during the pre-World War I period" (p. 135), then objects: "The fundamental
      problem with Naylor's work is that many of his major assertions are not proven" (p. 136);
      he names "the seriously flawed assumption ... that nascent industrialists were crying out
      for more capital" (p. 136), and Naylor's "cavalier or polemical style, irresponsible use of
      evidence, inadequate research, simplistic comparisons, and cheap epithets" (p. 139), while
      allowing the book is "an impressive and important contribution" (p. 139).
    - R. T. Naylor, "The History of Canadian Business: A Reply," *Histoire sociale / Social
      History* (1977), pp. 152–159 [read; vol./issue not checked; Wayback copy] answers Bliss's
      review (HS/SH 9:18, Nov. 1976, pp. 446–449; NOT read). Naylor's dependency claim: "business
      decisions in Canada reflected the country's status as an economic dependency" (p. 153).
      Bliss's objections survive only as Naylor quotes them: "by definition the explanation would
      have to involve a failure of entrepreneurship" and "the tariff was not revised upward in
      1907" (pp. 153–154). [secondary quotation of Bliss]
    - Bliss's own counter-thesis, as quoted in J. Igartua's review of *Northern Enterprise*
      (RHAF 41:3, 1988, pp. 419–422, read): "The invisible hand grinds on, sometimes slowly,
      always inexorably, and its power always wears down the structures created by human hands"
      (Bliss 1987, p. 10, quoted by Igartua p. 420); Igartua summarises that for Bliss Canada
      suffered "non pas d'un manque d'entrepreneurship, mais d'une surabondance" (p. 420).
      [secondary; Bliss's book unread]
    - Kellogg, "State, Capital and World Economy: Bukharin's Marxism and the 'Dependency/Class'
      Controversy," CJPS 22:2 (1989): 337–362 [abstract only]: "Canada defied the predictions of
      dependency theory and developed all the class relations appropriate to advanced capitalist
      societies. The origins of Canadian industrial capitalism were not such that the country was
      locked into a staple-trap, notwithstanding the very real reliance of the economy on
      staple-export."

## A3. Did dependency / world-systems theorists outside Canada engage Innis?

- **Wallerstein** [read: *The Modern World-System I*, 1974; UC Press 2011 ed., local copy]:
  uses Innis only as an empirical source on the fisheries — ch. 4 n. 133 quotes Innis, "The
  Rise and Fall of the Spanish Fishery in Newfoundland" (1931): "The decline of the Spanish
  fishery [in Newfoundland] is the reverse side of the opening of the Spanish market to France,
  England and New England fisheries"; ch. 5 n. 183 quotes *The Cod Fisheries* (1940), p. 49;
  ch. 5 n. 278 cites *The Cod Fisheries*, pp. 30–81. (Endnote numbers given; the e-book has no
  printed page numbers.) No engagement with the staple *theory*.
- **Frank**: Panitch (1981, p. 10) reports Frank's 1972 view that North America, including
  Canada, was "ignored" by the metropole — the opposite of the Canadian left-nationalist
  reading. No Frank text citing Innis was found or read.
- **Amin**: the join is Drache's (1982, p. 49), not Amin's.
- **Secondary, later (2014), read**: Gerry Helleiner, "Staples Theory on the International
  Stage," in J. Stanford, ed., *The Staple Theory @ 50* (CCPA, 2014; 2020 PDF): in development
  debates in Latin America, the Caribbean and Africa "Sometimes Watkins was footnoted in the
  relevant writings, sometimes not. (To my knowledge Innis never was.)" (p. 31); he names the
  Caribbean plantation-economy tradition, "not least our own Kari Polanyi-Levitt" (p. 31).
  Abraham Rotstein, in the same volume (p. 13), presents Watkins 1963 as Canada's answer to
  Prebisch's "dependencia doctrine": Canada "had in fact found a way out of its own staples
  dependence".
- Conclusion: the traffic ran one way. Canadian political economists read Frank, Amin, Sunkel,
  Galtung and Wallerstein into Innis; the outside theorists did not take up Innis as a theorist.

## A4. Judgment: an edge harold_a_innis → dependency?

**Supported, as `connection` / relationship_kind `influence`, not `contribution`, and worded as
a Canadian reception.** The in-period texts show Canadian political economists (Levitt 1970;
Watkins 1970, 1973, 1977, 1982; Clement 1977; Drache 1982, 1983; Drache and Kroker 1983) reading
Innis's staple and centre–margin analysis as an indigenous theory of dependency, and explicitly
laying it beside Frank (Clement 1977, p. 17; Watkins 1970 via Clement), Amin (Drache 1982,
p. 49) and Wallerstein's vocabulary of the semi-periphery (Drache 1982, p. 36; 1983, p. 25).
Panitch (1981, p. 7) states the convergence in a critic's voice: the two "converged in their
concern with unequal exchange between societies". The evidence does **not** support:

- that Innis contributed to dependency theory as such, or that Frank, Wallerstein or Amin took
  him up. Wallerstein uses Innis only for fisheries facts; Panitch reports that Frank set Canada
  apart from Latin America; Helleiner (2014) notes that Innis was, to his knowledge, never
  footnoted in the development literature.
- that Watkins 1963 was a dependency text. It is a growth model; Rotstein (2014) reads it as
  Canada's counter-case to Prebisch. Watkins's dependency turn is 1970–77 (Clement 1977 quoting
  Watkins 1970; McNally 1981 on Watkins 1973 and 1977). Watkins 1963 and 1977 bodies were not
  read; only the 1963 first page and 1977 through quotation.
- a Canadian consensus. Panitch and McNally (1981), Niosi (1983), Laxer (1985, 1989, through
  McNally 1990), Kellogg (1989) and historians of business (Tulchinsky 1978 on Naylor; Bliss
  through quotation) contested it.

**Suggested wording** (relationship field, ≤ ~20 words):
"Canadian political economists read the staple studies as an indigenous dependency theory
(Levitt 1970, Clement 1977, Drache 1982); Marxist critics disputed the fusion (1981)."

**map_label**: "Staples read as dependency, 1970–83".

(Levitt inserts Innis's Fur Trade passage into dependency theory; "prevailing orthodoxy");

**Pathway use**: putting harold_a_innis first in `global_scale_explanation` (before `modern`) is

the pathway's dependency stop descends from Innis. Alternative placement: between `modern` and
`dependency`, with the stop question "Was the staple approach a theory of growth (Mackintosh,
Watkins 1963) or of dependency (Innis as read by Drache)?" — Watkins 1982 p. 16 and Drache 1983
p. 27 give the two-versions contrast verbatim.

# PART B. A new entry "Extractivism"

## B1. Pre-2000 roots

1. **Innis's staples as the antecedent, as later Canadian and world-systems writers read it.**
   - Stephen G. Bunker, "Matter, Space, Energy, and Political Economy: The Amazon in the
     World-System," *Journal of World-Systems Research* 9:2 (2003): 219–258 [read pp. 223–227,
     238–240; open PDF, two printed pages per scan page, read column by column]:
     "descriptive economic historians such as Richard Albion (1926) or Harold Innis (1956)
     analyzed specific local extractive economies by (1) the material attributes of topography,
     geology, hydrology, and climate that produced the resource extracted, and (2) the
     topographic and hydrological characteristics of the space between the locus of extraction
     and the locus of consumption" (p. 223); "For Innis, space as a differentiated condition of
     natural production ... determined the material composition and the location of different
     extractive economies" (p. 224); "Innis's theorization of matter and space did not preempt,
     but rather facilitated and extended, his analysis of social and economic processes"
     (p. 226); recent studies "cannot achieve the dynamic dialectic that characterized Innis's or
     Albion's work" (p. 226); "In Underdeveloping the Amazon (Bunker, 1985), I elaborate such a
     model as a sequentially cumulative series of socially organized extractive cycles" (p. 227);
     "Reading Harvey and Innis together helped me to extend and refine my notions of transport
     systems as a capital-intensive, debt-creating, state-forming instruments" (p. 240).
     Caution: this is Bunker in 2003. Whether *Underdeveloping the Amazon* (1985) itself cites
     Innis is unchecked (book access-restricted). His article "Staples, Links, and Poles in the
     Construction of Regional Development Theories," *Sociological Forum* 4:4 (1989): 589–610,
     shows by its title that he engaged staples theory before 2000 [metadata only].
   - Éric Pineault, "The Panacea: Panax Quinquefolius and the Mirage of the Extractive Economy,"
     in J. Stanford, ed., *The Staple Theory @ 50* (CCPA, 2014; 2020 PDF) [read pp. 84–89]: "The
     great Harold Innis is credited with the most comprehensive study of the political economics
     of the 'primarization' of economies and societies" (p. 85); the extraction alliance's view of
     the world becomes "imbued with an extractivist ideology and culture" (p. 87); "This is the
     ecological paradox of extractivisim: the scarcer a resource becomes, the more its cost rises,
     the more profitable it is to exploit the least productive deposits" (p. 87). This is the
     most explicit translation of Innis into extractivism vocabulary found.
   - Graeme Wynn, "Forests, Frontiers, and Extractivism," *Environmental History* 28:4 (2023):
     640–655 [read; local copy]: "Central as 'staples theory' has been to understanding Canada's
     past, it has found less purchase among students of American history" (p. 643); "Perhaps then
     it is (past) time to rethink, even shelve, the old verities of frontier and staples theses"
     (p. 650); extractivism "emerged, in its current articulation, from the circumstances of, and
     theorizing about, uneven (geographical) development in Latin America ... It has analytical
     links with Wallerstein's world systems theory, and through Joan Martinez Allier's
     'environmentalism of the poor,' with the struggle for survival and equally pressing questions
     of environmental justice"; it is "but the latest episode in a centuries-long saga ... the
     essential characteristics of which were prefigured in the staples approach to economic
     history" (pp. 650–651).
   - Mel Watkins, "Bitumen as a Staple," rejoinder in *The Staple Theory @ 50* (2014) [read
     pp. 127–132]: the resource economy's "increasing capacity for environmental degradation —
     has actually deepened" (p. 128); "Resource extraction rapidly draws down resources created
     over billions of years" (p. 131); the staple trap as "the Canadian variety of path
     dependency" (p. 130); praise for those "notably aboriginal peoples, who are prepared to stop
     them [pipelines] by non-violent means" (p. 131).
   - Negative evidence: the Latin American and global genealogies do **not** cite Innis. Gudynas
     (2018) and Acosta (2013) have no Innis; Chagnon et al. (2022), a 33-page conceptual history,
     names Bunker (1988 ed.), Acosta, Galeano, Wallerstein and Moore but not Innis (grep of full
     text). The Innis → extractivism line is a Canadian and world-systems (Bunker) reading.

2. **Bunker (1984, 1985): extraction, unequal exchange, underdevelopment.**
   - "Modes of Extraction, Unequal Exchange, and the Progressive Underdevelopment of an Extreme
     Periphery: The Brazilian Amazon, 1600–1980," *AJS* 89:5 (1984): 1017–1064 [abstract only]:
     "The demographic, ecological, and infrastructural effects of extractive economies differ
     significantly from those of productive economies. Analysis of underdevelopment in extractive
     export economies requires time-lagged models of the cumulative effects of the sequence of
     local modes of extraction organized in response to world-system demands ... derived from a
     critical synthesis of various theories of development and underdevelopment."
   - *Underdeveloping the Amazon* (Urbana, 1985; Chicago pbk 1988) [NOT read: Internet Archive
     copy access-restricted]. Publisher description via HAHR review record [abstract-level]: the
     book "shows how different extractive economies have periodically enriched various dominant
     classes but progressively impoverished the entire region by disrupting both the Amazon
     Basin's ecology and human communities", proposing "a new model based on the use and depletion
     of energy values in natural resources".
   - Bunker's own retrospect (2003, read): he "abstracted these processes upward into a
     thermodynamic model of unbalanced energy flows to account for the systematic
     underdevelopment and instability of extractive economies" (p. 238); critics from right and
     left — Katzman's 1987 review deployed "neo-classical resource economists' claims that
     vent-for-surplus of natural resources was a regular and reliable means for the economic
     development of 'newly settled' frontiers", while Volk (1986) held that "Marx's labor theory
     of value provided all the mechanisms required" (p. 238); these "drove me to search for the
     reasons that some extractive peripheries, most notably the United States, but also Sweden,
     Denmark, parts of Germany, Canada, and Australia, had subsequently industrialized" (p. 239);
     and he came to think "Wallerstein's tripartite categorization of the world system ...
     impeded an adequate specification of particular or local economies" (p. 239).
   - Bunker is a sociologist working inside world-systems analysis; he is the clearest pre-2000
     bridge from `dependency` (unequal exchange, underdevelopment) to an environmental account of
     extraction. Later historical uptake: Jason W. Moore, "'Amsterdam is Standing on Norway' Part
     I," *Journal of Agrarian Change* 10:1 (2010): 33–68 [read p. 34]: "To identify natural
     resource flows implicates a certain logic of 'ecological' imperialism (Foster and Clark 2004;
     Bunker and Ciccantell 2005)."

3. **Dependency and unequal exchange as the frame the term grew in.** Acosta (2013, p. 63):
   "Dependency on the metropolitan centres via the extraction and export of raw materials has
   remained practically unaltered to this day." Svampa (2015, p. 47): the commodities consensus
   produces "new and dangerous forms of dependency and domination"; (p. 50) it relegates Latin
   America "to the role of nature-exporter, turning a blind eye to ... the new dependency
   frameworks and the consolidation of the export enclaves". Gudynas (2018, p. 71):
   "extractivisms generate a spill to deepen an economically and ecologically asymmetric and
   unequal trade". Chagnon et al. (2022, p. 766): neo-extractivism "continues to reinforce old
   dependency paths". Note the structural difference: dependency theory asked why industry did not
   develop; extractivism adds the physical drain (tonnes, "ecological backpack", Gudynas p. 62)
   and territorial conflict.

## B2. Post-2000 development (Latin American extractivismo / neo-extractivismo)

- **Eduardo Gudynas, "Extractivisms: Tendencies and Consequences," in R. Munck and R. Delgado
  Wise, eds., *Reframing Latin American Development* (Routledge, 2018): 61–76** [read in full;
  local copy]. "The term extractivism can be traced back at least as far as the 1970s as a means
  of describing developments in the mining and oil export sectors"; a conceptual discussion "began
  in the Andean countries in 2008" and "the first definition was discussed in Ecuador in 2009"
  (p. 61). Definition: "the appropriation of natural resources in large volumes and/or high
  intensity, where half or more are exported as raw materials, without industrial processing or
  with limited processing" (p. 62). Against overstretch: some use it "to describe all development
  strategies, or to refer to financial extractivisms. This is not useful since in becoming
  increasingly diffuse the term loses its descriptive and analytical attributes"; "In
  extractivism nothing is produced but everything is extracted" (p. 63). "The term
  neo-extractivism ... in its original sense was an abbreviation of the new extractivisms of
  progressivism" (p. 64). "Extrahección ... from the Latin word extraher, which means 'to pluck
  with violence'" (p. 69). Postextractivist transitions toward "Buen Vivir" (p. 75).
- **Alberto Acosta, "Extractivism and Neoextractivism: Two Sides of the Same Curse," in M. Lang
  and D. Mokrani, eds., *Beyond Development* (TNI / Rosa Luxemburg Foundation, 2013): 61–86**
  [read pp. 61–63, 73; open PDF]. "Extractivism is a mode of accumulation that started to be
  established on a massive scale 500 years ago"; working definition: "those activities which
  remove large quantities of natural resources that are not processed (or processed only to a
  limited degree), especially for export" (p. 62). "In practice, extractivism has been a mechanism
  of colonial and neocolonial plunder and appropriation" (p. 63). "neoextractivism maintains and
  reproduces key elements of the extractivism that dates back to colonial times" (p. 73).
- **Maristella Svampa, "The 'Commodities Consensus' and Valuation Languages in Latin America,"
  *Alternautas* 2:1 (2015): 45–59** (trans. of *Nueva Sociedad* 244, 2013) [read pp. 45–51, 55].
  "Over the last decade Latin America has shifted from the Washington Consensus, with its focus on
  finance, to the commodities consensus, based on the large-scale export of primary products"
  (p. 45); dependency language at pp. 47, 50 (above); the "eco-territorial turn" of
  socio-environmental struggles (p. 55). Svampa, *Neo-extractivism in Latin America* (Cambridge
  Elements, 2019) [metadata only].
- **Conceptual history (secondary, read)**: Chagnon et al., "From Extractivism to Global
  Extractivism: The Evolution of an Organizing Concept," *Journal of Peasant Studies* 49:4 (2022):
  760–792 [read pp. 765–766; Helda repository copy]: the predominant understanding "originated in
  the late twentieth century, born from Latin American conceptualizations of extractivismo"; "the
  earliest notions of the term extractivism in the Arctic region's academic discourses appeared
  only in the 2010s"; "Although the academic concept of extractivism is rather new, the dynamics
  ... can be traced to colonial era resource and labor exploitation" (p. 765).

## B3. Historians' uptake, Indigenous and Canadian work

- **Allan Greer, "Settler Colonialism and Beyond," JCHA n.s. 30:1 (2019): 61–86** [read pp. 61,
  74–77; Érudit] (already source screq_greer_2019_jcha; Greer is on `settler_colonialism` as
  critic). "In our own time, the most prominent form of colonialism affecting Indigenous peoples
  and their lands is surely the intrusion of companies exploiting the environment in pursuit of
  natural resources" (p. 74); "The term 'extractivism,' first devised in the Latin American
  context to designate the process by which vulnerable countries were impoverished through
  resource-based economic development, has been applied more recently to the state-abetted
  corporate assault on Indigenous territories in Canada" (p. 74; n. 42 cites Acosta 2013 and
  Veltmeyer and Petras 2014); "There was, of course, an extractivist dimension to the fur trade as
  well" but early fisheries, fur and logging were "one aspect among many of imperial/commercial or
  settler-colonial forms"; recent extractivism is "sufficiently powerful and all-encompassing as
  to constitute a specific variety of colonialism" (p. 75); "Is extractivism simply a renewed
  version of settler colonialism, as some have argued?" (p. 76; n. 51 cites Preston 2013,
  Huseman and Short 2012, Short 2016); "Extractivist enterprises, by contrast, have little
  interest in permanent territorial control" (p. 76); "extractivism can operate through
  infiltration and cooptation"; "the dynamics of colonialism have changed over time as
  extractivism has come to the fore in place of literal colonization by settlers" (p. 77). Other
  leads in his notes: Gordon and Webber, *Blood of Extraction: Canadian Imperialism in Latin
  America* (2016); McNeill and Vrtis, eds., *Mining North America: An Environmental History since
  1522* (2017) [metadata only].
- **Arn Keeling and John Sandlos, "Introduction," in Keeling and Sandlos, eds., *Mining and
  Communities in Northern Canada: History, Politics, and Memory* (Calgary, 2015)** [read pp. 4–7,
  20–21; open access]: "Harold Innis's foundational work on the rise and fall of hinterland
  staples economies provided a key pathway into our study" (p. 4); "Too often the
  staples-inflected literature has focused only on the rise and fall of largely non-Native mining
  towns, stories that ignored First Nations and Inuit communities" (p. 5); "We ultimately adopted
  historical political ecology as a major theoretical frame" (p. 6), connecting mining to "the
  settler colonial dispossession of indigenous people" (p. 7); mining was "a colonial incursion
  into Native territory" (p. 21). They do not use the word "extractivism" in the introduction:
  Canadian environmental historians reached the same ground through staples plus political
  ecology.
- **Glen Coulthard, *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014), Conclusion, "Lessons from Idle No More"**
  [read; local e-book, no page numbers]: "in the 1970s the Dene Nation sought to curtail the
  negative environmental and cultural impacts of capitalist extractivism by proposing to establish
  an economy that would apply traditional concepts of Dene governance". Greer (n. 53) cites
  Coulthard pp. 76–77 for the primitive-accumulation argument [Greer's page reference].
- Wynn (2023, above) is the environmental historians' explicit uptake.

## B4. Critics

- **Álvaro García Linera, *Geopolitics of the Amazon* (2012; English trans. R. Fidler,
  Climate & Capitalism, 2013)** [read pp. 30–33]: "If we conceptualize 'extractivism' as the
  activity that simply extracts raw materials ..., then all societies in the world, capitalist or
  non-capitalist, are also to a greater or lesser degree extractivist" (p. 31); "the central
  debate for the revolutionary transformation of society is not whether or not we are
  extractivist, but to what degree we are going beyond capitalism as a mode of production"
  (p. 32); "The critics of extractivism confuse technical system with mode of production" (p. 33).
- **Gudynas's internal critique of overstretch** (2018, p. 63, above).
- **Neoclassical and Marxist critics of Bunker**, as Bunker reports them (2003, p. 238): Katzman
  (1987, vent-for-surplus) and Volk (1986, labour theory of value suffices).
- **Against staples as antecedent**: Forkey (1998) and Wynn (2004), already in the atlas on
  edge_913; Keeling and Sandlos (2015, p. 5) on the staples literature's blindness to Native
  communities; Wynn (2023, p. 650): "rethink, even shelve".
- **Settler-colonial scholars who fold extractivism into settler colonialism** (Preston 2013;
  Huseman and Short 2012, as Greer reports them, p. 76) are the counter-position to Greer.

## B5. Proposal (evidence only, not a graph edit)

- **id**: `extractivism`. **Label**: "Extractivism". **entry_type**: "Research field"
  (a concept-led field, closer to `settler_colonialism` than to a school).
- **date_label**: "Roots: staple studies 1930–40, Bunker 1984–85 · Latin American (neo-)
  extractivism 2009–15 · historians' uptake 2015–23 · selected later readings". (Gudynas dates
  the term to the 1970s and the conceptual debate to 2008–09.)
- **Description sketch (~120 words)**: Inquiry into economies and colonial relations organised
  around removing large volumes of raw materials for export with little processing. The term
  came from Latin American critiques of mining, oil and agro-export after 2008: Gudynas, Acosta
  and Svampa described an extractivism that went back to colonial times, and a neo-extractivism
  under progressive governments that kept it. It reworks dependency's unequal exchange as a
  physical and territorial drain. Earlier roots include Innis's staple studies, read as studies
  of extractive economies by Bunker and by later Canadian scholars, and Bunker's Amazon (1985).
  Historians have used it to name a mode of colonialism distinct from settler colonialism
  (Greer 2019) and as a frame for environmental history (Wynn 2023). Critics question its
  breadth (García Linera; Gudynas) and its fit with staples history.
- **Scope note points**: post-2000 consolidation, so this is a selected-later-readings entry like
  `settler_colonialism`; the Innis link is a Canadian/world-systems reading, not the Latin
  American genealogy; Brazilian *extrativismo* (forest gathering) is a different usage and should
  be checked before being mentioned (not verified here).
