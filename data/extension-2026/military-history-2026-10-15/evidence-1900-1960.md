# Evidence: military history as a practice, 1900 to about 1960, and how military historians describe and criticise it

Research phase only. No graph edits and no git changes. Compiled 2026-10-15 against atlas revision 1.140 (`historiography-1920-2000.json`). The companion file `evidence-ancient-roots.md` in this folder covers the ancient and classical roots (Thucydides to Kromayer, Adcock, Tarn and Hanson). Those are not repeated here.

Question (user, 2026-10-15): "review military history again; there must be military history going back to 1900; Greeks and Romans wrote military history". This file covers 1900 to about 1960 in six areas: official and staff history; campaign and army history as scholarship; theorist-historians; war, state and society; institutions; and the field's own later account of the period.

**How sources are labelled.** Every source carries a reading label:
- **Read in full**, **read in part** (with the pages or sections given), **opening only** (an abstract or first paragraph), **via WebFetch extraction**, **mediated** (with the reporting source named), or **metadata only**.
- Quotations come from the text that was read, with page numbers where the text has folios.
- **OCR corrections** are marked in square brackets.
- **WebFetch quotations:** the WebFetch tool returns short, model-extracted quotations, and these are labelled as such.

## 1. Official and staff histories

### 1a. The British official history in its own words: J. E. Edmonds, *Military Operations, France and Belgium, 1914*, vol. I (Macmillan, 1922), Preface, pp. v–ix

**Read:** read in full (archive.org `militaryoperatio01edmouoft`, University of Toronto copy of the 1922 edition, djvu text). The preface is signed "J. E. E. April 1922."

**Stated purpose and evidence base (p. v):**
- "THIS history has been compiled with the purpose of providing within reasonable compass an authoritative account, suitable for general readers and for students at military schools, of the operations of the British Army in the Western theatre of war in 1914-1918. It is based on the British official records."
- The scale is chosen partly for "the lessons to be derived from the study of the work of these units in open warfare". This is the staff-college use, stated outright.
- "the difficulty has been not in obtaining information, but in compressing and cutting down what was available" (p. v).

**On the evidence (p. vi):**
- "written orders and messages are absolutely reliable evidence of the matters with which they deal, war diaries and reports of actions, written up immediately after events, are liable to contain mistakes."
- "even officers well known to be specially interested in military history have confessed that during the war the idea of collecting or keeping material for its future historian never occurred to them."

**Institution (pp. vi–vii):**
- **Name and duties:** the work is that of the "Historical Section, Committee of Imperial Defence", Military Branch. The branch was also "charged with the sorting and arrangement of the records dealing with operations overseas". "The Branch did not obtain a permanent home until October 1919."
- **Predecessor:** Edmonds thanks "Mr. C. T. Atkinson, his predecessor in charge of the Branch" (p. ix).
- **Dating:** the Section and its records work predate the first volume (1922). "From 1922" dates the first published volume, not the Section.

**International framing:**
- **French:** "The French General Staff has not yet issued any history" (p. vii, April 1922).
- **German:** "General Freiherr Mertz von Quirnheim, the Director of the German Reichsarchiv, Berlin, which has custody of the war records, has been good enough to furnish material" (p. viii). [OCR "Quirheim" corrected.]

**The feature later critics fasten on (pp. viii–ix):**
- "The typescript or proof sheets have been read by a number of commanders and staff and regimental officers who took part in the events narrated, and the compiler has been greatly assisted by their advice and criticism".
- This is Edmonds's own description. The judgement that this review compromised the history belongs to later critics (see 1f), and that literature is unread here.

### 1b. Criticism in the period: B. H. Liddell Hart on official and "camouflaged" history

**(i) *The Real War, 1914–1918* (London, 1930), Preface, pp. 9–10**

**Read:** read in full (archive.org `in.ernet.dli.2015.57906`, DLI scan, djvu text; folios 9 and 10 as printed).
- "This book may at least claim one merit, and one contrast to most war ‘histories’. ... my pursuit of the truth has not been interrupted by recourse to the pot of hypocritical varnish that is miscalled ‘good taste’." [OCR "Thy" corrected to "my".]
- "the time has come when a ‘real’ history of the war is possible. Governments have opened their archives, statesmen and generals their hearts with an unparalleled philanthropy."
- "The more that any writer of history has himself been at hand when history is being made, or in contact with the makers, the more does he come to see that a history based solely on formal documents is essentially superficial. Too often, also, it is the unwitting handmaiden of ‘mythology’."
- **His own limitation, stated in the same preface:** "Some may say that the war depicted here is not ‘the real war’ — that this is to be discovered in the torn bodies and minds of individuals. ... it is a secondary aspect." He adds that decisive impressions "were received and made in the cabinets and in the military headquarters, not in the ranks of the infantry nor in the solitude of stricken homes." This is a command-centred definition of the war's history, worth placing against Keegan's later combat history. No text read links the two.

**(ii) *Why Don’t We Learn from History?* (P.E.N. Books, London, 1944)**

**Read:** read in part: the foreword, pp. 8–9, 11–12 and roughly 15–16 (folio partly illegible in OCR). Archive.org `in.ernet.dli.2015.526495`.
- **On documents (pp. 8–9):** "Nothing can deceive like a document." [OCR "dccei\e" corrected.] "After twenty years’ experience of such work, pure documentary history seems to me akin to mythology. To those academic historians who still repose faith on it, I have often told a short story with a moral". The story is of a French general who dictated orders for a line already lost: "C’est pour l’histoire". That general "for a great part of the war ... had held a high staff position where the archives on which much official history would later depend had been under his control."
- **On gaps in the archives (p. 9):** "Many are the gaps to be found in official archives, token of documents destroyed later to conceal what might impair a commander's reputation. More difficult to detect are the forgeries with which some of them have been replaced."
- **The German "camouflage" episode (pp. 11–12):**
  - A "distinguished German general" (the year is garbled in the OCR) wrote an article, "Why Can’t we Camouflage?".
  - The general regretted that frankness "should have spread to the official military history compiled by the Reichsarchiv" [OCR "ReichsdrehiY"]. He objected to its mention of panic in East Prussia and of mistakes by the Higher Command.
  - The general is unnamed and the article is unidentified. This is Liddell Hart's report only.
- **Official history (p. 12):** "History that bears the qualification ‘official’ carries with it a natural reservation; and the additional prefix ‘military’ is apt to imply a double reservation. ... This camouflaged history not only conceals faults and deficiencies that could otherwise be remedied, but engenders false confidence ... It is the dry rot of armies."
- **Soldier-historians (pp. ~15–16):** "Deep is the gulf between works of history as written and the truth of history, and perhaps never more so than in books dealing with military history. If one reason is that these are usually written by soldiers untrained as historians, and another that there is frequently some personal link, whether of acquaintance or tradition, between author and subject, a deeper reason lies in a habit of mind." He reports participants suggesting episodes "might ‘best be glossed over’ in war histories".

**A later reader confirms the theme:** Speller (2011, p. 9; see 6e) cites the 1971 edition of this book: "Liddell Hart warns against such ‘camouflaged history’".

### 1c. US Army: *United States Army in World War II* (the "Green Books")

**Source:** Maurice Matloff and Edwin M. Snell, *Strategic Planning for Coalition Warfare, 1941–1942* (Office of the Chief of Military History, 1953). Foreword by Maj. Gen. Orlando Ward, 5 June 1952 (p. vii); Preface (pp. ix–x).

**Read:** read in full (HyperWar transcription, `ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-WD-Strategic1/`). history.army.mil returned 403.

**The authors, as the Chief of Military History describes them (p. vii):**
- "Mr. Snell was formerly an instructor in English at Harvard University and Mr. Matloff an instructor in History at Brooklyn College. Mr. Snell served in the Army and Mr. Matloff in the Army Air Forces during World War II. Both joined the Operations Division historical project of the War Department General Staff in 1946."

**Institutional framing (p. vii):**
- "It may seem to the reader that controversy and differences of opinion are stressed and that agreement and co-operative endeavor are slighted. ... The reader must bear in mind that the differences related herein are those among comrades in arms who in the end always made the adjustments required of the members of a team engaged in a common enterprise."
- This is the sponsoring institution softening the history's account of conflict. It is one documented instance, not a characterisation of the whole series.

**Audience and production (pp. ix–x):**
- **Audience:** "Anyone that writes on the subject of strategic planning itself is venturing into territory generally familiar only to a few professional officers, and to them mainly through oral tradition and their own experience." The intended readers are "staff officers, civil officials, diplomatic historians, and political scientists".
- **Collective production:** the project began in 1946 "by a group of associated historians, organized by Dr. Ray S. Cline". "Dr. Kent Roberts Greenfield, Chief Historian of the Office and the first and most attentive critic of this volume". Stetson Conn and Louis Morton are also thanked.
- **Records labour:** "Nearly every page in the volume bears some mark of Mrs. Bailey’s wide knowledge and exact understanding of the records kept by the War Department" (Mrs. Helen McShane Bailey). Mrs. Evelyn Cooper assembled the statistics.
- **Fit with the atlas:** this matches the atlas's existing principle that library and records work are part of the field's production (the `military` scope note).

**Not read:**
- Kent Roberts Greenfield, *The Historian and the Army* (1954); no open copy found.
- Stetson Conn, *Historical Work in the United States Army, 1862–1954* (CMH, 1980); history.army.mil is blocked.
- Any assessment of the series' independence.
- A search-result summary of a Strategypage review said official histories "tend to have a poor reputation among historians" but these volumes "were often written by professional historians". That is not usable as evidence.

**US Army Historical Section (SMH chronology, 1e):** "1914 The Historical Section of the United States Army is created to assist in the World War". It was "recreated" in 1918 "to prepare monographs, historical studies, and the copying of historical documents from other nations".

### 1d. US Navy: Samuel Eliot Morison, *History of United States Naval Operations in World War II*, vol. II, *Operations in North African Waters* (Little, Brown, 1947), Foreword (p. ix) and Introduction (pp. xi–xiii)

**Read:** read in full (archive.org `in.ernet.dli.2015.80804`, DLI scan). Vol. I's front matter is in an archive.org "opensource" upload of uncertain rights status and was not used. The Introduction is presumably the series introduction, but that is unverified.

**Foreword by the Secretary of the Navy (p. ix):**
- "EARLY IN 1942, Samuel Eliot Morison, Jonathan Trumbull Professor of American History in Harvard University, suggested to President Roosevelt the desirability of preparing for the American people a full, accurate, and early record of the activities of the Navy in World War II".
- "All naval activities, afloat and ashore, were directed to make available to Captain Morison such records as he might desire to consult."
- "This work, however, is in no sense an official history. The form, style and character of the narrative are the author’s own. The opinions expressed and the conclusions reached are those of Dr. Morison, and of him alone. He has been subject to no restrictions other than those imposed by the necessity of safeguarding information which might endanger national security."

**Morison's method (Introduction, pp. xi–xiii):**
- **Participant research:** "Believing that too many histories are written from the outside looking in, I or one of my assistants have visited every theater of naval warfare since 1941, and taken part in as many operations as possible."
- **Limits:** "No history written during or shortly after the event it describes can pretend to be completely objective or even reasonably definitive."
- **Participants and judgement:** "Historians with military experience will always be more tolerant of failure or lack of success than those without it". He states this as a virtue; later critics of official history would read it as the problem.
- **Documents:** "As rigorous a study of the written documents was made as if this were a war of the last century. Official reports are not impeccable". "a seaman’s eye has been applied to the technique of a professional historian, but the seaman has also learned to discount the evidence of his eye."
- **Scope:** "The story, in the main, is one of fighting; that is what the Navy is for". The administrative history went to Robert G. Albion.

**What this documents:** Morison's history was commissioned and given full records access while being declared "in no sense an official history". This semi-official form is well documented by the front matter itself. The AHA directory (6g) also shows Morison supervising Harvard PhDs, including "The Insular Campaign of 1759: Martinique and Guadeloupe" (1948).

### 1f. Official history after 1945 as an entry point for academic historians (Britain)

**Source:** David Morgan-Owen and Michael Finch, "The Unrepentant Historian: Sir Michael Howard and the Birth of War Studies", *British Journal for Military History* 8:2 (2022): 55–76 (CC BY-NC-ND).

**Read:** read in part: pp. 55–67 read; pp. 68–76 keyword-located only.

**Webster and Frankland (p. 65):**
- "in 1950 he was persuaded by J R M Butler, chief historian of the British official military history of the Second World War, to take on the official history of the Anglo-American Strategic Air Offensive in conjunction with a much younger co-author, Noble Frankland".
- "his experience with the official histories convinced him of the relevance and importance of war as a field of scholarship".

**Hancock and Gowing (pp. 66–67):**
- W. K. Hancock headed "the civil series of the official history of the war" from 1941. That series produced thirty volumes, "including British War Economy (1949) which he co-authored with Margaret Gowing."
- Hancock's lesson, quoted from *Country and Calling* (1954), pp. 196–97: "the armed forces nowadays were no more than the cutting edge of the nation at war and their history had no higher importance than that of munition making and agriculture, of shipping, land transport, mining and all the other civilian activities."

**Their judgement (p. 68):** "their roles in government and the official history programmes catalysed new approaches to the subject based upon extensive engagement with the conduct of the Second World War."

**Howard as an official historian.** Source: Hew Strachan, "Michael Howard and the dimensions of military history", Liddell Hart Centre annual lecture, 3 December 2002 (KCL PDF, 14 pp.). Read in full.
- "In 1959 Sir James Butler invited Michael Howard to take on the writing of volume 4 of the Grand Strategy in the official history of the Second World War" (p. 2).
- **Access constraints (p. 4):** "he could reckon on the records remaining closed to 1992, but in 1966 the Public Records Act reduced the period of closure from 50 years to 30". "he made no reference in that book to signals intelligence. Ultra was not in the public domain in 1972." His official history of deception (completed 1980) was postponed "until 1990".
- This adds to the existing `howard_strachan_memoir` note and is consistent with it.

**Later criticism of British official history (unread; do not use as read):**
- **Andrew Green, *Writing the Great War: Sir James Edmonds and the Official Histories, 1915–1948* (Frank Cass, 2003):** search-result summaries of a *JMH* (2004) review and a forum post say Green places the history "in the tradition of general staff histories" and argues against the "whitewash" view. The MUSE review page was blocked by a bot check. **Metadata only.**
- **David French, "‘Official but not History’? Sir James Edmonds and the Official History of the Great War", *RUSI Journal* (1986):** not located or read.
- **Jay Luvaas, "The First British Official Historians", *Military Affairs* 26:2 (1962): 49ff (doi:10.2307/1984366):** metadata only (Crossref).

## 2. Campaign and army history as scholarship

### 2a. Charles Oman

**A *History of the Art of War: The Middle Ages from the Fourth to the Fourteenth Century* (Methuen, 1898), Preface, pp. v–vii**
- **Read:** read in full (archive.org `historyofartofw00oman`, front portion only).
- **The plan:** a series of four volumes, "a general sketch of the history of the art of war from Greek and Roman times down to the beginning of the nineteenth century".
- "These volumes are concerned with the history of the art of war, and do not purport to give the complete military annals of the civilised world. Each section deals with the characteristic tactics, strategy, and military organisation of a period, and illustrates them by detailed accounts of typical campaigns and battles."
- **Sources:** he cites the Rolls Series, Bouquet, Pertz and Muratori. Advice came from York Powell, H. B. George, T. A. Archer and F. Haverfield. "When possible, I walked over important battlefields, e.g. Creçy, Bouvines, Bannockburn, Evesham, in order to supplement the information to be derived from maps by a personal acquaintance with the ground." (pp. vi–vii). Signed "Oxford, March 1, 1898."
- **Note:** the 1924 second edition (two volumes) appears in archive.org metadata only.

***A History of the Peninsular War*, vol. I (Oxford, 1902), Preface**
- **Read:** read in part, about the first two-thirds (Project Gutenberg #53264; no folios).
- "Napier’s splendid work has held the field for sixty years. Meanwhile an enormous bulk of valuable material has been accumulating in English, French, and Spanish, which has practically remained unutilized."
- He has been "working for some ten or fifteen years at the original sources".
- **The Vaughan papers:** the papers of Sir Charles Vaughan "passing into the hands of All Souls College" prompted the work.
- **Archives:** "I have worked at the Record Office on the British official papers of the first years of the war, especially noting all the passages which are omitted in the printed dispatches of Moore and other British generals. The suppressed paragraphs ... contain a good deal of useful matter".
- **Source criticism:** "The memoirs of the Peninsular veterans on both sides often require very cautious handling". He refutes Marbot from Murat's letter: "The aged Marbot’s memory had played him false". "Thiébault and Le Noble are bad offenders in this respect".
- **What this shows:** documentary source criticism of participant memoirs, two decades before the official histories. It sits alongside the Delbrück evidence already in the atlas, with no claim of influence.

**Assessment by another reader**
- **Read:** via WebFetch extraction. Peter Hicks, "Napoleonic Pages: Sir Charles Oman: A History of the Peninsular War" (napoleon.org, undated).
- Napier's history was "flawed in many parts, incomplete and often biassed". Oman's first volume "was clearly recognised as superior to Napier". The work "still stands as the starting point". Hicks cites Esdaile (2002) for later work. These are Hicks's judgements.

**People record:** none for Oman.

### 2b. J. W. Fortescue

***A History of the British Army*, vols. I–II (Macmillan, 1899), Preface, pp. v–ix**
- **Read:** read in full, as reprinted in the 2nd edition of 1910 (archive.org `in.ernet.dli.2015.210080`). One OCR-damaged footnote was skipped.
- **The civilian author:** "The civilian who attempts to write a military history is of necessity guilty of an act of presumption ... But England has waited long for a soldier to do the work" (p. v).
- **Political as well as military:** "I confess that I have been amazed when reading our innumerable political histories to see how unconcernedly Army, Navy, and the whole question of National Defence are left out of account. It is this, the political not less than the military aspect of the Army’s history that I have endeavoured ... to elucidate" (p. vii).
- **Regimental histories:** "such regimental histories as exist in print I have been careful to study, sometimes with advantage but not always with profound respect for their accuracy." [OCR "m print" corrected.] (pp. viii–ix)
- **Maps:** "Blocks of red and blue are pleasing indeed to the eye, but it is always a question whether their facility for misleading does not exceed their utility for guidance." (p. ix)

***Military History: Lectures Delivered at Trinity College, Cambridge* (Cambridge, 1914)**
- **Read:** read in part: the preface, the opening and closing of Lecture I ("Military History: Its Scope and Definition", pp. 1–45) and the opening of Lecture II (p. 46). Project Gutenberg #54417, with no folios, so page numbers are lecture-level only.
- **The first Trinity lectureship:** "When in the spring of the year 1913 my old College did me the honour to appoint me its first lecturer in Military History, I was obliged for the first time to ask myself seriously, What is military history?"
- **His definition:** "military history is the history of the strife of communities expressed through the conflict of organised bands of armed men". He adds "organised" "so as to indicate that, below a certain stage of civilisation, there can be no military history".
  - **A boundary to flag:** this excludes non-state and "uncivilised" warfare by definition, from a historian whose later lectures treat colonial and Indian campaigns (Lectures III–IV). Such boundaries are relevant to the atlas's geographic-scope criticisms.
- **On the field's critics:** "let no man persuade you that the subject is trivial or unimportant--that the study of war is the study of a relic of barbarism to be eschewed by the serious, the devout and the humane". "‘Ah,’ say the despisers of military history". Academic disdain is thus already answered in 1913–14.
- **On its value:** "knowledge of military history is essential to the right understanding not only of domestic and foreign politics, but of the whole story ... of the human race--which is mainly a story of fighting."
- **On the moral tradition:** "It will harm none of us to know well this story of duty done for duty's sake". This is the moral-exemplary register later called "nursery history" (6a).

**People record:** none for Fortescue.

### 2c. Douglas Southall Freeman

- **Read:** via WebFetch extraction only. Encyclopedia Virginia entry "Douglas Southall Freeman (1886–1953)"; the author's name was not captured. The books were not read.
- ***R. E. Lee* (1934–35):** "appeared in four volumes in 1934–1935"; it won "the 1935 Pulitzer Prize for Biography".
- **Method:** "the ‘fog of war’ technique", "providing readers only the limited information that Lee himself had at a given moment".
- ***Lee's Lieutenants* (1942–44):** "established him as the nation’s preeminent military historian" (the entry's wording).
- **Criticism (the entry's summary):** his books were criticised for failing to grapple with slavery and for "elevation of Lee to near-mythological status", and "have been criticized as helping to fuel the mythology of the Lost Cause". The entry also says "Freeman's books showed a preference for campaigns over social and political history".
- **Strength:** thin, a reference entry via a tool. For a roster row, read a scholarly review (Gary Gallagher or Thomas Connelly) or the AHR review of 1935.

**People record:** none for Freeman.

### 2d. Regimental history, in Howard's account

- **Read:** mediated by Strachan 2002 (p. 3) and Kitchen and Mitchell 2022 (pp. 14–15). See 6a.
- **Howard's own regimental history:** Howard wrote one himself: John Sparrow and Michael Howard, *The Coldstream Guards 1920–1946* (1951). Source: Strachan 2002, n. 9; Kitchen and Mitchell p. 5.

## 3. Theorist-historians between the wars

### 3a. J. F. C. Fuller, *The Foundations of the Science of War* (Hutchinson, 1926), ch. I §1, pp. 19–22

**Read:** read in part, pp. 19–22 (archive.org `foundationsofsci00jfcf`, Marine Corps library copy).
- "The history of war is a great romance, but as yet no true science of war has been written. ... in recent years the method of science has been applied to civil history, and to-day many historical works exist on the social, commercial, religious, and political evolution of nations." (p. 19)
- "Clausewitz, a military philosopher, never completed his great work, which is little more than a mass of notes, a cloud of flame and smoke" (p. 20).
- "if we are disallowed a science of war, we can have no true history of war, only a ‘terrible and impassioned drama.’ ... We do not want drama; we want truth. We require not merely a chronology of past events, but means of analysing their tendencies ... To deny a science of war and then to theorize on war as an art is pure military alchemy" (p. 21).
- **Note:** Fuller uses military history instrumentally, as a base for a science of war. This is the "lessons" use that Howard later criticised. His dismissal of Clausewitz bears on the `wartradition` Clausewitz-reception strand.

**People record:** none for Fuller.

### 3b. Liddell Hart as historian, critic and academic outsider

- **His own critique of official history:** see 1b.
- **Strachan 2002 (read in full), p. 2:**
  - "The dominant figure of course in the field of military history in 1953 was not Falls but the man whom he had beaten in his election to the Chichele chair ... Basil Liddell Hart."
  - "Like Cyril Falls, Liddell Hart was in many ways more a journalist than an academic, and neither of them had much sense of putting military history in context ... For Liddell Hart military history was a tool, a tool to communicate ideas about war that had present application and future implication more than a help to understand the past."
- **Election details (Morgan-Owen and Finch, p. 66 n. 46):** Hancock "was one of the electors to the Chichele Professorship of the History War at Oxford University in 1946, choosing Cyril Falls for the chair which had been unfilled since the retirement of Ernest Swinton in 1939, in preference to Liddell Hart". They cite Strachan, "The Study of War at Oxford 1909–2009" (2014), pp. 213–214, which is unread.
- **Howard's later critique (Strachan 2002, p. 5, quoting Howard's 1974 Neale Lecture, *The Causes of Wars*, p. 172):** Liddell Hart's *British Way in Warfare* was "A piece of brilliant political pamphleteering, sharply argued, selectively illustrated and [more] concerned to influence British public opinion and government policy than to illuminate the complexities of the past in any serious or scholarly way." Strachan adds: "The idea of the British way in warfare is not even Liddell Hart's, it was Julian Corbett's".
- **On Clausewitz (Strachan 2002, p. 9):** "before the Michael Howard and Peter Paret translation, all, at least in the English speaking world, was darkness ... Here again was another breach with Liddell Hart, who had above all been responsible for disseminating that darkness."
- **Not read:** John J. Mearsheimer, *Liddell Hart and the Weight of History* (1988), and Brian Bond, *Liddell Hart: A Study of His Military Thought* (1977). Not located open.

**People record:** none for Liddell Hart.

## 4. War, state and society before the "new military history"

### 4a. Spenser Wilkinson, *The University and the Study of War: An Inaugural Lecture Delivered before the University of Oxford, November 27, 1909* (Clarendon Press, 1909)

**Read:** read in part, pp. 3–14 in full; the remainder (pp. 14–c. 30) keyword-scanned only. Archive.org `universitystudyo00wilkrich`.

**Institutional history (pp. 3, 8–9):**
- He is "your first Professor of Military History" (Chichele).
- **About 1875:** "English military literature of permanent value was represented almost entirely by the work of Sir Edward Hamley. Modern English naval literature had not then come into existence." (p. 8)
- **After the South African War:** "Oxford admitted the subject of Military History as a special subject for candidates for Honours in the School of Modern History, and in 1905 the University, in consequence of the gift of a private donor, established a Lecturership in that subject." (pp. 8–9)
- **The first lecturer:** the lecturer, Sir Foster Cunliffe, sought "to make military history teach war by the full and accurate ascertainment of the facts from the contemporary documents" (p. 9). All Souls proposed turning the lectureship into a chair.

**Policy over strategy (p. 10):** "the control and direction of a war ... is the function primarily of the statesman rather than of the soldier, and ... in regard to war the architectonic art is policy or politics, not strategy or generalship".

**Method (pp. 11–13):**
- "Military History is the effort to understand war, to get to know what war is and what it means." (p. 11)
- **On delayed disclosure (p. 12):** "Reasons of state, considerations of friendship and of regard for the reputation of distinguished men, tend to postpone as long as possible the disclosure of the exact truth". "full and trustworthy knowledge of any war is obtainable only as the result of that prolonged and patient research to which we give the name of History."
- **On the evidence (p. 13):** "the only basis either for a science or for an art of war is Military History, the record of the facts ascertained by methodical collection, sifting, and classification of the evidence."

**The war-and-state thesis, in 1909 (p. 13):** "As the State is, so will its army be; and the war of any age is the reflection of the political and social condition of the communities engaged in it. ... Thus the determining factor is always the nature and character of the States engaged in the war".

**Why it matters:** this is a direct, read, pre-1914 statement that military history studies war as a function of state and society. It undercuts any framing of "war and society" as a 1960s invention.

**People record:** none for Wilkinson.

### 4b. Frederick Maurice, King's College London, 1927

- **Read:** mediated by Morgan-Owen and Finch, pp. 61–62, quoting Maurice's inaugural lecture "On the Uses of the Study of War" (14 January 1927; LHCMA LH 3/6/19). Not read directly.
- King's hosted "a new chair in Military Studies to which Major-General Sir Frederick Maurice was appointed in 1927".
- **Maurice's lecture (quoted p. 62):**
  - "We have learned that statecraft, economics, the supply of raw material, science, and industry are factors which are of prime importance to the issue". "the morale of the peoples is of even greater importance".
  - "my faith in military history as the foundation of military study remains unshaken, and military history is but a special branch of the general study."
  - "too often sailor and soldier historians have lacked the technique of the civilian historian, and the civilian historian the technique of the sailor and soldier."
- **What happened next (p. 63):** the department "was briefly re-named War Studies in 1943, however it was closed in 1948".
- **Howard's later use (p. 63):** Howard "used Maurice’s inaugural lecture to structure the introduction" to his 1964 professorial lecture.

**People record:** none for Maurice.

### 4c. Quincy Wright, *A Study of War* (University of Chicago Press, 1942), Foreword, pp. vii–viii

- **Read:** read in full, from the 1965 second-edition scan (archive.org `dli.ernet.238573`), which reprints the original foreword.
- "This study of war was initiated at the University of Chicago in 1926. It has involved ... the preparation of over fifty studies by research assistants and members of the faculty."
- "Professor Louis Gottschalk and Dr. Hymen Ezra Cohen read and criticized the earlier publication of lectures."

## 6. The field's own account of the period, including critiques

### 6a. Michael Howard, "The Use and Abuse of Military History" (RUSI lecture, 1961; *RUSI Journal* Feb. 1962, pp. 4–10)

**Original:** not read. The Parameters reprint (vol. 11 no. 1, March 1981, pp. 9–14) is behind a Cloudflare wall. The lecture is reported here through three readers who quote it with page numbers.

**Kitchen and Mitchell.** James E. Kitchen and Stuart Mitchell, "Michael Howard and the Historian’s Craft: An Introduction to the Michael Howard Special Issue", *BJMH* 8:2 (September 2022): 2–26, doi:10.25602/GOLD.bjmh.v8i2.1632. Read in part: pp. 2–5 and 14–17.
- **Status of the lecture (p. 14):** "field-defining". It "is frequently seen as giving military history its professional identity, it is also much misread and misunderstood, reduced simply to Howard’s three ‘general rules of study’ ... width, depth, and context. Yet this call constituted less than a quarter of the article".
- **Audience:** "the lecture was not addressed to academics but to military professionals." It aimed to keep officers from "an overly instrumentalist and needlessly narrow approach, concerned merely with tactical and operational ‘lessons’".
- **Regimental history (pp. 14–15, citing Use and Abuse pp. 4–5):** the institutional historian sustains the image of a "‘flawlessly brave and efficient’ formation". Howard contrasted this with "the ‘function of the historian proper’", which engages "‘complicated and disagreeable realities’" and results in the "‘critical examination of the “myth”’".
- **Amateurs:** Howard "also issued some criticism of amateur historians lacking ‘academic training’, who read into the past anachronistic thoughts or motives; here he was thinking of military men dabbling in crude historical analogies."
- **On order and chaos (p. 15, citing p. 6):** military historians must "‘create order out of chaos’", and tidy accounts can be a "‘blasphemous travesty of the chaotic truth’".
- **On lessons (p. 16, citing p. 7):** "‘Clio is like the Delphic oracle: it is only in retrospect, and usually too late, that we can understand what she was trying to say’".
- **The Annales (p. 16):** the first rule's longue-durée perspective is linked to "the significant influence of the Annales school on him in his memoir" (Howard, *Captain Professor*, pp. 206–207; unread).
- **Discussion after the lecture (p. 16):** questions came "in all but one instance, from serving or retired officers"; "a number focused on his criticism of regimental histories".
- **Reply to Anthony Verrier (p. 17, citing p. 8):** "‘I write military history because I am interested in military history.’ ... ‘one cannot deal with the past at all unless one understands the part which military affairs played in it’."

**Strachan 2002 (read in full), pp. 2–3:**
- Strachan quotes the lecture on the young soldier and the "myth" that "can and often does sustain him, even when he knows, with half his mind, that it is untrue". He glosses: "This, the sustaining of the myth, is, he tells us, the function of regimental history, or ‘nursery history’ as he self deprecatingly calls it."
- **On 1953:** "Michael has told us that we need to study the subject ‘in width, in depth and in context.’ ... That now happens. It didn't happen, or at least not very much, in 1953."
- **On experience:** "Up until Michael Howard's appointment, almost nobody who had studied the history of war had not themselves experienced war."
- **On Delbrück (p. 7):** "surely the founding father of academic military history".
- **On the new military history (p. 8):** "very often the ‘new’ military history has seen to be the history of war with the fighting left out."

**Speller 2011.** Ian Speller, "The use and abuse of history by the military" (presentation at Liverpool Hope University, 24 February 2011; Maynooth eprint 3843). Read in part: pp. 1–5 and 7–10.
- **On the lecture (p. 1):** "justly regarded as a classic and is required reading".
- **Officers and lessons (p. 7, citing Howard 1962, p. 7):** "steer between the dangers of repeating the errors of the past because he is ignorant that they have been made, and of remaining bound by theories deduced from past history although changes in conditions have rendered these theories obsolete".
- **"Nursery education" (p. 8, citing Howard 1962, pp. 4–5).**
- **Width, depth and context:** "should be written in bold type across the top of every study guide" (p. 10).

**Howard's 1956 article.** "Military History as a University Study", *History* 41 (1956): 184–191, doi:10.1111/j.1468-229x.1956.tb02175.x (Crossref).
- **Read:** mediated by Morgan-Owen and Finch, p. 60, citing pp. 185–86 and 190.
- "If the word ‘military’ evokes narrow and technical associations, the fault lies with the type of military history which we have been accustomed to read". "military historians have not always been conscious of this wider conception of their task". The study of war demanded "far more than the limited resources and technique of the old-style military historian".
- **A 1955 letter to Liddell Hart (p. 59):** Howard urged studying "‘social and economic aspects of war in close connection with studies of military techniques: which the Germans have done for a hundred years (Jähns, Delbrück) and the Americans for twenty’".

**Morgan-Owen and Finch's correction (pp. 56–57).** They call the War Studies origin story "seductive in its simplicity. It casts Howard as an heroic figure, almost single-handedly ... delivering the study of war from the academic wilderness", and add that it is "a story that Howard himself consciously cultivated". Their article restores Laughton, Maurice, Webster and Hancock. This is direct evidence that the atlas's `war_studies_institutions` strand (Howard and Paret only) repeats that origin story.

**3. `chairs_journals_soldier_scholars` — "Chairs, journals and the soldier-scholar"**
- **Focus (draft):** "University teaching of military history began as an adjunct to officer education.
