## 2. Sources read

**N1. Dipesh Chakrabarty, "The Human Condition in the Anthropocene", The Tanner Lectures on Human Values, delivered at Yale University, 18–19 February 2015** (official Tanner Lectures lecture page and its text; https://tannerlectures.org/lectures/the-human-condition-in-the-anthropocene/). Two lectures: I "Climate Change as Epochal Consciousness"; II "Decentering the Human? Or, What Remains of Gaia". **Read in part, no page numbers:** lecture I opening, "Epochal Consciousness", "From the World to the Globe or the Planet" (opening), "Introducing a Pragmatic Distinction: Anthropos and Homo" and its notes; lecture II "Climate Justice and Homocentrism" (opening), the Big History passage, "Falling into Deep History" and the closing section, with the notes on Christian, McNeill and Smail. Other sections only searched.
- Aim: "I want to share some thoughts with you on the word common in the expression 'common but differentiated responsibilities.' ... Both the word common and what it may stand for have to be composed, in the Latourian sense of that word" (lecture I).
- "From where and how does a humanist historian begin to think in order to contribute to the work of composing 'the common' without in the process denying all that divides us in the space of politics?" Jaspers's "epochal consciousness" is chosen because it "comes out of a particular tradition—mainly German—of taking the whole of humanity as the object of philosophy of history" (lecture I).
- On the critics: "Some scholars, mainly from the Left, have expressed deep discomfiture with the label 'Anthropocene' and attacked it for its presumed ideological nature. Why not call it 'capitalocene,' they have asked, when it is the capitalist mode of production that made our greenhouse gas emissions and technologies have an impact on the climate of the planet." The note to that sentence (n. 49 by sequence; the text shows no numbers) cites "the essay by Andreas Malm and Alf Hornborg's, 'The Geology of Mankind? A Critique of the Anthropocene Narrative,' Anthropocene Review ... (published online January 7, 2014)". His reply: names of geological periods "are not usually required to say something about the causes"; "The name does not assign moral responsibility" (lecture I).
- The pragmatic distinction: "Let us call this figure of one-but-divided humanity by the Latin word homo simply to distinguish it from the Greek anthropos that has already been claimed by scientists"; read as homo, "we see climate change as a continuation of the story of capitalist globalization accentuating all the human inequities that are central to that story, however told" (lecture I).
- On world and Big History: "Within my own field, history, this assumption that thinking on large scales leads to a sense of human solidarity or 'global citizenship' has found a resurgent expression in a branch of historical scholarship that calls itself Big (or sometime Deep) History. In the hands of a pioneer of this movement, David Christian—as in the writings of other Big Historians—the history of the human species merges seamlessly with the history of 'humanity.'" He notes that "Christian directly cites the world-history pioneer William McNeill's 1986 presidential address to the American Historical Association as a source of his inspiration" and quotes McNeill's call for "an ecumenical history" (lecture II; notes cite Christian, "The Return of Universal History", History and Theory 49, 2010, 6–27, and "McNeill cited in ibid., 26": McNeill's words are therefore quoted at second hand through Christian).
- A note registers "a small but significant conceptual disagreement" with Daniel Lord Smail's On Deep History and the Brain (2008): on "If humanity is the proper subject of history ...", "I agree, and I do not: it depends on how one understands the words humanity and our"; he announces a paper "provisionally titled, 'From World-History to Big History: Some Friendly Amendments'" (lecture II, final note).
- "Humans, humans as a species, and humans as the makers of the Anthropocene are three distinct categories; we construct their archives differently" (lecture II); "we have fallen into 'deep' history, into deep, geological time" (lecture II).
- Close: "we may have to think of these divisive political histories not simply in the context of the history of capitalism but on the much larger canvas of geological and evolutionary histories" (lecture II).
- Biographical note at head (publisher's): "recently named the recipient of the 2014 Toynbee Foundation Prize for his contributions to global history"; lists Provincializing Europe as "(2008; 2000)".
- Discrepancy: the lecture's own note cites the 2012 New Literary History essay as "43 (2012): 25–42"; Crossref gives 43:1, pp. 1–18 (see N22).

**N5. José Neves and Marcos Cardão, "Provincializing History: A conversation with Dipesh Chakrabarty on the 20th Anniversary of Provincializing Europe", Práticas da História 11 (2020), 25–34** (https://praticasdahistoria.pt/article/view/24184). **Read in full.** Held in Lisbon, January 2020.
- Origins in labour history: in the archives of early-1970s Calcutta he found "a lot of new evidence for conflicts ... between Hindu and the Muslim workers"; "My Marxist friends and teachers, however, felt very uneasy about my findings. For, as readers of E. P. Thompson, they assumed that there was a story waiting to be written about 'the making of the Indian working class'"; "That very controversial paper was later published in the English journal Past and Present" (pp. 26–27; n. 1: "Communal Riots and Labour: Bengal's Jute Mill-Hands in the 1890s", Past & Present 91, May 1981, 140–169).
- On Marx: "my intellectual alienation from Europe, ironically, began outside of India" (p. 27); "without somebody like Marx guiding our analytical framework it would be very hard to think of modernity, capitalism, and modern India. - "the 20th century is a hunt for a series of substitutes for the proletariat" (p. 31); Guha, "our teacher", "undertook structuralist operations to distil out of empirical peasants an abstract consciousness" (p. 32).
- "part of Provincializing Europe, and part of the work I'm doing in climate change, has to do with this deeper question of what does it mean to be a human being in our times" (p. 33); the establishment of Aboriginal history in Australia was "the most revolutionary thing that happened in my academic world" there (p. 33).

**N8. Dipesh Chakrabarty, "Introduction", in "A Symposium on Dipesh Chakrabarty's The Climate of History in a Planetary Age", The Review of Politics 84:4 (2022), 592–593** (open access, CC BY; doi:10.1017/S0034670522000717; online 18 Oct. 2022). **Read in full.**
- p. 593: "This collapse of the distinction, defended over the last two centuries by philosophers ranging from Hegel and Marx to Croce and Collingwood, between humanist histories of humans and their natural histories provides the starting point for my book. The convergence of human and natural histories on a planetary scale, I argue, has rendered insufficient (but not unnecessary) the 500-year-old history of European imperial expansion ..., racial oppression, and global capitalism and technology that historians have so far used as a framework for explaining or understanding the modern world. Their periodizing labels such as 'modern' or 'global' now have to be thought together with the much larger-scale units of geological time".
- p. 593: "The global is anthropocentric while the planet de-centers and provincializes the human. Structured around this distinction, my book argues for a new philosophical anthropology."

### E1. `dipesh_chakrabarty → global`: critique / critique, directed

## 6. Proposed text

**description** (replace; keeps the existing pre-2000 sentences, adds post-2000 sentences, each attributed):
"A historian of modern South Asia in the Subaltern Studies collective. He argues that, within university-based historical discourse, an imagined 'Europe' remains its dominant theoretical reference: 'the sovereign, theoretical subject of all histories, including the ones we call “Indian,” “Chinese,” “Kenyan”', where 'Europe' and 'India' are 'hyperreal terms', figures of the imaginary 'subject to contestation' (1992). Rethinking Working-Class History (1989) reads Bengal’s jute-mill workers against an assumption of 'a hegemonic bourgeois culture' in Marx’s category of capital. 'Postcoloniality and the Artifice of History' (1992) calls Europe 'a silent referent in historical knowledge', and Provincializing Europe (2000) criticises the historicist 'first in Europe, then elsewhere' that placed colonized peoples in 'an imaginary waiting room of history', while holding European thought 'both indispensable and inadequate'; a 2000 essay separates History 1, the past 'posited by capital', from History 2, pasts that are 'not pasts separate from capital' but 'inhere in capital and yet interrupt and punctuate' its logic. In period, Sumit Sarkar called the trajectory he described 'debilitating', and the labour historian Rajnarayan Chandavarkar found a 'static, timeless, indeed Orientalist' culture in his Bengal book. Later readers of Provincializing Europe disputed it: Carola Dietze (2008, abstract only) regarded him as 'a thinker of radical historicism rather than as a critic of the discipline of history', and William Sewell (2014) found its 'Europe' a construct 'fused with' Marxism; at its twentieth anniversary Christopher Hill found 'ambiguities and contradictions' in it that were also 'the source of its wide impact' (2020). After 2000 he turned to climate. 'The Climate of History' (2009) argued that climate change spells 'the collapse of the age-old humanist distinction between natural history and human history'. His Tanner Lectures (2015) questioned Big History's hope that large-scale history yields 'global citizenship', and The Climate of History in a Planetary Age (2021) sets the planet beside the globe: 'The global is anthropocentric while the planet de-centers and provincializes the human' (2022). Critics answered from several sides: Malm and Hornborg (2014; abstract only, and as he reports) on the 'species' category; the historian of science Richard Staley (2024) on the novelty of the divide; Brooke Ackerly (2022) on his treatment of Indigenous knowledge. He replies that capital and empire are 'a necessary but insufficient framework'. The Calling of History (2015) studies the colonial historian Jadunath Sarkar."
