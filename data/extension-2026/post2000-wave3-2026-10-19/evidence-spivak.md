### 1i. Richard M. Eaton, "(Re)imag(in)ing Other²ness: A Postmortem for the Postmodern in India", Journal of World History 11:1 (Spring 2000): 57–78
- DOI `https://doi.org/10.1353/jwh.2000.0008` — **read in part**: pp. 57–61 and 77–78 (with notes 1–10) read; pp. 62–76 (his case studies) skimmed only. A historian of India writing in a world-history journal.
  - p. 57: the essay asks "how can one account for the appearance of postmodernist thought and postcolonial criticism in Indian historiography?"
  - p. 60: members of the collective shifted "from a positivist and empiricist orientation to one grounded more squarely in a literary criticism that draped itself in the banner of an amorphous, obscurantist phrasing: cultural studies"; "in the same volume [Subaltern Studies IV, 1985] Gayatri Spivak criticized subalternist historians for having adopted positivist methodologies and for treating the objects of their research--the subaltern classes--as enduring, essentialized categories ... Nonetheless Spivak, herself a literary critic, urged historians to continue their efforts to recover subaltern consciousness even while knowing this was impossible, and to do so by deploying “a strategic use of positivist essentialism in a scrupulously visible political interest.”" (n. 8 cites SS IV p. 342 and p. 345). "Articles in subsequent volumes of Subaltern Studies reflected the new discursive approaches pioneered in the 1985 volume".
  - p. 78 (balance sheet): granting the "modest reminder" of power in texts ("Careful historians have always done these things"), he finds it "vastly outweighed" by "a reinsertion of the British Raj to the center stage of Indian history, a neo-colonial vision of an essentialized India and Europe locked in implacable binary opposition, an ahistorical and structuralist methodology incapable of explaining historical particularity".
  - Limit: Eaton's target is the postmodern turn in Indian history as a whole; Spivak's 1985 essay is one named hinge (with Cohn), not his sole object. He does not discuss "Can the Subaltern Speak?" in the pages read.

### 1j. Arik Moran, "‘The Rani of Sirmur’ Revisited: Sati and sovereignty in theory and practice", Modern Asian Studies 49:2 (March 2015): 302–335; published online 17 September 2014
- DOI `https://doi.org/10.1017/S0026749X13000401` — **read in part**: abstract, introduction, the section "Redressing post-colonial discourse theory and Pahari sati" (opening argument), conclusion, and notes 1–5 (publisher HTML; print page numbers not visible, so passages are cited by section).   - Abstract: "a close reading of the records reveals profound discrepancies between Spivak's interpretation and conditions that existed in and around the kingdom at the time"; "the rani was actually an astute ruler, similar to her peers in the West Himalayan elite".
  - Introduction: "In 1985, Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak first applied literary theory to archival documents in ‘The Rani of Sirmur: An Essay in Reading the Archives’"; the essay's ideas "were further developed in the more widely circulated ‘Can the Subaltern Speak?’".   - Critique section: "Spivak's interpretation stems from a distinct political agenda that remained consistent" through CPR ch. 3 (1999); "while Spivak concedes that her analysis is ‘not historical work’ insofar as ‘historical knowledge cannot be established on single cases’, her misreading of evidence goes beyond the boundaries of the discipline as such"; e.g. treating "‘Gulari’, ‘Gulani’, and ‘Guleri’" as misspellings of the rani's name "rather than her official title"; "While the omissions resulting from Spivak's ideological reading of the archives may be misleading, her fresh approach to source material does point to the cardinal importance of interdisciplinary research."
  - Conclusion: "contrary to the claims of post-colonial discourse theory ... Pahari women in positions of power did find a voice and actively participated in the political struggles of their time".
  - n. 3 places "Can the Subaltern Speak?" in "the second phase of the development of the Subaltern Studies movement, in which empirical research gave way to postmodern literary criticism", citing Eaton (2000). n. 1 gives the 1985 essay as History and Theory 24:3 (October 1985), pp. 247–72 (Moran's note misprints the journal title).
