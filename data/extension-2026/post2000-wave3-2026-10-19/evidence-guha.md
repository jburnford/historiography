## 1. Sources read

### 1a. Bernardo A. Michael, review of *History at the Limit of World-History*, *Journal of World History* 15:4 (December 2004), pp. 530–532. DOI 10.1353/jwh.2005.0128
**Read in full.**
- Origin: "grew out of a series of lectures delivered by Ranajit Guha, one of the founding members of the Subaltern Studies Collective, at the Italian Academy for Advanced Studies in America at Columbia University" (p. 530).
- Argument as Michael states it: "he strives to show philosophy's complicity with colonialism and its forms of knowledge. For this reason he takes the German philosopher Georg Hegel and his conception of 'World-History' (Weltgeschichte) to task" (p. 530); everyday experience ("historicality, as Guha terms it") is pushed to the margins; what lay outside "was dismissed as 'Prehistory.'" (p. 530).
- Guha quoted by Michael: history outside World-History is "a creative engagement with the past as a story of man's being in the everyday world. It is in short, a call for historicality to be rescued from its containment in World-History" (book p. 6; review pp. 530–531). And (book p. 51): "World-History made its way to the subcontinent as an instrument of the East India Company's colonial project and helped it set up the Raj" (review p. 532).
- Chapter 4: indigenous traditions of *itihasa*; chapter 5: Tagore; Tagore quoted on "weal and woe of human life" (book p. 91).
- Praise: "welcome in that it joins a growing chorus of scholars such as Henri Lefebvre, Michel DeCerteau, Erving Goffman, and Emmanuel LeRoy Ladurie who have written on various aspects of everyday life" (pp. 531–532); "definitely worth a read for those interested in questions pertaining to everyday life and also in recent postcolonial efforts to rethink the practices of disciplinary history and thereby provincialize its European lineages" (p. 532).
- Criticism: "losing sight of the state at the level of the everyday might mean the loss of it as an object of critique"; "Guha's arguments are mostly confined to the conceptual level", with no evidence "about, for instance, how Hegel's ideas were translated into colonial realities"; "At times Guha seems to attribute to Hegel's World-History an agency that is hard to locate in the historicality of the colonial world" (p. 532).

### 1b. Columbia University Press page, *History at the Limit of World-History* (Italian Academy Lectures)
**Publisher description, contents and endorsements only.** URL https://cup.columbia.edu/book/history-at-the-limit-of-world-history/9780231124195.

### 1c. Amalendu K. Chakraborty, review of *Dominance without Hegemony*, *Journal of World History* 11:2 (Fall 2000), pp. 373–379. DOI 10.1353/jwh.2000.0032
**Read in full.** Reviews the Harvard University Press paperback (he gives 1998; the graph's book date is 1997).
- Characterisation: "a compilation of three interrelated essays", "an ideological critique of the South Asian colonial and nationalist state and its historiography from the perspective of Antonio Gramsci's philosophical reflections on 'hegemony' and 'domination'" (pp. 373–374).
- He reports Guha's argument that colonial historiography perpetuated "the prejudicial view, derived from the Hegelian view of history, that precolonial India had no state, therefore no history" (p. 376).
- Criticism: the absence of "the voices of the 'subalterns' themselves" reduces the project "to a mere armchair ideological 'discourse'" (p. 375); Guha "conveniently skips the portrayal of the Hindu social order in the Muslim historiography of India" (p. 377); Guha "views the subalterns as no better than 'noble savages'" (pp. 378–379); "knowingly disregards" the record of Indian participatory democracy; "It is unfortunate that Professor Guha's intellectual indebtedness to the Marxist-Leninist-Gramscian ideology of western European history does not allow him to examine Indian nationalist history from the perspective of Indian reality" (p. 379). Also calls him "a formidable Marxist historian" (p. 375).
- Judgement: a hostile review by a reviewer identified only by place (Minneapolis). Used for reception and the description; no edge.

### 1d. Vivek Chibber, response to Partha Chatterjee, published by Verso, 25 February 2014 (with an introduction by Clément Petitjean)
URL https://www.versobooks.com/blogs/1529-subaltern-studies-revisited-vivek-chibber-s-response-to-partha-chatterjee
**Read in part:** the introduction and the sections "Guha and the Bourgeois Revolutions" and "Does Guha refer to the bourgeois revolutions?".
- Context: "In April 2013, at the annual Historical Materialism conference held in New York, Vivek Chibber appeared alongside Partha Chatterjee in a much-anticipated critical debate" (Petitjean).
- Chibber's claim: "I argue in PTSC that Guha's work is both foundational for the entire Subalternist project ... and that it is fundamentally flawed. Its flaw is that it generates a set of contrastive claims about the Indian bourgeoisie and Indian capitalism that rest on a comparison with the European achievement. But the comparison fails because Guha quite dramatically misunderstands the European story." "The European bourgeoisie was no more committed to an integrated political culture than was its Indian counterpart."
- Chatterjee's defence, **as Chibber reports it** (Chatterjee's text unread): Guha "never makes any claims about the classic bourgeois revolutions" — his criticism is of the historiography of colonial India; "Indian bourgeoisie" means the Congress's middle-class leadership; any European referent is the mid-twentieth-century British state.
- Guha quoted by Chibber (*Dominance without Hegemony*, p. 19): liberal historiography presumes that capital "succeeded in overcoming the obstacles to its self-expansion ... well enough to enable the bourgeoisie to speak for all of that society, as it had done on the occasion of its historic triumphs in England in 1648 and France in 1789." Also DH p. 5 (the Indian bourgeoisie's "failure to measure up to the heroism of the European bourgeoisie in its period of ascendancy").

### 1e. Review symposium on Chibber, *Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital* (Verso, 2013), *Journal of World-Systems Research* 20:2 (2014), pp. 281–317 (open access)
- **Ho-fung Hung, "Introduction", p. 281, DOI 10.5195/jwsr.2014.560 — read in full.** "Focusing on the historically and empirically grounded works of Ranajit Guha and Dipesh Chakrabarty, Chibber juxtaposes their interpretation of European and Indian histories to his own interpretation, claiming that their case against Enlightenment universalism was premised on erroneous historiography." Five commentaries and Chibber's response.
- **William H. Sewell Jr., "On Vivek Chibber's *Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital*", pp. 300–303, DOI 10.5195/jwsr.2014.557 — read in full.** Guha's *Dominance without Hegemony* "was written from within a basically Marxist framework" (p. 300). On Guha's portrayal of the English and French revolutions: "this portrayal ... is standard-issue orthodox Marxism—and is by now well past its 'sell-by' date" (p. 301). He finds Chibber's grasp of French and English history "a bit shaky" (no mention of 1688; liberal contributions called "weak"), "Nevertheless, Chibber's most important conclusions about the European experience seem to me quite correct" (p. 301). He also notes he has "only a passing acquaintance" with Indian history (p. 300). [His pages on Chakrabarty go to the Chakrabarty draft.]
- **George Steinmetz, "On the Articulation of Marxist and Non-Marxist Theory in Colonial Historiography", pp. 282–288, DOI 10.5195/jwsr.2014.558 — read in part (pp. 282–284, 287).** "A more accurate title for this book would be something like The Subaltern School of History and the Specter of Capital" (p. 283). On Guha: "Guha was relying on a version of Marxist political theory and German history that was demolished by the critics of the Sonderweg thesis" (p. 284). He also criticises Chibber elsewhere (colonial states sometimes preserved "archaic" forms antithetical to capitalism's expansion, p. 287).
- **Bruce Cumings, "Back to Basics?", pp. 289–293, DOI 10.5195/jwsr.2014.563 — read in part (pp. 289–292).** On Guha's definition of hegemony: "Chibber's critique of Ranajit Guha's (1998) *Dominance without Hegemony* strikes me as similarly persuasive, at least at the level of how one defines hegemony" (p. 290). But "I don't know Guha's work, but this is a preposterous caricature of Chakrabarty's scholarship" (p. 292).
- **Vivek Chibber, "Confronting Postcolonial Theory: A Response to Critics", pp. 311–317, DOI 10.5195/jwsr.2014.561 — read in part (pp. 311–314).** "Guha's argument for there being a historic break between the political projects of the bourgeoisie in the West and in the East flows from a highly mythologized story about the great Bourgeois revolutions, one that was rejected decades ago by historians" (p. 313). He claims "All the critics in this forum accept both my reconstruction of Guha as well as my criticism of him" (p. 313) — an overstatement on his own forum's evidence, since Cumings disclaims knowledge of Guha's work. Answers Sewell: the revolutions' shifts "were nowhere near what is needed to validate Guha's argument", and movement towards inclusiveness came from "pressure from the laboring classes" (pp. 313–314).
- The book itself is **unread**; its date (Verso, 2013) and focus come from Hung and the Verso post.

### 1f. Discover Society: New Series 4:1 (4 January 2024), "Remembering Ranajit Guha"
- **John Holmwood, "Editorial: Remembering Ranajit Guha", DOI 10.51428/dsoc.2024.01.0001 — read in full.** "He died in Austria on April 28th, 2023, at the age of 99." The articles "are drawn from a memorial event – Remembering Ranajit Guha – held at the University of Sussex on October 20th, 2023." Bhambra's piece called "a major contribution to the history of political economy". The editorial also says Guha "was not especially productive during his period at Sussex".
- **Gurminder K. Bhambra, "Property and the Political Economy of Colonialism", DOI 10.51428/dsoc.2024.01.0005 — read in full.** On *A Rule of Property for Bengal* (1963): it addresses the debates among East India Company administrators over a "permanent settlement", in which revenue was to be entrusted to "a class of native entrepreneurs who had solid interests in the land and were politically reliable" (Guha, p. 9, as she quotes). Guha "set out their different economic premises – deriving from mercantilist, physiocratic, and classical political economy – and demonstrated that, despite their differences, they nonetheless shared a common political focus, establishing the permanence of orderly dominion." Reception she reports: Sarvepalli Gopal's 1966 review regarded it "primarily as an exercise in British intellectual history"; Guha rebutted such critics in the 1981 preface to the second edition (both mediated through Bhambra). Her criticism: "his failure to locate the discussions of the East India Company administrators within their colonial context"; "the refusal to consider colonialism as central to the emergence and development of capitalism perhaps blunted the force of his arguments"; also, citing Subrahmanyam's obituary (unread), little on the property regimes before Company rule.
- **Sanjay Seth, "Guha, Indian history, and me", DOI 10.51428/dsoc.2024.01.0004 — read in full.** A former doctoral student (ANU). It was "often commented" that Subaltern Studies applied the "history from below" of Thompson, Rudé and Hobsbawm, but that missed that they "characterised their subjects as 'backward', and their forms of protest and rebellion as 'prepolitical'." He finds in *Elementary Aspects* "two reasons ... in tension with each other" for the politics of peasant revolt. "This is the core idea underpinning his *Dominance without Hegemony* – that colonial capitalism and the colonial state integrated India into global capital and modern forms of statehood, but without establishing a hegemonic bourgeois culture."

### 1g. Dipesh Chakrabarty, "Ranajit Guha FAHA 1923–2023", Australian Academy of the Humanities, May 2024
URL https://humanities.org.au/?p=49088. **Read in full.** A Fellow's obituary by a former member of the collective.
- Honorary Fellow of the Academy, 1996. Died in Vienna on 28 April 2023. Left Canberra for Vienna in 1999.
- Guha "edited the first six volumes of the series".
- On *A Rule of Property*: it "showed an early awareness of the importance of ideas in economic history. He was clearly no votary of any kind of economic determinism".
- On the 2002 book (which he calls "History and the Limit of World History"): "a grand statement about literature being a far better communicator of senses of the past in the Indian subcontinent than the European discipline of history that had come to India as a practice of the colonial state"; "All Indian history of the disciplinary kind, Guha argued, was statist in inspiration"; "This book thus signaled his farewell to the discipline of history". "He also bade goodbye to writing in English. In the last ten or fifteen years of his life, Guha wrote several books and essays in Bengali and went on to win a prestigious literary prize in Calcutta" (prize not named; the Bengali books are not identified here).
- On the late phase: "He seems to have lost faith in emancipatory politics and developed a deeply humanistic sense of sympathy for the everyday tales of weal and woe".
