## 0. Priority question: does Winona Wheeler engage settler colonial studies?

**Answer: yes, once, and late, by adoption. No critique of the field was found.** Her earlier work used other colonial frames. In order:

1. **1995/1998, internal colonialism and "intellectual neocolonialism".**
   - Source: "'Ethnic' Assimilates 'Indigenous': A Study in Intellectual Neocolonialism", *Wicazo Sa Review* 13:1 (1998): 33–51. First given at UBC on 2 November 1995 (p. 50 note). Read in full.
   - She works from Robert Blauner's colonial model of the "third world within" (pp. 34–35). She argues that Indigenous peoples "are not an ethnic minority" but "members of our own First Nations" (pp. 40, 49). To classify them as an ethnic minority "is colonialist" (p. 40). She proposes "Fourth World Studies" as the comparative frame (p. 49).
   - There is a land-and-dispossession argument on p. 42: "every North American occupies our land". The "heirs of colonialism" cannot "deny the benefits they derive from our dispossession".
   - This anticipates later settler-colonial reasoning, but **she does not use "settler" or settler-colonial vocabulary**. Encode it as what it is (an Ethnic Studies / Native Studies critique, edge W3), **not** as settler-colonial theory.
2. **2000, postcolonial and anti-colonial theory.**
   - Source: *Decolonizing Tribal Histories* (PhD, Ethnic Studies, UC Berkeley, 2000). OCR read: abstract, contents and pp. 1–27 (with family passages skipped), pp. 62–99 and pp. 99–157.
   - Her frame is "intellectual colonialism" (pp. 79–80, with Fanon, Vizenor and L. T. Smith) and "Postcolonial Studies" (pp. 117–118, Fanon and Memmi).
   - "Settler" occurs once (p. 117), as one strand of the postcolonial debate: colonial relations "take on internal configurations between settler and Indigenous populations".
3. **2001.** Source: "Thoughts on the Responsibilities for Indigenous/Native Studies", *CJNS* 21:1 (2001): 97–104. Read in full. The vocabulary is decolonization and "anti-colonial research" (pp. 98, 100), with no settler vocabulary.
4. **2010.** "Cree Intellectual Traditions in History" (AU Press). Re-read in full. She names her teachers as including "critical and post-/anti-colonial scholars" (p. 48). No settler vocabulary.
5. **2018.** "'their brains were like paper'" (Shekon Neechie, 20 June 2018). Read in full. Neither "colonial" nor "settler" is used, apart from "colonization" (¶10).
6. **2021, adoption of Wolfe.**
   - Source: Denise Henning and Winona Wheeler, "Rural Indigenous students' postsecondary experiences: North and South of the Medicine Line", *New Directions for Student Services* 2020, nos. 171–172: 57–67. Online 30 April 2021. DOI 10.1002/ss.20365. Read in full (early-view pp. 1–11).
   - Section "Colonization and intergenerational trauma" (early-view p. 3): "In the Americas classic colonialism evolved into settler colonialism where foreigners immigrated to and permanently occupied Indigenous lands over which they forcefully asserted sovereignty. The goal of settler colonialism is the erasure of Indigenous peoples in order to establish settler rights to Indigenous lands and resources (Wolfe, 2006)."
   - Wolfe 2006 (*JGR*) is in the reference list. The article is co-authored, in higher-education research, and Henning is first author. The edge label should say so.
7. **Supporting context, not edge evidence.**
   - Columbia OHMA workshop notice, "Land Back! The Importance of Oral History in First Nation Land Claims Cases" (18 February 2021, read). Its first-person description says "the fight against settler state incursions continues". Authorship of the notice is not stated.
   - Columbia 2017 bio (read): her interests include "colonialism and anti-colonial studies".
8. **Blocked.** The C Magazine conversation with Joi T. Arcand (https://cmagazine.com/articles/im-a-little-too-rebellious-for-that-a-conversation-with-joi-t-ar) was tried once on 2026-10-28. Again only the title and site chrome were served. **Blocked**; no workaround attempted.
9. **Unread.**
   - *Indigenous Oral History Manual: Canada and the United States* (Routledge 2023, with Trimble, Quinlan and Sommer; DOI 10.4324/9781003159049). Known through catalogue listings only.
   - "Indigenous Voices, Indigenous Histories, Part III" (1999).
   - The 2005 *Walking a Tightrope* chapter.

**Proposal:** one influence edge, `patrick_wolfe → winona_wheeler` (W1), plus a `settler_colonialism` roster row (below). Also edge W2, recording the postcolonial frame she actually used from 2000 to 2010. No `winona_wheeler → settler_colonialism` contribution or critique edge: nothing read supports one.

**The same question for the other three, in brief:**
- **McCallum:** her own 2017 abstract uses settler language: "a white settler nation" (edge C2). Named readers attach her work to the frame:
  - Williams 2016, "Following Wolfe";
  - Razack and CHOICE on *Structures of Indifference* (2018).
- **Innes:** his 2018 essays frame the 1880s deaths as "colonial genocide" (¶1, n. 1, after Woolford, Benvenuto and Hinton). "Settler" appears only in his paraphrase of Daschuk (¶¶3, 11 of "Clearing the Plains of Accountability"). There is no settler-colonial edge. Edge_2153 is rerouted for this reason.
- **Macdougall:**
  - No settler-colonial vocabulary in what was read.
  - In 2010 she states she does "not place this research exclusively within" the framework of challenging colonialism (*One of the Family*, p. xii).
  - Her 2019 contribution to *Settler City Limits* is unread (held).
