## 2. Latour and Callon criticise SSK (source `latour` → target `ssk`)

### 2a. Qualified appropriation (1979–1988)

- Latour, *Pasteurization* (1988), p. 256 n. 21: "David Bloor (1976) has most clearly defined the task of any sociology of science by introducing the notion of symmetry. The losers and the winners must be studied in the same way and explained with the same set of notions. If the evolution of our field has made the notion of a 'social' explanation obsolete, the principle of symmetry remains the basis of most work in the area." The same note keeps Bloor's symmetry and drops social explanation.
- Ibid., p. 257 n. 27: "the British school courageously entered into the content, despising this American sociology of scientists that was doing only half the job. See D. Bloor (1976); H. Collins (1985); S. Shapin (1982). In spite of its great achievements, this enterprise appears disappointing because the contents and the contexts remain very far apart. Most of the sociology of science is internalist epistemology sandwiched between two slices of externalist sociology. We are now at a new crossroads: we must either give up studying the contents of science or change the sociology we started with." The same note says Merton's programme "seems more reasonable". Bloor 1999, p. 82, cites this page when he aligns Latour with Merton.
- Ibid., p. 258 n. 28: "Conservatism, Catholicism, love of law and order, fidelity to the Empress, brashness, passion—those are approximately all we get of the 'social factors' acting on Pasteur. […] But they are not much if we put on the other side all the scientific work to be explained."
- Ibid., p. 260 n. 5: "whenever I use the words 'interest' and 'interested,' I am not referring to the 'interest theory' expounded by what is now called the Edinburgh School. B. Barnes (1974); D. Bloor (1976). […] Interests cannot explain science and society; they are what will be explained once the experiment is over."
- Latour 1999, p. 114 (debt): "I feel entirely in David's debt. His symmetry principle, his Strong Program, allowed me and many colleagues in France to escape from the utter domination of the French epistemologists". Latour also says he "translated, published, taught, discussed and defended for ten years the contributions of SSK" (n. 2: Bloor translated in 1982). This is the basis for edge_685.

### 2b. Explicit critique (1992–1999)

- Latour 1992, "One More Turn", §2: "In our small field, Bloor's book (1976) was the high tide mark of this asymmetrical philosophy. As an obedient child of the Critique, Bloor designated Durkheimian social structures to occupy the Sun's focus […] However the very success of this principle of symmetry disguised the complete asymmetry of Bloor's argument. Society was supposed to explain Nature!" Bloor 1999, p. 83, cites the last sentence as Latour 1992, p. 278. The same section: "To this day, Bloor has not realized that his principle cannot be implemented if another much more radical symmetry is not introduced […] This 90° shift is what I call 'one more turn after the social turn'."
- Ibid., §3 ("Third modification" passage): "the intermediary solution —social realism alternating with natural relativism— advocated for many years by colleagues like David Bloor or Harry Collins are, in the long run, counterproductive for the field as well as for their own program". In §1 Latour describes Collins's work as internalist: "When, for instance, Harry Collins added to the gravitation waves, animals such as replication, negotiation, styles, core-sets, and authority, philosophers of science mistook that zoo for the social (and so did Collins (1985))."
- Callon and Latour 1992, p. 349: their yardstick is the one "that allows us to read most of SSK as 'reactionary,' because they start from a closed definition of the social and then use this repertoire as an explanation of nature—most of the time to no avail." On p. 348: "We have never been interested in giving a social explanation of anything, but we want to explain society". On p. 351: "it is utterly impossible to achieve the social students' very goals—disputing scientists' hegemony, explaining the closure of controversies, applying Bloor's principle of symmetry […]—without shifting from the horizontal axis to the vertical one, that is, without completing their symmetry principle with ours."
- Callon and Latour 1992, p. 355, on Collins: "Extremely good at showing the opening of controversies […] Collins has nothing to say about the closing of controversies, the non-negotiability of facts, and the slow routinization that redistributes skills; he simply shifts the burden to the Edinburgh school." On pp. 367–368: "Collins's solution is a good old Kantian divide." The reply is not wholly hostile. On p. 344 they write: "We learned a great deal from Collins's work—the study of active controversies, the meticulous application of symmetry in the treatment of parasciences, the emphasis put on local skills, the careful study of replication, the dismantlement of epistemologists' hegemony".
- Latour, *We Have Never Been Modern* (1993), p. 94: "the principle of symmetry defined by [Bloor] leads rapidly to an impasse. If it requires an iron discipline in its explanation, the principle itself is asymmetrical […] it brackets off Nature and makes the [Society] pole carry the full weight of explanation. Constructivist where Nature is concerned, it is realistic about Society (Callon and Latour, 1992; Collins and Yearley, [1992])." The words in brackets were lost in the OCR and are supplied from context. On p. 92: "The first principle of symmetry upset traditional sociology of knowledge by requiring that error and truth be treated in the same terms". On p. 55: "the Edinburgh daredevils deprived the dualists - and indeed themselves, as they were soon to realize - of half of their resources".
- Latour 1999, p. 116: "The Strong Program was useful and still is against the few remaining epistemologists. It has become an obstacle for the continuation of science studies." On p. 117, on objects in SSK: "This is the position I have criticized and if this aligns me with the worst enemies of science studies, well so be it." On p. 128: "I have always described the failure of the Strong Program as a 'felix culpa' […] we had to abandon the first principle of symmetry (and also now the generalized principle, I am happy to announce)."
- *Science in Action* (1987), quoted by Bloor 1999, p. 101 (Bloor's quotation, not read directly): relativists' "position is called symmetric…" (Latour 1987, p. 195); "in the symmetric stand it is the very existence of the scientific network […] that is utterly ignored" (p. 196). In "One More Turn", §3, Latour cites "(Latour, 1987, rule 3 and 4)" for the claim that neither Nature nor Society can explain the practice of science.

The criticised parties replied in their own words (§3 below). Bloor 1999, p. 82: "his criticisms are based on a systematic misrepresentation of the position he rejects, and […] his own approach, in so far as it is different, is unworkable."

## 3. SSK criticises Latour and ANT (source `ssk` → target `latour`)

- Bloor 1999, p. 81, frames the exchange: "Bruno Latour is a vehement critic of the sociology of knowledge in general, and the Strong Program in particular." On p. 82: "I think Latour is wrong: there is no further 'turn' to be taken. […] My point is that Latour's ideas do not represent the way forward. If anything they are a step backwards."
- Ibid., p. 87, the core objection: "Latour's attack depends on a specific characterisation of the aims of those who pursue the Strong Program. They are said to be trying to explain nature in terms of society. In reply it has to be said that this is a profound misrepresentation. The aim isn't to explain nature, but to explain shared beliefs about nature." On p. 88: "Somehow the naturalistic and materialistic emphasis on 'both' has become transformed, in the critic's mind, into an idealistic and subjectivist 'neither'."
- Ibid., p. 84, partly concedes: "Latour, rightly, rejects the symmetry principle understood in this way." That is, as the principle "forbidding both groups access to the real".
- Ibid., p. 95: "A number of writers have already responded in a thorough and critical way to Latour's proposals (see for example Amsterdamska, 1990; Collins and Yearley, 1992; Gingras, 1995; Knorr-Cetina, 1985; Schaffer, 1991; Shapin, 1988; Sturdy, 1991; Van den Belt, 1995). […] Taken collectively I find that the criticisms in this literature are devastating." On p. 94 Bloor notes: "Critics have pointed out that something remarkably like direct or naive realism turns up in Latour's methodology (Collins and Yearley, 1992)."
- Ibid., p. 97, on actants and the Leibnizian vocabulary: "this looks like a formula for imposing confusion on ourselves: it is obscurantism raised to the level of a general methodological principle."
- Ibid., pp. 110–111, conclusion: "Latour has endorsed, and encouraged, a wholly false stereotype of the Strong Program. He has joined those who insist on reading it as a species of idealism rather than, as it really is, as a species of materialism. He belongs to the ranks of those, like the recent contributors to The Flight from Science and Reason […]".
- Latour's reply acknowledges the target and the charge. In 1999, p. 113, he sums up Bloor's paper: "The result is a devastating critique: when it is good, the sociology done at CSI is nothing more than traditional SSK couched in a fancy vocabulary; when we tried to amend the tradition, we succeeded only in adding obscurity and falling back into naive realism". In n. 6, p. 117: "The critiques made by Schaffer (1991) of my position are exactly the same as those of Bloor, and exactly as ill-conceived."
- Collins and Yearley (1992). Their own text was not read. Callon and Latour 1992 quote two passages, on p. 347. The first concerns Collins's gravity waves rewritten in Callon's terms: "the resulting account […] would look just like the account of a conventional historian of science […] The language changes but the story remains the same." The second concerns Latour's door-closer paper: "The consequences of the semiotic method [of Latour] amount to a backward step, leading us to embrace once more the very priority of technological, rule-bound description, adopted from scientists and technologists, that we once learned to ignore." On p. 345 the respondents summarise the objection: "even if our position is philosophically radical and justified, its practical effect on the use of empirical material is prosaic, reactionary, and dangerously confusing […] we are accused of going back to the realist position to explain scientific facts and to technical determinism to account for artifacts." Latour 1992 ("One More Turn", §4) reports: "If I begin, for instance, granting activity to the non-humans once again, sociologists of science (Collins and Yearley, 1990) begin protest that outmoded realist positions are back".
- The Collins and Yearley paper criticises Callon's scallops paper as well as Latour's work (Callon and Latour 1992, pp. 344 and 354). Callon has no atlas entry. He sits on the `sts` roster.

## 4. Philosophy of science against SSK: Laudan, and the SSK replies

- Laudan 1977, *Progress and Its Problems*, pp. 202–203, proposes the "arationality assumption": "the sociology of knowledge may step in to explain beliefs if and only if those beliefs cannot be explained in terms of their rational merits." He adds that "it has recently come under attack by historical sociologists". On p. 201 he argues that a total sociology of belief would be "self-indicting". Bloor and Barnes are not named in the pages I read. Bloor 1999, p. 81 n. 1, files "Lakatos (1971), Laudan (1977) and more recently Haack (1996)" under the "'weak' programme".
- Laudan 1981, "The Pseudo-Science of Science?" (direct critique of Bloor's symmetry thesis). **I did not read the text.** The only verbatim words available come through Fehér 1996, who quotes the 1996 reprint in *Beyond Positivism and Relativism*. On p. 170 she quotes Laudan: "a belief is rational or reasonable provided the agent can give reasons for it and can show that those reasons were antecedent to the adoption of the belief" (Laudan 1996, p. 195). On p. 169 she quotes his new appendix: his "original uneasiness about the strong program persists" (Laudan 1996, p. 209). Treat these as secondary quotations.
- Bloor's 1981 reply ("Strengths of the Strong Programme") is likewise known only through Fehér 1996, pp. 169–170, who quotes the 1984 reprint, p. 84. Laudan "shifts his definition of rationality. It begins as a descriptive concept, but finishes as an evaluative one, and in the course of this evolution it also changes its reference from being a property of an individual to being a property of a group." Secondary quotation.
- Shapin 1982 positions SSK against these critics (read, OCR). On p. 157: "One can either debate the possibility of the sociology of scientific knowledge or one can do it. […] more recently Professor Laudan has echoed Mannheim's half-century old remark […] In Laudan's view the cognitive sociology of knowledge has lamentably failed in that task." On p. 158: "Unfortunately for writers who take this view, it is already too late; the historical sociology of scientific knowledge has gone ahead without them." Note 3 (p. 199) cites Laudan 1977, ch. 7, and then lists "programmatic criticisms of Laudan's strictures: Barry Barnes, 'Vicissitudes of belief' […] (1979); David Bloor, 'The strengths of the strong programme' […] (1981)." On pp. 194–195 Shapin builds the "coercive model" of sociology, and note 32 says this model "is most explicitly set forth in Laudan, Progress and its problems". On p. 198 he closes: "there might also be no more talk of 'the widely acknowledged failure of cognitive sociology to explain any interesting scientific episodes'" (note 40: Laudan 1977, p. 219). On p. 176 he also names "a programme, such as that of Lakatos and his followers, that makes empirical history dependent upon philosophical judgments". His title answers Lakatos's "History of science and its rational reconstructions" (cited in his bibliography).
- Latour 1992, §1, takes Shapin 1982 as settling that "the content of science is thoroughly studiable […] I take those two points as being established beyond doubt (Shapin, 1982; Latour, 1987)." On this point Latour and Shapin agree. The split comes over explanatory resources.

## 5. Woolgar's reflexivity critique

- Woolgar's own texts were not read: *Science: The Very Idea* (1988), *Knowledge and Reflexivity* (1988), "Interests and Explanation" (SSS 1981) and "Some Remarks about Positionism" (1992). There is no verbatim Woolgar evidence here.
- Others describe it in period. Latour 1992, §1: "Reflexivists have argued all along that it was not desirable to provide a social explanation of scientific content since it would mean that sociology was immune to the critical treatment that it applied to chemistry or physics (Woolgar, 1988; Ashmore, 1989)." Callon and Latour 1992, p. 346 (caption of fig. 12.2): "reflexivists are those who hamstring the players of the Social team". On p. 364: "Collins attacks Woolgar in the same paper where he attacks us". So Collins and Yearley criticised Woolgar as well.
- The 1981 Woolgar–Barnes exchange in SSS 11 is attested by metadata. Barnes's title, "(Response to Woolgar)", confirms a reply. Neither text was read.
- Atlas home: Woolgar sits on the `sts` roster (laboratory strand, Latour's coauthor). He has no entry of his own.

## 6. Feminist critiques bearing on SSK/STS

- Haraway 1988 (read), p. 576: "Recent social studies of science and technology […] have made available a very strong social constructionist argument for all forms of knowledge claims". On p. 577: "The strong program in the sociology of knowledge joins with the lovely and nasty tools of semiology and deconstruction to insist on the rhetorical nature of truth, including scientific truth." Also on p. 577: "the further I get in describing the radical social constructionist program […] the more nervous I get." On p. 578: "we end up with a kind of epistemological electroshock therapy". Note 1 (pp. 596–597) makes Latour's *Pasteurization* the main exhibit, calling it "brilliant and maddening aphoristic polemic", while borrowing his anti-purity point "for feminists".
  - This is a critique of a constructionist position that bundles SSK, Latour and deconstruction together. It is not aimed at the SSK programme as such. Her gloss of the strong program as insisting on "the rhetorical nature of truth" is one Bloor would reject (cf. Bloor 1999, pp. 87–88: SSK is "naturalistic and causal"). Haraway sits on the `sts` roster, and her critique sits inside that field. Harding and Latour also cite her favourably (Latour 1999, n. 4).
- Harding 1986: not read. Haraway's note at p. 596 says "Situated Knowledges" originated as a commentary on that book. No evidence was found here of a direct Harding critique of SSK.

## 7. Judgement

**Supported**

1. **`latour` → `ssk` (critique).** Strong support, all in-period and in Latour's own words. See Latour 1992, §2 ("Society was supposed to explain Nature!", cited by Bloor at p. 278); Callon and Latour 1992, p. 349 ("read most of SSK as 'reactionary'") and p. 355 (Collins "shifts the burden to the Edinburgh school"); *We Have Never Been Modern*, p. 94 ("Constructivist where Nature is concerned, it is realistic about Society"); and Latour 1999, p. 116 ("It has become an obstacle for the continuation of science studies"). Bloor 1999, p. 87, replies: "a profound misrepresentation". This is a critique that follows a qualified appropriation (Latour 1988, n. 21; 1999, p. 114). The edge note should say so, and edge_685 should stay as the debt edge. Callon is a co-critic (1992). He has no entry, so he belongs on the evidence note or roster.
2. **`ssk` → `latour` (critique).** Supported in Bloor's own words: Bloor 1999, p. 82 ("a step backwards", "unworkable"), p. 87 ("profound misrepresentation"), p. 97 ("obscurantism raised to the level of a general methodological principle") and p. 110 ("a wholly false stereotype"). Collins and Yearley 1992 is a second SSK critique (title, pages and target confirmed). Their words appear here only as Callon and Latour quote them (p. 347), so that evidence is secondary. Latour 1999, p. 113, confirms he was the target. Shapin 1988 and Schaffer 1991 are cited as further critiques but were not read.
3. **Philosophy of science → `ssk` (critique by Laudan).** The critique is real and in-period: Laudan 1977, pp. 202–203 (read), and Laudan 1981 (unread here; secondary quotations only). SSK replied in its own words: Shapin 1982, pp. 157–158, 198 and n. 3; Bloor 1981 via Fehér. **No atlas entry houses Laudan or philosophy of science.** `philhistory` covers philosophy of history, and `epistemology` is restricted to Bachelard and Canguilhem. Recommendation: add Laudan as `critic` on the `ssk` roster, with Shapin 1982 as the reply. Do not draw a `science → ssk` edge, because Laudan is not speaking for history of science. Before any edge or roster text quotes Laudan 1981 directly, read the article.

**Not supported, or not yet**

4. **`sts`-level critique edges.** No evidence found. The disputes target the strong programme, Edinburgh or "SSK" by name. Latour's 1992 diagnosis that "social studies of science are at a standstill" is a self-critique from inside the field, aimed at SSK's explanatory frame. Haraway criticises a constructionist strand from inside STS. Laudan targets the "cognitive sociology of knowledge". None warrants an edge to or from `sts`.
5. **Woolgar → `ssk`.** Probably real (1981, 1988), but unverified in Woolgar's own words. Not supported until *Science: The Very Idea* or "Interests and Explanation" is read. Woolgar also has no entry.
6. **Feminist edges (`gender` → `ssk`/`sts`, or Haraway/Harding).** Not supported as edges. Haraway 1988, pp. 576–578, is a qualified critique of "radical social constructionism", including the strong program. She makes it from within STS while drawing on Latour. At most it belongs in a strand or evidence note. Harding was not read.

**edge_138 (ssk–latour comparison).** If both critique edges are added, edge_138 becomes redundant. Its evidence note already records the 1999 disagreement ("Bloor defends material and social causal explanation; Latour contests the assigned roles of objects and society"), which is the content of the two critique edges. Keeping it would give one pair four edges (comparison, contribution, and two critiques). Recommendation: retire edge_138 and move its caution ("not an assertion of a one-way genealogy") into the edge_685 or critique-edge notes. If the comparison view needs a teaching link, keep edge_138 but cut its note back to the method contrast (programme explanation vs laboratory ethnography). The disagreement text then belongs to the critique edges alone. This is an editorial call for the user.

**Gaps worth closing before release:** Laudan 1981 and "More on Bloor" (1982); Bloor 1981; Collins and Yearley 1992 (both chapters); Woolgar 1988 and 1992; Bloor's 1999 rejoinder (pp. 131–136); Shapin 1988; Schaffer 1991; the English text of *Science in Action*, pp. 99 and 195–196, and rules 3–4.
