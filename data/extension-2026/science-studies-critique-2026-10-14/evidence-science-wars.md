## 2. Gross and Levitt, *Higher Superstition* (1994)

**Text not read.** archive.org copies are lending-only (IA search-inside refused); no open copy found; Google Books API quota exhausted. Every Gross–Levitt sentence below is a **secondary quotation from an in-period text that I read**, with the page number that text gives.

Whom they attacked, as recorded by in-period readers:

- **Science studies as such (historians, philosophers and sociologists of science).** Jardine and Frasca-Spada (SHPS 1997, editorial, §I, read): "Gross and Levitt showed sharp eyes for the pretentious and absurd in the works of American postmodernists, feminists, multiculturalists, radical environmentalists and, alas, exponents of science studies -- that is, historians, philosophers and sociologists of science."
- **The Edinburgh strong programme (SSK).** Edge, "The Umpire Strikes Back" (web text, EASST site, 16 Feb 1997, read), n. 4: G&L p. 25 name as a target of "social constructivism" "the 'strong programme' in sociology of science associated with the Edinburgh school"; "No citations are given, and no work by the key members of the Edinburgh School (Barry Barnes and David Bloor) is listed in this book's References, although 'a certain David Bloor... a sociologist identified with the Edinburgh School of unyielding cultural constructivists' (52) makes a brief appearance, and his logic (as recounted by a certain Stanley Aronowitz) is duly derided."
- **Aronowitz (*Science as Power*, 1988).** Harrell, AMS *Notices* Oct 1996 (web version, read) quotes G&L pp. 50–51: "Its chief method seems to be to invoke from the philosophy of science as many na[mes as possible] ... names and phrases are simply run in and out of the text as props for Aronowitz's views."
- **Feminist critique (Keller; cell-biology study group).** JFS 1997 §II quotes G&L's reply to Evelyn Fox Keller (they cite G&L p. 139): "Few serious thinkers about science, however, outside the camps of feminists and social constructivists, argue that the stable results of science, those that have been subject to empirical test over time and have survived, are not written in nature. Most know that whatever the underlying calligraphy, self-correcting science is the best translation of it we have." JFS §IV: G&L suggest the authors of "The Importance of Feminist Critique for Contemporary Cell Biology" mistake "imagery" for biological content.
- **Postmodernism / chaos-theory extrapolations.** JFS §IV epigraph, cited to G&L p. 106: "We must hope that the painful bolus of postmodernism will pass through the costive bowels of academic life sooner rather than later. Pass, of course, it will eventually. Keeping the hard sciences from contamination should not be impossible..." JFS: G&L dismiss metaphorical extensions of chaos theory as "'confused', 'vaporous' and 'grotesque'".
- **Their own method claim.** JFS §III, cited to G&L p. 24: "the dissecting blade of scientific scepticism, with its insistence that theories are worthy of respect only to the extent that their assertions pass the twin test of internal logical consistency and empirical verifications".
- Chapter plan (ERIC document resume ED 400 744, abstract, read): chapters on "the academic left and academic insularity", "the history of the American left", "cultural constructivism", "postmodernist approaches to cultural studies and chaos theories", "feminist thinking and science", radical environmentalism (ch. 6), race and AIDS (ch. 7), causes of hostility (ch. 8), and the future (ch. 9). Kantrowitz's review (*Physics Today*, Jan 1995, p. 55, read) names chapters "The Realm of Idle Phrases" and "Does It Matter?".
- **Sokal and Bricmont** (1998, p. 86 n. 120, read) cite "the 'homely example' in Gross and Levitt (1994, pp. 57-8)" against the strong reading of Latour's Third Rule; so pp. 57–58 of *HS* belong to the constructivism chapter.
- Who was **not** a named target, so far as the evidence goes: no in-period source I read says G&L attack Kuhn or the history-of-science profession by name. (Shapin and Schaffer, Pickering, Collins: not attested as G&L targets in the texts read.)

Replies to Gross and Levitt (in-period):
- **Social Text "Science Wars" issue was conceived as a reply.** Duke publisher's page for the issue (read): "now the nineties may bear witness to the 'Science Wars,' a conflict led by conservatives in science such as Paul Gross and Norman Levitt against so-called science bashers." Robbins and Ross (Lingua Franca July/Aug 1996, read): "We wanted to gauge how science critics were responding to the attacks of Paul Gross and Norman Levitt, and other conservatives in science." Sokal (A House Built on Sand, n. 13, read): "The 'Science Wars' special issue of Social Text was conceived primarily to attack Paul R. Gross and Norman Levitt".
- **SSK reply: David Edge** (founding director of the Edinburgh Science Studies Unit; editor, *Social Studies of Science*). "The Umpire Strikes Back" (read): "A powerful group of American scientists, apparently for political reasons, have chosen to mount a vicious attack on those scholars whom they take to be 'flying from science and reason'. One of their prime targets is 'social constructivism'". On the strong programme: "this group of scholars has always maintained that reality plays a central role in what comes to be believed about it". The result is "a sense of insidious harassment which comes close to constituting an offence against natural justice." He quotes Levitt's description of SSK, from *Technoscience* 9.2 (Spring 1996) p. 29 (not read): "a body of work founded on silly philosophy, sloppy history, anemic research, boundless ignorance, and just plain lousy scholarship"; and Gross, ibid. p. 30: "a deeply disapproving stance towards science, sometimes combined with a profound ignorance of it". Edge's petition after the 1996 4S/EASST Bielefeld meeting (read) complains of "personal harassment and stress arising from the so-called 'Science Wars' campaign".
- **Historian of science reply: M. Norton Wise**, "The Enemy Without and the Enemy Within", *Isis* 87.2 (June 1996) 323–27. **Not read** (JSTOR). Edge n. 4 reports that Wise "accuses Gross & Levitt of 'a willful strategy of distortion and demonization' (323)". This is the HSS journal answering the book.

## 3. Sokal's hoax and the *Social Text* "Science Wars" issue (1996)

Texts read: "Transgressing the Boundaries" (*Social Text* 46/47, pp. 217–252; author's web copy, no print pages, cited by note number); "A Physicist Experiments with Cultural Studies" (*Lingua Franca*, May/June 1996, pp. 62–64; author's ASCII copy, cited by range); Robbins and Ross, editors' reply (*Lingua Franca*, July/Aug 1996; copy on Sokal's site); "What the Social Text Affair Does and Does Not Prove" (author's text dated 8 April 1997, printed in Koertge ed., *A House Built on Sand*, OUP 1998; cited by note number); Social Text issue contents from the Duke publisher's page.

**3a. Whom the parody names.** The opening paragraph claims that "revisionist studies in the history and philosophy of science have cast further doubt on its credibility", with note 2: "Kuhn (1970), Feyerabend (1975), Latour (1987), Aronowitz (1988b), Bloor (1991)"; and that "feminist and poststructuralist critiques have demystified the substantive content of mainstream Western scientific practice", note 3: "Merchant (1980), Keller (1985), Harding (1986,1991), Haraway (1989,1991), Best (1991)". It names as its main exhibits "Aronowitz's analysis of the cultural fabric that produced quantum mechanics", "Ross' discussion of oppositional discourses in post-quantum science", "Irigaray's and Hayles' exegeses", and "Harding's comprehensive critique". Its note 30 praises "Latour (1988)", "A relativistic account of Einstein's relativity", as "an eminently accessible introduction to special relativity". The bibliography also lists Woolgar (1988), Pickering (1984), Merz and Knorr Cetina (1994).

**3b. Whom the confession names (Lingua Franca 1996, pp. 62–64).** The targets are "certain precincts of the American academic humanities", "a leading North American journal of cultural studies --- whose editorial collective includes such luminaries as Fredric Jameson and Andrew Ross", "the intellectual arrogance of Theory --- meaning postmodernist _literary_ theory", and "epistemic relativism": "The recent turn of many 'progressive' or 'leftist' academic humanists and social scientists toward one or another form of epistemic relativism betrays this worthy heritage". It opens with a Laudan epigraph (*Science and Relativism*, 1990). **No science-studies author is named in the confession.** The argument against "the social construction of reality" is pitched at cultural studies, not at SSK or history of science.

**3c. The editors' reply (Robbins and Ross, LF July/Aug 1996).** "Others were concerned that his hoax might spark off a new round of caricature and thereby perpetuate the climate in which science studies and cultural studies have been subject recently to so much derision from conservatives in science." "Like Gross and Levitt, he appears to have absorbed these critiques only at the level of caricatures, and has been re-issuing these caricatures in the form of otherworldly fanatics who deny the existence of facts, objective realities, and gravitational forces." "What's important to us is not so much the gulf of comprehension between 'the two cultures,' but rather the gulf of power between experts and lay voices". They list the issue's contributors: "Sandra Harding, Steve Fuller, Emily Martin, Hilary Rose, Langdon Winner, Dorothy Nelkin, Richard Levins, George Levine, Sharon Traweek, Sarah Franklin, Ruth Hubbard, Joel Kovel, Stanley Aronowitz, and Les Levidow"; the book version (Duke, 1996) adds "Katherine Hayles, Michael Lynch, Roger Hart, and Richard Lewontin".

**3d. Sokal turns to science studies proper (1997/1998).** "What the Social Text Affair Does and Does Not Prove" is the text in which Sokal names SSK and Latour:
- Disclaimer: "From the mere fact of publication of my parody I think that not much can be deduced. It doesn't prove that the whole field of cultural studies, or cultural studies of science -- much less sociology of science -- is nonsense."
- On SSK: "I think most scientists and philosophers of science would be astonished to learn that 'the natural world has a small or non-existent role in the construction of scientific knowledge', as prominent sociologist of science Harry Collins claims[10]; or that 'reality is the consequence rather than the cause' of the so-called 'social construction of facts', as Bruno Latour and Steve Woolgar assert.[11]" (n. 10: Collins, SSS 11 (1981) p. 3, with the concession that Collins may intend "a methodological injunction"; n. 11: *Laboratory Life* p. 237).
- On Latour: "Latour has produced 40 pages of comical misunderstandings of a theory that is nowadays routinely taught to intelligent college freshmen, and Social Studies of Science found it a worthy scholarly contribution." On the Third Rule of Method: "much sloppy thinking in Science Studies, like that in Latour's Third Rule of Method, involves conflating concepts that need to be distinguished."
- On the strong programme: "The extreme versions of social constructivism and relativism -- such as the Edinburgh 'strong programme' -- are, I think, largely based on the same failure to distinguish clearly between ontology, epistemology, and the sociology of knowledge." Conclusion: "the 'strong programme', like Latour's Third Rule of Method, is ambiguous in its intent; and, depending on how one resolves the ambiguity, it becomes either a valid and mildly interesting corrective to the most naive psychological and sociological notions -- reminding us that 'true beliefs have causes too' -- or else a gross and blatant error."
- He endorses Kitcher's essay in the same volume: "his essay pleases me. Indeed, I agree with nearly everything in it."

**3e. Responses to the hoax (read).**
- Latour (Le Monde, 18 Jan 1997, "Y a-t-il une science après la guerre froide?"; **not read**, text not located). Two sentences survive in in-period quotations: (i) as quoted by Jardine and Frasca-Spada (SHPS 1997, §I, in English): "A small number of theoretical physicists deprived of the fat budgets of the cold war, seek a new menace against which they heroically offer the protection of their esprit... France, in their eyes, has become another Colombia, a country of dealers who produce hard drugs -- derridium, lacanium, to which American doctoral students have no more resistance than to crack." (ii) JFS §V: "All of us, researchers in the exact and in the soft sciences, politicians and users, have an interest in possessing the most realistic possible vision of what the sciences are and are not capable of doing. We are all in the same boat, launched into the same controversies." Sokal reports (Le Monde, 31 Jan 1997, his English translation, read) that Latour had told *Libération* (3 Dec 1996): "It's a clever joke, an astute intervention. It gives a good thrashing to people who deserve it. [But not to] researchers who, like me, work in Science Studies" who "have scientific training".
- Sokal's reply to Latour (Le Monde, 31 Jan 1997): "Latour is too audacious when he claims, without offering the slightest evidence, that 'a very small number of theoretical physicists, deprived of their fat Cold War budgets, are searching for a new threat'"; "how then does one explain the numerous sociologists, historians, literary critics and philosophers who have joined in the critique of postmodern relativism?"; "among these Masters one indeed finds Derrida and Lacan, Aronowitz and Haraway -- but one also finds our overly modest friend ... Bruno Latour"; Latour "mixes up ontology and epistemology, and he attacks positions that no one would defend."
- Stanley Fish, "Professor Sokal's Bad Joke", NYT 21 May 1996 (read; ELTE reproduction): defends the sociology of science; JFS quote Fish's line that "'socially constructed' is a compliment paid to a fact".
- The David Edge petition and "Umpire" essay (§2 above).

## 4. Sokal and Bricmont on Latour and SSK (1997/1998); Latour's responses

Text read: *Intellectual Impostures* (Profile Books; English 1998; the copy read is the Profile printing "This edition published in 1999, 2003", archive.org user upload). Its printed page numbers are cited; I have not checked them against the 1998 first printing. French original *Impostures intellectuelles* (Odile Jacob, Oct 1997) not read.

**4a. Whom they attack (ch. 4, "Intermezzo: Epistemic relativism in the philosophy of science", pp. 49–96).**
- Scope (p. 51): "we shall examine critically some recent tendencies in the sociology of science (Barnes, Bloor, Latour)". The "relativist Zeitgeist originates partly from contemporary works in the philosophy of science, such as Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions and Paul Feyerabend's Against Method, and partly from extrapolations of these philosophers' work by their successors" (p. 50).
- **Kuhn** (pp. 67–70): they separate "a moderate Kuhn and his immoderate brother" (after Maudlin): "the immoderate Kuhn - who became, perhaps involuntarily, one of the founding fathers of contemporary relativism" (p. 70). On historians (p. 69): "Of course, historians have the perfect right to say that this is not what interests them"; n. 88 accepts the rejection of "Whig history" but rejects "the refusal to use all the information available today ... in order to draw the best possible inferences concerning history". p. 70: "We have no objection of principle to the moderate Kuhn, and we leave to historians the task of investigating the extent to which these ideas are correct in concrete situations." This is a critique of the reception of Kuhn, explicitly sparing historians' practice.
- **Strong programme** (pp. 79–85): "Their aim was to explain in sociological terms the content of scientific theories. Of course, most scientists, when they hear about these ideas, protest and point out the substantial missing piece in this kind of explanation: Nature itself" (p. 79). n. 108 lists "case studies in which scientists and historians explain the concrete mistakes contained in analyses by supporters of the strong programme": Gingras and Schweber (1986), Franklin (1990, 1994), Mermin (1996–97), Gottfried and Wilson (1997), Koertge (1998). On Barnes and Bloor's definition of truth: "What we have here is a radical redefinition of the concept of truth, which nobody (starting with Barnes and Bloor themselves) would accept in practice for ordinary knowledge" (p. 81). Verdict (p. 84): "the 'strong programme' is ambiguous in its intent; and, depending on how one resolves the ambiguity, it becomes either a valid and mildly interesting corrective to the most naive psychological and sociological notions - reminding us that 'true beliefs have causes too' - or else a gross and blatant error." p. 85: methodological relativism "is untenable if one abandons philosophical relativism, because it ignores an essential element of the desired explanation, namely Nature itself."
- **Latour, *Science in Action*** (pp. 85–90): "The strong programme in the sociology of science has found an echo in France, particularly around Bruno Latour. His works contain a great number of propositions formulated so ambiguously that they can hardly be taken literally" (p. 85). On the Third Rule of Method: "Note how Latour slips, without comment or argument, from 'Nature's representation' in the first half of this sentence to 'Nature' tout court in the second half" (p. 85). Their remedy (p. 90): "sociologists of science ought not to study scientific controversies on which they lack the competence to make an independent assessment of the facts ... But it goes without saying that Latour would not enjoy this conclusion." n. 123 (pp. 88–89) attacks Latour's *La Recherche* (1998) piece on Ramses II and tuberculosis: "the article oscillates between extreme banalities and blatant falsehoods."
- **Latour on relativity** (ch. 6, pp. 115–123): Latour's 1988 SSS article "illustrates perfectly the problems encountered by a sociologist who aims to analyse the content of a scientific theory he does not understand very well" (p. 115); "Latour confuses the pedagogy of relativity with the 'technical content' of the theory itself" (p. 120); "Latour's analysis is fatally flawed by his inadequate understanding of the theory Einstein is trying to explain" (p. 121). Postscript (pp. 121–123) answers Mermin's partial defence of Latour (*Physics Today*, Oct 1997, p. 13).
- They cite G&L for support (p. 86 n. 120: "the 'homely example' in Gross and Levitt (1994, pp. 57-8)").

**4b. Latour's responses (in-period).**
- *Le Monde*, 18 Jan 1997 (not read; quoted in §3e via JFS and Sokal). Hostile: "a small number of theoretical physicists deprived of the fat budgets of the cold war".
- LSE debate with Sokal, 2 July 1998. Known only through Steve Fuller's report, "Whose Style? Whose Substance? Sokal vs. Latour at the LSE", *Technoscience* (4S newsletter) 11.3 (Fall 1998) (web reproduction, read): "on this particular evening, Latour supported the former [constructivism] but opposed the latter [relativism]"; "Latour won 'on points' -- his defense was subtler and his critique more incisive."
- *Pandora's Hope* (1999), ch. 1, "Do You Believe in Reality? News from the Trenches of the Science Wars". **Read in a retyped web copy (12 pp., no book pagination; provenance unclear); cited by section.** Latour presents science studies as attacked from both sides: "in this guerrilla warfare being conducted in the no-man's-land between the 'two cultures,' we were the ones being attacked by militants, activists, sociologists, philosophers, and technophobes of all hues, precisely because of our interest in the inner workings of scientific facts." "That we are studying a subject matter does not mean that we are attacking it. Are biologists anti-life, astronomers anti-stars, immunologists anti-antibodies?" "If science studies has achieved anything, I thought, surely it has added reality to science, not withdrawn any from it." "It is quite simple, even though it may sound incredible in these times of the science wars: we are not at war." He repudiates "social construction" as the label: "far from being the ones who have limited science to 'mere social construction' ... we in science studies may be the first to have found a way to free the sciences from politics—the politics of reason". The physicist side: "a few disgruntled cold-war physicists still wishing to help Socrates shut the mouths of the 'ten thousand fools'"; "The opposite of relativism, we should never forget, is called absolutism (Bloor [1976] 1991)."
- Note the asymmetry for the atlas: Latour's 1999 reply to the physicists cites Bloor approvingly the same year he publishes "For David Bloor… and Beyond" against him (see `evidence-ssk-latour.md`).

## 5. *The Golem* (Collins and Pinch 1993) and Mermin's *Physics Today* exchange (1996–1999)

Texts read: Mermin, "What's Wrong with this Sustaining Myth?", *Physics Today* 49.3 (Mar 1996) 11, 13; Mermin, "The Golemization of Relativity", *PT* 49.4 (Apr 1996) 11ff. (opening read); Collins and Pinch, letter "Sociologists, Scientist Continue Debate about Scientific Process", with "Mermin replies", *PT* 49.7 (Jul 1996) 11; Mermin, "What's Wrong with This Reading", *PT* 50.10 (Oct 1997) 11, 13 (AIP scans). *The Golem* itself not read (lending only).

- **Mermin → Collins and Pinch (SSK).** Mar 1996, p. 11: *The Golem* "is a central text in a growing controversy between scientists and those who study science"; "though there are many fascinating tales about science in The Golem, Collins and Pinch infer from these studies a seriously deficient picture of the scientific enterprise." He quotes *Golem* conclusions, e.g. "The truth about the natural world [is] what the powerful believe to be the truth about the natural world" and "Science works the way it does not because of any absolute constraint from Nature, but because we make our science the way that we do." His test: "The pertinent issue in assessing the claims of The Golem is not whether scientific truth is determined by constraints from nature or by social construction, but whether Collins and Pinch strike a satisfactory balance between these two aspects of the process. I believe their book furnishes an instructive demonstration of what can go wrong if you focus too strongly on the social perspective." p. 13: "their own sustaining myth of the social construction of scientific truth has lulled them into finishing their case studies with an incomplete story".
- **Collins and Pinch reply** (Jul 1996, p. 11): "Many scientists, especially those who have become spokespersons, are evidently scientific fundamentalists. ... Where we sociologists think of ourselves as trying to gain social and historical insight into science, the fundamentalists see heresy." "The Golem—which is about as inoffensive a social analysis of science as one could wish for—seems to have engendered some responses more appropriate to the appearance of a heretical tract." They lean on historians: "Our chapter was based on only one or two of those many works"; "there is good historical evidence that relativity, including special relativity, was resisted by many scientists across the world for about two decades (see, for example, Stanley Goldberg, Understanding Relativity ... 1984)"; "the history of science is difficult to reconcile with scientific fundamentalism unless a body of perfectly respectable interpretations are censored."
- **Mermin replies** (same page): "They think I used the example of relativity because I was offended by their defilement of a sacred text. I think I did it because I understand relativity better than the other subjects they discussed ... They overlook this obvious explanation because the rightness/wrongness axis is not a relevant dimension in their kind of sociological analysis of science." "So why should facts be irrelevant to the sociological study of the practice of science?"
- **Mermin defends Latour against the physicists** (Oct 1997, p. 11): "Fronts are opening in the science wars on which some scientists are misrepresenting and oversimplying as egregiously as those at whom they direct their fire"; on Latour's relativity essay, "I believe such attacks miss the point of Latour's essay." He also notes "Many distinguished British critics of science find him a far-from-easy read, and they have fired more accurate salvos in his direction" (i.e. the SSK critics in `evidence-ssk-latour.md`). He glosses "critics" as "the full array of practitioners from sociology, anthropology, history, literature and cultural studies".
- Character of the exchange: a physicist's **qualified** critique of SSK (balance, not refutation), conducted as a dialogue; Collins and Pinch call Mermin "our friend and colleague". It continued in Labinger and Collins (eds), *The One Culture?* (Chicago 2001) — **later reading, not read** (archive.org lending only; Sokal's chapter "Science and Sociology of Science: Beyond War and Peace" is on his site, not read here).

## 6. The anti-constructivist anthology: Koertge (ed.), *A House Built on Sand: Exposing Postmodernist Myths about Science* (OUP, 1998)

**Volume not read.** Contents and chapter abstracts from Oxford Scholarship Online chapter records (DOIs 10.1093/0195117255.003.0001–0019) via OpenAlex. **These abstracts are publisher summaries, probably written for the online edition, not the authors' text.** They establish who targeted whom; quotations below are from the abstracts only.

- Koertge, introduction (003.0001): "The interdisciplinary enterprise of Science, Technology and Society Studies (STS) fosters the view that the results of scientific inquiry are social constructions ... This anthology, with essays by philosophers, historians, scientists, and engineers, scrutinizes these claims in detail."

## 7. Did historians intervene? (priority 5)

### 7a. Historians of science, and physicist-historians

- **M. Norton Wise**, "The Enemy Without and the Enemy Within", *Isis* 87.2 (1996) 323–327 (review essay on *Higher Superstition*; byline confirmed from Crossref metadata in the repo's history-journals harvest). **Not read.** Edge (1997, n. 4) reports Wise "accuses Gross & Levitt of 'a willful strategy of distortion and demonization' (323)". This is the History of Science Society's journal answering the book.
- **Frederick Gregory**, "Embracing Polarization", *Isis* 88.2 (1997) 312–315 (review of Gross, Levitt and Lewis eds., *The Flight from Science and Reason*, 1996). **Not read**; byline from Crossref.
- **Nick Jardine and Marina Frasca-Spada**, "Splendours and Miseries of the Science Wars", editorial, *SHPS* 28.2 (1997) 219–235 (read, web reproduction, no internal pages). Cambridge HPS. They declare themselves "bound to sit on the fence, suspicious both of glib pronouncements about the social construction or gendered constitution of scientific truth and reality, and of uncritical claims for the power of the scientific method to yield theories which mirror the world-in-itself." Even-handed criticism of both sides:
  - of Gross and Levitt, Sokal and Weinberg: "one may doubt the efficacy of Sokal's short way with social constructivism"; "Nor are Gross and Levitt safe in supposing such views to be the consensus of scientists"; on G&L and the cell-biology study: "the distinction appears unworkable in a field as pervaded by gender-laden language as reproductive cytology"; the warriors' "language of the purification rituals" (after Mary Douglas).
  - of science studies: "many historians and sociologists have, it seems, been lured into ontological relativism by the attractions of methodological relativism"; "considerations of historical method appear to count against certain forms of relativism about truth ... the evaluation of testimony, on which inquiry in the history of the sciences ultimately rests, often involves accounting for scientific beliefs in terms of their truth or falsity"; on Collins's experimenter's regress: "here at last we find a genuine challenge to realist views of scientific objectivity and progress"; on Latour's tactics: "provocation and satire ... much in evidence in Latour's writings ... are liable to be mistaken by scientists for subversion and flippancy". Harding is defended: her remarks are "open to interpretation not as espousals of relativism ... but as claims about the effectiveness of power and prejudice in defining the agendas and applications of science".
  - Historiographic point: "In the 1950s and 60s -- the 'golden age' of Popper, Conant, Kuhn, Feyerabend, Bronowski, Merton -- the emerging fields of science studies were in constant dialogue with the sciences. Then, with the consolidation of science studies as university disciplines in the 70s and 80s, the dialogue with scientists faded away."
- **Steven Shapin**, "Rarely Pure and Never Simple: Talking about Truth", *Configurations* 7 (1999) 1–14 (read; Harvard DASH scan, my OCR; printed pages). The fullest historian-of-science reply. pp. 3–4: "For sociologists and historians a proper (innocuous and constructive) maxim of method is to 'treat knowledge as what counts as knowledge' ... Relativism—as it is widely embraced in my part of the academic world—is just an aspect of this maxim of method. It implies nothing at all about epistemological evaluations or ontological judgments." n. 6 (p. 4): "Crystal-clear, but routinely misrepresented, statements of methodological relativism are ... Barry Barnes and David Bloor ... and David Bloor, Knowledge and Social Imagery". p. 11: "We are now depressingly familiar with charges that (1) sensibilities such as those represented by the mundaneness postulate are motivated by hostility to science or ignorance of it, or that (2) however motivated, they will nevertheless corrupt the youth of Athens. The first accusation—when directed to the work of many distinguished historians and sociologists of science—now deserves to be called by its proper name: it is malevolently misinformed." p. 11: "Unconvinced of the truth of the laws of physics, they will jump off tall buildings" (Sokal's window); "Such sentiments are as ludicrous as they are prevalent among the academy's newly self-appointed Tribunes of Truth." p. 12: "Were science studies academics hostile to science and intent on its destruction, they would not find the job quite so easy"; "a massively uninformed imputation about how expert claims about the world are credited or rejected in lay society". Shapin writes as "a historian and a sociologist of science" (p. 1) and defends SSK methodological relativism; he names none of his opponents in the pages read (the target is the Sokal/Weinberg/G&L line, identified by their arguments).
- **John Krige** (director, CRHST, Cité des Sciences, Paris), "Cannon-fodder for the science wars", *Physics World*, Dec 1998, pp. 49–50 (read; copy on Sokal's site): *Intellectual Impostures* "is essentially a political tract"; "Being of that genre, it is inevitably and lamentably indifferent to the history and philosophy of science, and to the evolution in the social and historical studies of science itself"; "Those of us working in the humanities and social sciences ... should not be so quick to trust people in the 'hard' sciences, notably physicists ... Which is, of course, just what researchers in the history and social studies of science have been insisting on for the last two decades." (Sokal and Bricmont replied, *Physics World*, Feb 1999; not read.)
- **Mara Beller** (historian and philosopher of physics, Hebrew University), "The Sokal Hoax: At Whom Are We Laughing?", *Physics Today* 51.9 (Sept 1998) 29–34 (read; AIP scan and web copy). Turns the hoax on the founders of quantum mechanics: "The philosophical pronouncements of Bohr, Born, Heisenberg and Pauli deserve some of the blame for the excesses of the postmodernist critique of science." "Astonishing statements, hardly distinguishable from those satirized by Sokal, abound in the writings of Bohr, Heisenberg, Pauli, Born and Jordan." On G&L: they "ridicule Aronowitz when he 'naively echoes...the view that the causal and deterministic view of things implicit in classical physics has been irrevocably banished'", but "Aronowitz had been relying on the assertions of the inevitable and final overthrow of determinism, endlessly repeated by the most honored heroes of 20th-century physics."
- **Silvan Schweber** (physics and history of ideas, Brandeis), "Reflections on the Sokal Affair: What Is at Stake?", *Physics Today* 50.3 (Mar 1997) 73ff. (read, opening): places the affair in "the post-cold-war marginalization of physicists"; "I find my response to Sokal ambiguous".
- **Allan Franklin** (physicist and historian of experiment): chapters against Collins and Pickering in Koertge 1998 (§6; abstracts only); also "How to Avoid the Experimenter's Regress", *SHPS* 25 (1994) 463–91 (cited by JFS; not read).
- **Margaret C. ### 7b. The `truthdebate` historians

- **Appleby, Hunt and Jacob, *Telling the Truth about History* (1994)** — read in the Norton 2011 e-book upload (archive.org), **which has no print page numbers**; cited by chapter, section and note. The book predates Gross and Levitt and Sokal, but it already criticises SSK and Latour from the historians' side:
  - Ch. 5 ("Discovering the Clay Feet of Science"), section "Truth and the Consequences of Social History": "Back during the War between the Internalists and the Externalists ... a small group of philosophers said that they could provide a 'strong programme'—the phrase, complete with British spelling, became a battle cry—for showing the socially determined or constructed nature of all scientific inquiry [n. 24: Barnes and Bloor, 'Relativism, Rationalism and the Sociology of Knowledge']. Although they caused a stir ... the social constructionists could not explain the way in which technical problems, or new natural phenomena like viruses, set whole research agendas." "The social insight ... does not, however, underwrite the more extreme claims of strong programmers who have never offered an adequate theory to address the complexity of circumstances and situations that produce reasonably true human knowledge." Section "Relativism Redux": "The strong programme or social constructionist approach offered relativism in response, but most historians and philosophers of science, although accepting the importance of social context and social framing, did not find that relativism could adequately account for the truths found in science." "why not embrace the historian's version of a strong programme and take up philosophical relativism? But if embraced, the resulting relativism fails to address adequately the search for historical truths".
  - Ch. 6 ("Postmodernism and the Crisis of Modernity"): quotes the claim that "'Nothing special,' ... 'nothing extraordinary, in fact nothing of any cognitive quality' occurs in the laboratory" and attributes it in n. 9 to Latour, "Give Me a Laboratory and I Will Raise the World" (1983), p. 161: "Latour calls himself an 'a-modernist,' but his position seems indistinguishable from postmodernism." n. 8 cites Shapin and Schaffer's *Leviathan* (pp. 333–43) as "a more nuanced, yet similar, view" to Foucault's.
  - Ch. 7 ("Truth and Objectivity"): "Some skeptics are social constructionists, once called strong programmers, who see both science and history as intellectual contrivances or discourses, spun out of words ... In the Anglo-American academy, social constructionism surfaced in the history and philosophy of science in the late 1970s." (Bloor and Barnes would contest the "spun out of words" gloss; cf. Edge and Shapin above.)
  - Ch. 8: "It is time to come out of the trenches."
  - Margaret Jacob, the historian of science among the three, then carried this into the Science Wars volumes (1998 against Latour; 1999 "Science Studies after Social Construction").
- **Richard J. Evans.** *In Defence of History* (1997) **not read** (lending only; no search-inside). In his web "Author's response to his critics" (History in Focus, IHR; first version March 1998, revised November 1999; read), part 7, Evans concedes he only touched on Saussure, Derrida and Lyotard, "something that other critics such as Sokal and Tallis have done far more effectively and knowledgeably than I could ever hope to do." This is Evans enlisting Sokal against literary postmodernism; no engagement with SSK, Latour or history of science was found in the nine response pages (keyword search: Latour, Bloor, Kuhn, "science studies", "sociology of science": no hits).

## 11. Judgement (evidence only; editors decide)

**Is a Debate entry "The Science Wars, 1994–2000" supported? Yes, on balance**, and it is the only model that lets the evidence in.

Reasons:
- The debate was named by its participants in period: "the 'Science Wars,' a conflict led by conservatives in science such as Paul Gross and Norman Levitt" (Duke page for *Social Text* 46/47, 1996); used by Mermin (1997), Weinberg (1998), Jardine and Frasca-Spada (1997), Latour (1999) and Hacking (1999, via Croissant).
- The main attackers (Gross, Levitt, Sokal, Bricmont, Weinberg, Mermin, Koertge) have no atlas home. Without a debate entry there is no source node for their critique edges. Roster rows alone would put physicists on `ssk`/`sts` as "critics" with nowhere to draw from. `who_killed_debate` is the precedent: a debate entry hosts the charge and its answerers, with critique edges out (debate → criticised field) and in (answering field → debate).
- Historians did intervene, on both sides: Wise (*Isis* 1996), Gregory (*Isis* 1997), Jardine and Frasca-Spada (*SHPS* 1997), Krige (1998), Beller (1998), Schweber (1997), Shapin (1999) answered the attackers; Jacob (1998), Newman (1998) and Franklin (1998) wrote for the attackers' anthology.
- Conditions for the entry's text: (i) a teaching entry about the reception of science studies, not a historians' quarrel about historical writing; (ii) "the protagonists were mostly scientists and philosophers"; (iii) Canadian History PhDs cite none of the attackers' books (0) but do cite Hacking (6), Appleby–Hunt–Jacob (6) and *The Golem* (3); (iv) the 2000 end-date is soft (Latour 2004 and Labinger–Collins 2001 are later readings).

**Critique edges the evidence supports (new entry `sciencewars`, provisional id):**

1. **`sciencewars` → `ssk` (critique). Strong.**
   - Sokal 1997/98 (read): "The extreme versions of social constructivism and relativism -- such as the Edinburgh 'strong programme' -- are ... largely based on the same failure to distinguish clearly between ontology, epistemology, and the sociology of knowledge."

   - Mermin, *PT* Mar 1996, p. 11, on *The Golem*: "an instructive demonstration of what can go wrong if you focus too strongly on the social perspective".

## 12. Summary (under 250 words)

The Science Wars evidence supports a new Debate entry, "The Science Wars, 1994–2000". Its participants named it in period (*Social Text* 46/47, 1996; Mermin; Weinberg; Jardine and Frasca-Spada; Latour). Its attackers were scientists and philosophers with no atlas home, so only a debate entry can carry their critique edges.

Gross and Levitt (1994) targeted "exponents of science studies", the Edinburgh strong programme and Bloor (pp. 25, 52, quoted by Edge). Their book was not read; it is cited here only through quotations in other writers. Sokal's parody cited Kuhn, Feyerabend, Latour, Bloor, Harding and Haraway. His confession attacked cultural studies. His 1997/98 essay and Sokal–Bricmont (pp. 79–90, 115–123) attacked the strong programme, Latour and the "immoderate Kuhn". Mermin criticised *The Golem* (1996) but later defended Latour (1997).

The replies came from:
- SSK: Collins and Pinch ("scientific fundamentalists"); Edge.
- Latour: Le Monde 1997; *Pandora's Hope*.
- Historians of science: Wise (*Isis*), Shapin ("malevolently misinformed", 1999), Krige, Beller.

Historians also wrote for the attackers: Jacob against Latour (1998), and Franklin and Newman. The `truthdebate` book *Telling the Truth about History* (1994) already criticised the strong programme and assimilated Latour to postmodernism. Evans (1999) cited Sokal approvingly.

Supported edges:
- `sciencewars`→`ssk`/`latour`/`sts`/`kuhn`/`language`
- `ssk`/`latour`/`science`→`sciencewars`
- `truthdebate`→`ssk` and →`latour`
- a `truthdebate`–`sciencewars` comparison

Thinner: →`science`, →`gender`. Not supported: Hacking or Kitcher as critics, or a `truthdebate`→`sciencewars` critique.
