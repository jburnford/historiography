## 1. *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge* (1970)

These are the proceedings of the 13 July 1965 Bedford College symposium. The preface calls the volume "a rational reconstruction and expansion rather than a faithful report of the actual discussion" (p. vii, read).

### 1a. John W. N. Watkins, "Against 'Normal Science'" (pp. 25–37), LSE

**Objection.**
- Kuhn's normal science is a closed society. Watkins recalls suggesting to Kuhn in 1961 that he set "his view of the scientific community as an essentially closed society, intermittently shaken by collective nervous breakdowns followed by restored mental unison" against "Popper's view that the scientific community ought to be, and to a considerable degree actually is, an open society" (p. 26).
- "What is genuinely scientific for Kuhn is hardly science for Popper, and what is genuinely scientific for Popper is hardly science for Kuhn" (p. 29).
- The analogy with religion: "My suggestion is, then, that Kuhn sees the scientific community on the analogy of a religious community and sees science as the scientist's religion" (p. 33).
- The historical counter-argument, which he calls the "Instant-Paradigm thesis", rejects Kuhn's account (pp. 35–36). His conclusion is that "the scientific community is not, after all, a closed society whose chief characteristic is 'the abandonment of critical discourse'" (p. 37).
- On p. 34 n. 1 he credits "A similar point has been made independently by Dudley Shapere: cf. his [1964], p. 387." This is second-hand evidence for Shapere's matter-theory objection.
- Watkins admits "a certain conscious unfairness, or at least one-sidedness" (p. 26). He says normal science is "of considerable sociological importance" and might be a sociologist's "ideal type" (p. 27). That makes it a methodological critique, not a denial that normal science exists.

**Reply (Kuhn).**
- Kuhn calls the exchange "mutually impenetrable views" (p. 231) and promises "to unravel Watkins's extraordinary distortion of my position" (p. 236).
- On the religion charge, Masterman also replies (p. 61 n. 1). She calls Watkins's text "a really very gross distortion of Kuhn's real view".

**Atlas home:** none. A Popperian philosopher of science, not on any roster.

### 1b. Stephen Toulmin, "Does the Distinction between Normal and Revolutionary Science Hold Water?" (pp. 39–47)

**Objection.**
- Rhetorical exaggeration: "To say 'all normal science rests on a foundation of dogma' was like saying 'we are all mad really'" (p. 39).
- On Collingwood, which is a philhistory-relevant link: "The intellectual function of Kuhn's 'paradigms' is precisely that of Collingwood's 'absolute presuppositions'" (p. 40, citing *Essay on Metaphysics*, 1940).
- Political historians have outgrown "revolution" as an explanation (p. 41).
- Kuhn's earlier account "went too far by implying the existence of discontinuities in scientific theory far more profound and far less explicable than any which ever in fact occur" (p. 41).
- Physicists who lived through 1890–1930 "appeared curiously unaware" of total incomprehension (p. 44).
- Kuhn's retreat to "micro-revolutions" means "the very basis for distinguishing between 'normal' and 'revolutionary' change in science which was the very heart and core of Kuhn's theory, collapses" (p. 45).
- He offers an evolutionary variation-and-selection alternative. He claims it can "resolve some of the old perplexities about the relationship between external and internal factors" (pp. 46–47). This is in-period evidence of the internal/external debate being brought to bear on Kuhn.
- He ends: "the idea of 'scientific revolution' will have to follow that of 'political revolutions' out of the category of explanatory concepts and into that of mere descriptive labels" (p. 47).

**Reply (Kuhn).**
- "the retreat from a concern with macro- to a concentration on micro-revolutions is not among them [the changes in his position]" (p. 249).
- Toulmin dates the 1961 paper before the book; Kuhn says the paper "was, however, both written and published after the book" (p. 249). Note 2 on p. 249 calls Toulmin's 1967 claim "this biographical canard".
- He accepts the geological analogy but turns it: "in science as in geology there are two sorts of change" (p. 250).
- "partial communication was never, as Toulmin would have it, 'complete [mutual] incomprehension'" (p. 250).
- He concedes "The fundamental challenge of his paper, however, remains" (p. 251) and would now begin from "the community structure of science" (p. 252).

**Atlas home:** none. Toulmin is a philosopher and historian of scientific ideas, not on any roster. `science` is the nearest; `philhistory` only via his Collingwood comparison.

### 1c. L. Pearce Williams, "Normal Science, Scientific Revolutions and the History of Science" (pp. 49–50), Cornell historian of science

This is **the one historian-of-science critique in the volume.** It is aimed at Kuhn and Popper alike.
- "Both Kuhn and Popper really base their views of the structure of science on the history of science and the main point of my remarks here is that the history of science cannot bear such a load at this time. We simply do not know enough to permit a philosophical structure to be erected on a historical foundation" (p. 50).
- On Faraday's manuscripts: "How many 'normal' scientists (as defined by their published papers) are really revolutionaries at heart?" (p. 50).
- On sociology: "this 'may' be done, but … it has not yet been done" (p. 49).
- He dismisses Masterman's point that new fields grasp the paradigm: "the figure of the drowning man and the straw is a familiar one" (p. 49).
- "As a historian, then, I must view both Popper and Kuhn with a somewhat jaundiced eye … neither has amassed sufficient hard evidence … I shall continue to use both as guides to my researches" (p. 50).

This is critique plus continued use: a qualified appropriation.

**Replies.**
- Kuhn: "There is no area in which I am more deeply conscious of the need for additional historical research, though I dissent from the conclusions Pearce Williams draws from that need" (pp. 251–252).
- Popper finds turning "to the history of science" for enlightenment "surprising and disappointing" (p. 57).

**Atlas home:** `science` (historian of science). He is not on the roster.

### 1d. Karl Popper, "Normal Science and its Dangers" (pp. 51–58)

The atlas has already checked this text. The quotations below were read for this file.
- "Kuhn's criticism of my views about science is the most interesting one I have so far come across" (p. 51).
- "In my view the 'normal' scientist, as Kuhn describes him, is a person one ought to be sorry for" (p. 52).
- The normal scientist "has been taught in a dogmatic spirit: he is a victim of indoctrination" (p. 53).
- "I see a very great danger in it … a danger to science and, indeed, to our civilization" (p. 53).
- "I disagree with Kuhn both about some historical facts, and about what is characteristic for science" (p. 54).
- "Kuhn's logic is the logic of historical relativism" (p. 55).
- On relativism: "This is a widely accepted and indeed a fashionable thesis: the thesis of relativism … I regard the thesis as mistaken" (p. 56). He calls it "The Myth of the Framework" and says it "is, in our time, the central bulwark of irrationalism" (p. 56).
- "It would thus be simply false to say that the transition from Newton's theory of gravity to Einstein's is an irrational leap" (p. 57).
- The historiography link, relevant to the person-entry question: turning to sociology or psychology "is disappointing because it shows that all I have said before against sociologistic and psychologistic tendencies and ways, especially in history, was in vain" (p. 58).
- Qualified acceptance: "'Normal' science, in Kuhn's sense, exists" (p. 52). He feels "indebtedness to Kuhn for pointing out the distinction" (p. 52).

**Reply (Kuhn).**
- Kuhn rejects the claim that our being prisoners of a framework is merely "Pickwickian" (p. 232).
- "I am dumbfounded by Sir Karl's attempt to convict me of self-contradiction because I employ logical arguments myself" (p. 261).
- On method: "On these points I am an unrepentant Popperian" (p. 236).
- "Perhaps science could again become like philosophy, as Sir Karl wishes, but I suspect that he would then admire it less" (p. 254).

**Atlas home:** `science` (already on the roster as critic). See §6 for philhistory.

### 1e. Margaret Masterman, "The Nature of a Paradigm" (pp. 59–89; pp. 59–66 read), Cambridge Language Research Unit

This is a **friendly amendment, not a hostile critique.**
- She writes "on the assumption that T. S. Kuhn is one of the outstanding philosophers of science of our time" (p. 59).
- She defends normal science as "the crashingly obvious fact" and dismisses "Popper and Feyerabend pontificating at them like eighteenth-century divines" (p. 60).
- Her objection: "On my counting, he uses 'paradigm' in not less than twenty-one different senses in his [1962], possibly more, not less" (p. 61). She lists them on pp. 61–65 and groups them as metaphysical, sociological and artefact or construct paradigms (p. 65).
- She disagrees with Kuhn on one point: "his conception of verification in experience (or the absence of it), I do not agree with him and on this it seems to me that the philosophical empiricist world has indeed a case against him" (p. 61).
- She complains that Kuhn's critics "have never taken the trouble to find out what it is" (p. 61).

**Reply (Kuhn).**
- "I concur in her judgement that the term 'paradigm' points to the central philosophical aspect of my book but that its treatment there is badly confused … her paper has helped in that development" (p. 234).
- In the Postscript he credits "One sympathetic reader … concluded that the term is used in at least twenty-two different ways" (p. 181, note 7: "Masterman, op. cit."). This is twenty-two in Kuhn's recollection against "not less than twenty-one" in her text.
- He splits paradigm into "the entire constellation of beliefs, values, techniques" versus "the concrete puzzle-solutions" (p. 175).
- Note 4 on p. 174 names Masterman and Shapere 1964 as "particularly cogent criticism of my initial presentation of paradigms".

**Atlas home:** none (philosopher and computational linguist).

### 1f. Imre Lakatos, "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" (pp. 91–196; pp. 92–93 and 177–180 read)

- Background: Kuhn's "intellectual debt is to Koyré rather than to Popper … But, unfortunately, his message had some authoritarian and irrationalist overtones" (p. 92 n. 3). This links to Koyré on the `science` roster. On p. 92 n. 2, "The main contemporary protagonists of the ideal of 'truth by consensus' are Polanyi and Kuhn."
- The charge: "Kuhn … seems now to fall back on irrationalism" (p. 93).
- "For Kuhn scientific change … is a mystical conversion which is not and cannot be governed by rules of reason … Scientific change is a kind of religious change" (p. 93).
- On truth and power: "truth lies in power. Thus Kuhn's position would vindicate, no doubt, unintentionally, the basic political credo of contemporary religious maniacs ('student revolutionaries')" (p. 93).
- The most-cited phrase, italicised in the original: "Thus in Kuhn's view scientific revolution is irrational, a matter for mob psychology" (p. 178).
- Partial absorption: "Kuhn is right in objecting to naive falsificationism … But Kuhn is wrong in thinking that by discarding naive falsificationism he has discarded thereby all brands of falsificationism" (p. 177).
- He describes his research programme as "an objective, 'third world' reconstruction of Kuhn's socio-psychological concept of 'paradigm'" (p. 179 n. 1).
- "Kuhn certainly showed that the psychology of science can reveal important and, indeed, sad truths" (p. 179).

This is critique combined with appropriation.

**Reply (Kuhn).**
- "When Lakatos writes, 'But Kuhn's conceptual framework … is socio-psychological: mine is normative', I can only think that he is employing a sleight of hand to reserve the philosophical mantle for himself" (p. 233).
- "In all but a few respects, only two of them essential, his position is now very close to my own" (p. 238).
- Kuhn asks whether anything "suggest[s] the appropriateness of phrases like decision by 'mob psychology'? I think not" (pp. 262–263).
- On Lakatos's history-writing, a **historian's counter-critique**: "a historian would not include in his narrative a factual report which he knew to be false … Both groups are scrupulous, but they differ in what they are scrupulous about" (p. 256 n. 1).

**Atlas home:** `science` (already a contributor on the roster and in the `normal_science_debates` strand).

### 1g. Paul Feyerabend, "Consolations for the Specialist" (pp. 197–230)

This is critique combined with partial agreement.
- He would not "accept the general ideology which I thought formed the background of his thinking. This ideology … could only give comfort to the most narrowminded and the most conceited kind of specialism" (p. 197).
- On ambiguity: "are we here presented with methodological prescriptions … or are we given a description, void of any evaluative element" (p. 198). "I venture to guess that the ambiguity is intended and that Kuhn wants to fully exploit its propagandistic potentialities" (p. 199).
- On organised crime: "Every statement which Kuhn makes about normal science remains true when we replace 'normal science' by 'organized crime'" (p. 200).
- He suspects "that normal or 'mature' science, as described by Kuhn, is not even a historical fact" (p. 207).
- Agreement on incommensurability: "a point of Kuhn's philosophy which I wholeheartedly accept" (p. 219). "I now want to defend Kuhn against Lakatos" (p. 214, keyword hit, read in context).

**Reply (Kuhn).**
- "The answer, of course, is that they should be read in both ways at once" (p. 237).
- Kuhn rejects "irrationality, relativism, and the defence of mob rule … even when they are used in my defence by Feyerabend" (p. 234).
- To call Feyerabend's argument "a defence of irrationality in science seems to me not only absurd but vaguely obscene" (p. 264).
- The Postscript repeats the is/ought reply and cites Feyerabend as the example (p. 207, n. 19).

**Atlas home:** none.

### 1h. Kuhn's general reply: "Reflections on my Critics" (pp. 231–278)

- On the two Kuhns: "I am tempted to posit the existence of two Thomas Kuhns … Kuhn2 is the author of another book with the same title" (p. 231).
- On the charges: "My critics respond to my views on this subject with charges of irrationality, relativism, and the defence of mob rule. These are all labels which I categorically reject" (p. 234).
- **Who raised the charges:** "The sorts of misinterpretations just outlined are voiced only by philosophers" (p. 260). This is Kuhn's own testimony that the irrationalism and relativism critique came from philosophers, not historians.
- Values, not rules: "accuracy, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness, and the like … such reasons constitute values to be used in making choices rather than rules of choice" (pp. 261–262).
- In the notes on p. 261 he names his targets. Note 2: Shapere 1966, p. 67, "the decisions of a scientific group to adopt a new paradigm cannot be based on good reasons of any kind, factual or otherwise". Note 3: Scheffler 1967, p. 18, "adoption of a new scientific theory is an intuitive or mystical affair, a matter for psychological description rather than logical or methodological codification".
- On relativism: "In one sense of the term I may be a relativist; in a more essential one I am not … scientific development is, like biological evolution, unidirectional and irreversible" (p. 264).
- On historians of science (p. 252): "Historians of science who engage in them must cease to rely exclusively on the techniques of the intellectual historian and use those of the social and cultural historian as well". This is Kuhn's own internal/external move.

## 4. Kuhn's later replies on irrationality and objectivity

- **Postscript 1969** (read):
  - Paradigm in "two different senses" (p. 175).
  - "I am occasionally accused of glorifying subjectivity and even irrationality" (p. 186).
  - "Nothing about that relatively familiar thesis implies either that there are no good reasons for being persuaded … accuracy, simplicity, fruitfulness, and the like. What it should suggest, however, is that such reasons function as values" (p. 199).
  - "There is no neutral algorithm for theory-choice" (p. 200).
  - "§6 Revolutions and Relativism": "That is not a relativist's position, and it displays the sense in which I am a convinced believer in scientific progress" (p. 206). He then adds: "if the position be relativism, I cannot see that the relativist loses anything needed to account for the nature and development of the sciences" (p. 207).
  - "Historians of literature, of music, of the arts, of political development … have long described their subjects in the same way" (p. 208). This is Kuhn's own account of where historians' periodisation comes from.
- **"Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice"** (1973 lecture; *Essential Tension* 1977, 320–39; read in the Cover–Curd reprint, **reprint pagination**):
  - "A number of philosophers have greeted remarks like these in a way that continues to surprise me. My views, it is said, make of theory choice 'a matter for mob psychology'" (p. 102). Note 3 identifies this as Lakatos, CGK p. 178.
  - "Reports of this sort manifest total misunderstanding" (p. 103).
  - The five values are "accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness" (p. 103).
  - "every individual choice between competing theories depends on a mixture of objective and subjective factors" (p. 106).
  - His critics appeal to "the well-known distinction between the contexts of discovery and of justification" (p. 107).

## 6. Popper as a possible person entry (evidence only; no decision)

This section is relevant to the principle that person entries go to big-idea thinkers whom students meet across fields.

- **Historiographical texts (read):**
  - *Poverty of Historicism*: "I mean by 'historicism' an approach to the social sciences which assumes that historical prediction is their principal aim, and which assumes that this aim is attainable by discovering the 'rhythms' or the 'patterns', the 'laws' or the 'trends' that underlie the evolution of history" (p. 3).
  - The dedication is to victims of "the fascist and communist belief in Inexorable Laws of Historical Destiny" (p. v).
  - His §30 is a covering-law account of historical explanation. It says history is "characterized by its interest in actual, singular, or specific events" (p. 143). It explicitly engages Morton White, "Historical Explanation" (*Mind* 1943), and Hempel (pp. 144–145). Both are on the `philhistory` roster.
  - On the objectivity of "classical historians", who "adopt points of view without being aware of them" (pp. 151–152).
- **In-period historian's response (read):** Carr, *What Is History?* (1961; Penguin 1990 pagination):
  - "Professor Popper uses 'historicism' as a catch-all for any opinion about history which he dislikes" (p. 92 n. 3).

## JUDGEMENT (evidence only; editors decide)

**Supported**

1. **`science` → `kuhn` (critique). Supported, from read in-period texts.**
   - Critics who sit in `science`:
     - Popper, already a `science` critic: "Kuhn's logic is the logic of historical relativism" (CGK p. 55); the Myth of the Framework is "the central bulwark of irrationalism" (p. 56); the normal scientist is "a victim of indoctrination" (p. 53).
     - Lakatos, already a `science` contributor: "in Kuhn's view scientific revolution is irrational, a matter for mob psychology" (p. 178); Kuhn's message "had some authoritarian and irrationalist overtones" (p. 92 n. 3).
     - Pearce Williams, a historian of science not on the roster: "the history of science cannot bear such a load at this time" (p. 50).
   - Kuhn's replies on record: "labels which I categorically reject" (p. 234); good reasons "function as values" (Postscript p. 199).
   - Qualifications:
     - The irrationalism charge is, in Kuhn's words, "voiced only by philosophers" (p. 260). The historians' line (Williams) is evidential scepticism aimed at Popper as much as at Kuhn.
     - Popper's and Lakatos's texts mix acknowledgement and appropriation with critique.
     - The edge would sit beside the existing edge_332 science→kuhn (influence).
     - Watkins, Toulmin, Masterman and Feyerabend have no atlas home. Masterman is a friendly amendment that Kuhn adopted (p. 234; Postscript p. 181).
