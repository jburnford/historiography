## 1. Texts read in his own words

### 1a. "Divergence Displaced," *Comparativ* 26:3 (2016), 71–100. **Read in part**: pp. 71–80 read closely; pp. 81–100 (a critique of Peer Vries, *State, Economy and the Great Divergence*, 2015) skimmed. The German abstract (p. 71) is not quoted.
- p. 72: the essay "offers an assessment of where historians are in understanding what Kenneth Pomeranz famously called the 'great divergence'".
- p. 73: Pomeranz "arrives at his account for the origins of the modern world economy after rejecting numerous putative differences between the early modern Chinese and European economies, some of which had been raised in by previous scholarship, including my China Transformed."
- p. 74, on Brenner: "when my colleague and Marxist historian Robert Brenner famously argues that it is the absence of the agrarian social relations found in Britain that prevented the emergence of capitalism elsewhere, the importance of the system through which he sees the modern world economy emerging cannot be falsified because the enclosures and the social relations that attended them were particular to Britain. Like Pomeranz's coal and colonies, Brenner cites traits that are British and not present elsewhere."
- p. 75: "Neither the scientific revolution nor agrarian social relations lend themselves very easily to counterfactual exercises because they are themselves so large and encompassing."
- p. 76, on his 1997 book: "The much shorter enquiry into economic change in China Transformed followed the same method to highlight demographic similarities, the presence of well-developed long-distance commerce and rural industries in both China and Europe. The cluster of differences stressed in that work concerned European science and technologies, and in particular, following arguments of E. A. Wrigley, the importance of technologies to exploit coal as a source of energy to power new kinds of machines." (The note cites Wrigley, *Continuity, Chance, and Change*.)
- p. 77: "Pomeranz's The Great Divergence, as well as my China Transformed, shared with other explanations mentioned above the basic problem of non-falsifiability."
- p. 77, on *Before and Beyond Divergence* (2011): "Rosenthal and I ended up focusing on an important political difference between China and Europe – political fragmentation in Europe and the presence of an unitary state for the Chinese empire"; war gave "craft producers an incentive to locate in cities"; "We turned the issue of divergence from a question of why was Britain the first industrializer to why was industrialization more likely to occur in Western Europe than in China."
- p. 78: "By making the question of the great divergence a more general one we recognize that even a profoundly transformative process like modern economic growth could take place in several ways and begin at several locations."
- p. 79: "The presence of high wages alone did not trigger the development of technological innovations in cotton textile production, or else we should expect to witness a similar development in the Netherlands".

### 1b. *China Transformed: Historical Change and the Limits of European Experience* (Cornell, 1997) (existing `wong_china_primary`). Chapter openings only (read in an earlier revision); not re-read. Its argument is otherwise given through readers (§2) and his own 2016 summary (§1a).

## 2. Readers

- **Pomeranz** (2000, pp. 7–8; existing, read): a regional approach "anticipated in important ways by my colleague R. Bin Wong"; "I am following a procedure outlined in Wong's recent China Transformed … viewing both sides of the comparison as 'deviations' when seen through the expectations of the other"; "greater stress than Wong does on global conjunctures and reciprocal influences" (n. 13).
- **Mokyr** (2002 draft, pp. 2–4; re-checked): "Wong's argument is stark in its simplicity and yet frustrating in its inability to be resolved"; he quotes Wong (1997, p. 31): the "supply-driven Industrial Revolution that followed the demand-led Industrious Revolution in Europe did not happen in China"; and (p. 41): "Europeans … escaped the damaging dynamics of rural industrialization in the nineteenth century. Not so the Chinese." Objection: "Bin Wong does not cite any book by Needham" (pp. 3–4). Agreement: "as Wong argues, Smithian growth would not have brought about the European Miracle" (p. 4).
- **Huang** (2002, p. 520; existing, read in 1.144): Pomeranz's use of the industrious revolution was "along the same lines as R. Bin Wong's brief discussion earlier (1997, 30–31)".
- **Parthasarathi** (2011, p. 5; existing publisher excerpt): "In his China Transformed, R. Bin Wong argues that early-modern China, as did Europe, experienced Smithian growth … Kenneth Pomeranz builds upon this work".
- **Debin Ma**, EH.Net review of Rosenthal and Wong (2012; existing, read in full): "they stake their claim on differences in political structure, namely the dichotomy between a unified and centralized imperial China versus a politically fragmented Europe"; "This stimulating and striking hypothesis is supported by fairly meager evidence in the chapter".
- **Kelly, Mokyr and Ó Gráda** (2014, p. 16.2; author-posted proof, read): of Allen's induced-innovation argument, "Rosenthal & Wong (2011) adopted it wholesale in their account of the difference between nineteenth-century technological progress in China and Britain."
- **Metadata only:** Pierre-Étienne Will and R. Bin Wong, *Nourish the People: The State Civilian Granary System in China, 1650–1850* (Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Center for Chinese Studies, 1991; Crossref doi:10.3998/mpub.19044; Fulcrum page returned 403). Jean-Laurent Rosenthal and R. Bin Wong, *Before and Beyond Divergence: The Politics of Economic Change in China and Europe* (Harvard UP, 2011; doi:10.4159/harvard.9780674061293).
