## 1. Texts read in his own words

### 1a. E. A. Wrigley and R. S. Schofield, *The Population History of England, 1541–1871: A Reconstruction* (Edward Arnold, 1981; Cambridge University Press paperback, 1989). **Publisher excerpt** of the CUP paperback (ISBN 978-0-521-35688-6): Introduction, pp. 1–5 (page images, OCRed).
- p. 1: "since pre-industrial societies normally experienced great difficulty in achieving a rapid expansion in their capacity to produce goods and services, population was the more dynamic element in the striking of a balance with production."
- p. 2: the parish registers make it "possible for the first time to establish with some precision what changes took place in a pre-industrial population over a period of time long enough to encompass a secular cycle of population development, and to investigate the relationship between demographic and economic change during its course."
- p. 3: monthly totals "taken from the registers of a total of 404 parishes".
- p. 4: "the technique of back projection, which was developed to make the best use of the aggregative data assembled".
- p. 5: "problems peculiar to the data set assembled by the Cambridge Group"; the aggregative tabulations began "as a subsidiary element" of the family-reconstitution project of "the middle 1960s".
- **Edition.** The excerpt is of the 1989 Cambridge paperback of the 1981 book; the event is dated 1981 (first publication), and the 1989 reissue is not a separate event.

### 1b. Wrigley, R. S. Davies, J. E. Oeppen and R. S. Schofield, *English Population History from Family Reconstitution, 1580–1837* (Cambridge, 1997). **Publisher excerpt**, chapter 1, pp. 3–4 (OCRed).
- p. 3: "This book is intended as a complement to the Population history of England. The two books are offspring of the same overall research project". Family reconstitution "had then recently been perfected by Louis Henry"; p. 4: "An initial experiment was made using the register of the parish of Colyton in Devon".

### 1c. *Continuity, Chance and Change* (Cambridge, 1988) (existing `cd_wrigley_ccc_1988`). Publisher excerpt pp. 1–8 read in 1.145; quotations re-used from that reading (evidence-industrialisation.md §1d).
- p. 1: "This book began as the Ellen McArthur lectures delivered in the University of Cambridge in the Lent Term 1987" (his own statement of delivery).
- p. 3: "My main aim in the lectures was to question the appropriateness of the view that the industrial revolution was a cumulative, progressive, unitary phenomenon."
- p. 4: growth "seen as the product of two different sets of forces having only an accidental relationship to one another in the early stages of their overlap in time."
- p. 5: "an economy bounded by the productivity of the land, what I shall term an organic economy".

### 1d. *Poverty, Progress, and Population* (Cambridge, 2004). **Publisher excerpt**, Introduction pp. 1–3 (OCRed).
- p. 1: "Since my days as a research student, I have always been ultimately more preoccupied with the wish to achieve a better understanding of the industrial revolution than with any other issue".
- p. 2: "The 'heroic' view of the industrial revolution, still prominent half a century ago, laid emphasis on a period of marked acceleration in the growth rate in real output per head, the step change which Rostow termed 'take-off' … But this view is now almost impossible to maintain."
- p. 3 n. 6: "those who have reservations about the capacity of national income accounting … remain unimpressed by the new consensus. The counter-view was put vigorously and effectively by Berg and Hudson, 'Rehabilitating the industrial revolution'."

### 1e. *Energy and the English Industrial Revolution* (Cambridge, 2010). **Publisher excerpt** (ISBN 978-0-521-76693-7): Introduction pp. 1–6; chapter 1 pp. 9–11.
- p. 3: the term "industrial revolution" is so embedded "that it is idle to suggest that it should be replaced, even though both the adjective and the noun are somewhat misleading."
- p. 4: "In my view the most important single issue on which to focus in trying to gain a clearer understanding of the industrial revolution is not how the period of more rapid growth began, but why it did not come to an end."
- p. 4: "There can be no single, 'true' account of the industrial revolution."
- pp. 4–5: institutional and scientific explanations get "only glancing references"; "it would be both presumptuous and mildly ridiculous to downplay their importance", but they are "essentially incommensurable".
- p. 9: "All economies which developed in the wake of the neolithic food revolution may be termed organic … Thus the production horizon for all organic economies was set by the annual cycle of plant growth."
- p. 2: fossil fuels have "vastly increased the power to produce … but it is accompanied by environmental hazards which threaten disastrous consequences."

## 2. Readers

- **Berg and Hudson** (1990 working paper, existing `cd_berg_hudson_1990`): "The work of Wrigley and Schofield dominates the population history of this period and illustrates some of the more general problems of causal analysis based on aggregative studies."
- **Mokyr** (2002 draft, p. 2; re-checked): Pomeranz's coal argument "joins a venerable tradition established by Carlo Cipolla, E.A. Wrigley and others that looks at the Industrial Revolution as primarily a revolution of fossil fuel usage."
- **Huang** (*JAS* 61:2, 2002, pp. 532–533; existing `huang_pomeranz_review`, read in 1.144): Wrigley (1988) is "a very different thing from arguing that chance, or coal, alone mattered"; Pomeranz has turned "Wrigley's sophisticated argument that chance figured in England's industrialization into a simple argument that coal and colonies alone mattered."
- **Clark and Jacks** (2007, abstract, existing): "both E. A. Wrigley and Kenneth Pomeranz have made coal central to the story".
- **Wong** (2016, *Comparativ* 26:3, p. 76; read, see evidence-wong.md §1a): *China Transformed* stressed "European science and technologies, and in particular, following arguments of E. A. Wrigley, the importance of technologies to exploit coal as a source of energy to power new kinds of machines" (n. cites *Continuity, Chance, and Change*).
- **de Vries** (2008, p. 8; publisher excerpt): "The pioneering work of 'The Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure' established the view that the nuclear family structures of modern western European countries … are not a product of industrialization but have much earlier origins." (Credited to the Group, not to Wrigley by name.)
- **Economic History Society** obituary (existing `wrigley_economic_ehs`): institutional account; used only for the 2022 retrospective point.
