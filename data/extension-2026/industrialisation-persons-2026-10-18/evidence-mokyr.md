## 1. Texts read in his own words

### 1a. "Editor's Introduction: The New Economic History and the Industrial Revolution," in Mokyr (ed.), *The British Industrial Revolution: An Economic Perspective*, 2nd edn (Westview, 1999). **Read in part**, author-posted manuscript (84 pp.; manuscript pagination), pp. 1–2, 14, 16.
- p. 1: "The revision of national income statistics should therefore not, in itself, be enough to abandon the concept." He answers the claim, after Coleman (1983), that the Industrial Revolution is "a concept too many".
- p. 14: "On the whole, a successful macroinvention meets three criteria: novelty, workability, and potential for further improvement."
- p. 16: "A technological definition of the Industrial Revolution is a clustering of macroinventions leading to an acceleration in microinventions."
- n. 1 thanks "Gregory Clark, Stanley Engerman, C. Knick Harley, David Landes and Rick Szostak".
- The pages recorded in the existing source note (pp. 1–3, 12–16) are consistent with this reading.

### 1b. "Demand vs. Supply in the Industrial Revolution," *Journal of Economic History* 37:4 (1977), 981–1008. **Abstract only** (Cambridge Core, publisher page).
- "While demand factors cannot be ruled out altogether, they were definitely of a secondary order of importance. Neither exogenous increases in the demand for manufactures, nor induced technological change are likely to have been a factor of decisive importance."
- de Vries quotes the article's p. 1005 as the supply-side orthodoxy he set out to revise (2008, p. 6 n. 17; see evidence-devries.md §1d).

### 1c. "Useful Knowledge as an Evolving System: The View from Economic History," draft "Preliminary and Incomplete. Revised, February 11, 2002", presented to the Santa Fe conference "The Economy as an Evolving System" (16–18 Nov. 2001). **Read in part**, author-posted, pp. 1–7.
- Title page: "Some of the material in this paper is adapted from my books The Gifts of Athena: Historical Origins of the Knowledge Economy. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2002 and Neither Chance nor Necessity … in preparation."
- p. 2: "the big story is that in the past centuries useful knowledge has become a dominant factor in History."
- p. 5: "The knowledge set is partitioned into two subsets which are distinct. One is propositional or Ω-knowledge which describes and catalogues natural phenomena and the relationships between them. The other is prescriptive or λ-knowledge which contains instructions that can be executed." (The text layer renders Ω and λ as "S" and "8"; the symbols are confirmed by Khan's review, §2a.)
- p. 5 n. 4: the dichotomy "is symmetric to Ryle's (1949 …) distinction between knowledge 'that' and knowledge 'how'". An analogy in a note; not drawn as an edge (see §4).
- p. 7: "Inventions based on narrow epistemic bases have low adaptability and tend to lead to technological stasis fairly soon after their emergence."

### 1d. "The Enduring Riddle of the European Miracle: The Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution," draft "Version of October, 2002" for the Riverside conference of 8–10 Nov. 2002 (existing source `cd_mokyr_riddle_2002`). **Re-read in part** in the author-posted copy, pp. 1–4 and 10 (draft pagination).
- The title page calls it a "Draft (actually, little more than notes)" adapted from *The Gifts of Athena* (2002) and *The Enlightened Economy* (then announced for Penguin, 2004).
- p. 2, on Pomeranz's coal: "Without denigrating the importance of coal or steam, the argument can be and has been overdone." He places this in "a venerable tradition established by Carlo Cipolla, E.A. Wrigley and others".
- p. 3, on ghost acreage: "this argument is not incorrect but by itself cannot do the job". "China's coal reserves are immense."
- pp. 3–4: "Bin Wong does not cite any book by Needham. Pomeranz (2000, p. 64) makes a single reference to him, but does not confront the 'Needham Questions' squarely."
- p. 4: "the sheer enormity of the gap requires a better explanation than historical luck (European discoveries) or geographical accident (coal, proximity to America)"; and "as Wong argues, Smithian growth would not have brought about the European Miracle".
- p. 2, on Wong: "Wong's argument is stark in its simplicity and yet frustrating in its inability to be resolved."
- p. 10: Enlightenment thinkers "created the attitudes, the institutions, and the mechanisms by which new knowledge was created, spread and put to good use. It is this specific aspect of the period that I have called the Industrial Enlightenment. To repeat: the movement was not the key to invention; it was the key to sustained and accelerating invention."
- **Dating.** An October 2002 draft for a November conference. It is not evidence that the paper was delivered.

### 1e. *The European Enlightenment, the Industrial Revolution, and Modern Economic Growth*, Max Weber Lecture No. 2007/07 (European University Institute, 2007; "Version of June 2007"). **Read in part**, author-posted copy of the EUI lecture text, pp. 1–5, 13, 16–17.
- Note *: "Parts of this lecture are based on my forthcoming book, The Enlightened Economy".
- p. 2: most of the countries in the 1914 "convergence club" "were countries that in the eighteenth century were subject to the European Enlightenment. This strong correlation is not in and of itself proof of causality".
- p. 2: "The Enlightenment changed the outlook of key persons on their natural environment, and their inventions and discoveries turned what might have become another technological efflorescence into a sea change in economic history."
- p. 3: before 1750 "all techniques in use were supported by very narrow epistemic bases"; "it was a world of engineering without mechanics, iron-making without metallurgy, farming without soil science".
- p. 5: the tightening interaction of propositional and prescriptive knowledge was "this phenomenon, more than anything else, that prevented the early Industrial Revolution from fizzling out and enabled it to become the taproot of modern growth."
- p. 13: the Royal Institution's charter (1799) "summarizes what the Industrial Enlightenment was about".
- p. 16: "the results of the Industrial Enlightenment in the eighteenth century were few and far between".

### 1f. Morgan Kelly, Joel Mokyr and Cormac Ó Gráda, "Precocious Albion: A New Interpretation of the British Industrial Revolution," *Annual Review of Economics* 6 (2014), 363–389 (existing source `cd_kmo_2014`, abstract only until now). **Read in part**, author-posted proof (pagination 16.1–16.27), pp. 16.1–16.4.
- p. 16.2: "the main reason for its precocity was its higher level of human capital."
- p. 16.2: "Allen (2009a,b; 2010) … has suggested that the higher wages themselves may have been instrumental in bringing about the Industrial Revolution. Allen has resuscitated the idea of induced innovation"; "Rosenthal & Wong (2011) adopted it wholesale".
- p. 16.4: "Many other possible objections to the argument have been raised"; the success of Cornish engineers "suggests that the driver of technological progress was something deeper and stronger than cheap coal and high wages".
- **Use.** The body text names Allen and argues against his high-wage, cheap-energy explanation. The 1.145 strand `why_britain` records that "the abstract does not name Allen" (true of the abstract); a text revision is proposed (batch).

### 1g. Kelly, Mokyr and Ó Gráda, "The Mechanics of the Industrial Revolution," *Journal of Political Economy* 131:1 (2023), 59–94 (existing `cd_kmo_2023`). **Read in part**, published version posted on the author's site, pp. 59–61.
- p. 60: "One influential interpretation holds that the Industrial Revolution can be explained by induced technological innovation, with cheap coal power being substituted for expensive labor (Allen 2009a)."
- p. 61: "The prediction that low-wage areas should be the ones that industrialized runs directly counter to the influential argument of Allen (2009a)".
- The unit of analysis is "the 41 counties of England" (p. 60): the finding concerns differences within England (as the 1.145 strand already says).

### 1h. Mokyr, review of Parthasarathi, *Why Europe Grew Rich and Asia Did Not*, EH.Net (Jan. 2012) (existing `cd_mokyr_parthasarathi_2012`, read in full in 1.145). Not re-read; used only for a reception-side chronology point.

### 1i. Joel Mokyr and Guido Tabellini, "Social Organizations and Political Institutions: Why China and Europe Diverged," *Economica* 91 (2024), 347–382 (published online 27 Dec. 2023; Crossref). **Read in part**, author-posted revised manuscript ("This version: November 2023"), abstract and pp. 1–3.

## 2. Readers (mediated) and recognition

### 2a. B. Zorina Khan, review essay on *The Gifts of Athena* (Princeton, 2002), EH.Net (January 2003). **Read in full.**
- "Useful knowledge comprises two categories: propositional knowledge about natural regularities; and prescriptive knowledge or techniques" (Omega and Lambda).
- Khan quotes the book (p. 16): "The obvious notion that economies are limited in what they can do by their useful knowledge bears some emphasizing simply because so many scholars believe that if incentives and demand are right, somehow technology will follow automatically".
- "when the Industrial Revolution did occur, it was due to what Mokyr calls an 'Industrial Enlightenment.'"
- Khan's reservation: "I have strong doubts about the relevance of the European experience to understanding either the information economy or global technology and culture today."
- She names *The Lever of Riches* (Oxford University Press, 1990) as "a standard reference".
- **Use.** The 2002 book is unread; its argument is given only as Khan reports it, and through the two 2002 drafts that say they adapt it.

### 2b. Claude Diebolt, review of *A Culture of Growth: The Origins of the Modern Economy* (Princeton, 2017 imprint; JSTOR/Crossref record dated 25 Oct. 2016), EH.Net (November 2017). **Read in full.**
- "It tells the story of economic growth with 'culture' — a mushy word for most of us — as the invisible hand."
- "It is not about a mass movement. It is a phenomenon related to an elite: philosophers and scientists of course, but also engineers, instrument makers, and even industrialists".
- Reservation: "I regret the lack of in-depth consideration of the German language literature."

### 2c. C. Knick Harley, review of *The Enlightened Economy* (2009), EH.Net (Dec. 2010) (existing `cd_harley_mokyr_2010`). Read in part in 1.145 (no pages); see `../capitalism-debate-2026-10-18/evidence-industrialisation.md` §1g. "The reader cannot, however, help wondering if causation from Enlightenment, as an intellectual stance, to economic change actually existed. The so-called Industrial Enlightenment seems almost to describe the nature of successful change at least as much as its cause." ### 2d. The 2025 prize. Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, press release, 13 October 2025, nobelprize.org. **Read in full.**
- The Academy awarded the 2025 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel to Mokyr, Aghion and Howitt "for having explained innovation-driven economic growth", "with one half to Joel Mokyr … 'for having identified the prerequisites for sustained growth through technological progress'".
- "He demonstrated that if innovations are to succeed one another in a self-generating process, we not only need to know that something works, but we also need to have scientific explanations for why."
- Popular science background, "From stagnation to sustained growth" (nobelprize.org; **read in part**): Mokyr's "propositional knowledge" and "prescriptive knowledge"; "Mokyr stressed that sustained growth first occurred in Britain because it was home to many skilled artisans and engineers."
- **This replaces** the Northwestern wording used as a fallback in 1.145 ("in recognition of his groundbreaking theory on sustained economic growth"). The prize is a reader's verdict, not evidence for any historical claim.
