## 1. Texts read in his own words

### 1a. *The Economy of Europe in an Age of Crisis, 1600–1750* (Cambridge, 1976). **Cambridge Core chapter opening** (chapter 1, "The age of crisis", summary text, p. 1) and the contents list.
- p. 1: "The subject of this book is the economic life of the five generations of Europeans who lived in the last great epoch before the onset of industrialization."
- p. 1: "many historians have focused their attention on the structure – the immutable features – of economic and social life in this period."
- Contents: "2 The agrarian economies on divergent paths … 6 Capitalism creating its own demand 7 Capital accumulation and the bourgeoisie 8 Mercantilism, absolutism, and economic growth".
- Pomeranz (2000, p. 3 n. 2; existing reading): "A good general critique of the overseas extraction thesis is DeVries 1976".

### 1b. "The Industrial Revolution and the Industrious Revolution," *JEH* 54:2 (1994), 249–270. **Abstract only** (Cambridge Core, read 2026-10-18), plus the article's **reference list** on Cambridge Core (metadata).
- Abstract: "The Industrial Revolution as a historical concept has many shortcomings. A new concept—the 'industrious revolution'—is proposed to place the Industrial Revolution in a broader historical setting. The industrious revolution was a process of household-based resource reallocation that increased both the supply of marketed commodities and labor and the demand for market-supplied goods. The industrious revolution was a household-level change with important demand-side features that preceded the Industrial Revolution, a supply-side phenomenon."
- Reference list (metadata): Crafts (1985), Crafts and Harley (1992), Hajnal (1965), Kriedte, Medick and Schlumbohm (1977/1981), Laslett (1965, 1977), Medick (1976), Mokyr (1976; 1977), Wrigley and Schofield (1981).
- Voth (2009, §2) confirms it was his Economic History Association presidential address.

### 1c. Jan de Vries and Ad van der Woude, *The First Modern Economy: Success, Failure, and Perseverance of the Dutch Economy, 1500–1815* (Cambridge, 1997). **Publisher excerpt**, chapter 1 "By way of introduction", pp. 1–5 (page images, OCRed).
- p. 1: historians including "Eric Hobsbawm, Fernand Braudel … and Immanuel Wallerstein" "felt certain that the Dutch achievement was essentially backward looking, the culmination of something with no future"; they quote Braudel ("The emergence of Amsterdam prolonged the old pattern") and Hobsbawm (against the temptation "to exaggerate the 'modernity' of the Dutch").
- p. 2: they quote North and Thomas: "the Netherlands was the first country to achieve sustained economic growth".
- p. 4: "This book is the product of co-authors — one Dutch, one American — who have set as their goal the establishment of a new synthesis of Dutch economic history … With this work we accept the challenge of the modern historical schools; indeed, we even seek to integrate them." The Annales school "advocated a break with 'event-centered history'"; cliometrics "shifted the focus of the literature from politics and institutions to markets and rational decision makers".
- p. 4: "This book, in short, presents the history of the first modern economy."
- p. 5: "the experience of the Netherlands stands as a challenge to many historical conventions".

### 1d. *The Industrious Revolution: Consumer Behavior and the Household Economy, 1650 to the Present* (Cambridge, 2008) (existing `cd_devries_2008`, pp. 1–5 read in 1.145). **Publisher excerpt, now read pp. 1–10.**
- p. 6: "Studies of modern economic growth are inevitably founded on a decisive 'supply-side' advance"; n. 17 quotes Mokyr (1977, p. 1005): "The determination of 'when,' 'where,' and 'how fast' are to be sought first and foremost in supply, not demand-related processes".
- pp. 6–7: "the accumulating evidence for an earlier increase of per capita income in northwestern Europe paired with a major refinement of material life casts serious doubt on the orthodoxy that the Industrial Revolution was the actual starting point for long-term economic growth. Consequently, I turned my attention increasingly toward a reconsideration of the place of consumer demand in economic development."
- p. 7: "What began as an effort to restore demand as one of the cutting blades of Marshall's supply and demand scissors led me to an even larger – and even less tractable – problem."
- pp. 7–8, four literatures that "guided my thinking": "1. The Revolt of the Early Modernists"; "2. The Revisionist Macroeconomics of the British Industrial Revolution", which "reduces the earlier growth estimates by more than half" (n. 21: Crafts and Harley 1992; Mokyr 2004); "3. The Western European Marriage Pattern. The pioneering work of 'The Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure' established the view that the nuclear family structures of modern western European countries and their offshoots are not a product of industrialization but have much earlier origins" (with Hajnal); "4. The New Household Economics" (Becker).
- p. 9: the approach "does aspire to question the claims of the twin revolutions – the British Industrial and the French political – to function as gatekeepers to modern history."
- p. 9 n. 27: "The term was coined by Akira Hayami to contrast the labor-intensive path of industrial development of Japan with the capital-intensive industrialization of the West. I first encountered the term in conversation with Professor Hayami."
- p. 10: "northwestern Europe and British North America experienced an 'industrious revolution' during a long eighteenth century, roughly 1650–1850, in which a growing number of households acted to reallocate their productive resources (which are chiefly the time of their members) in ways that increased both the supply of market-oriented, money-earning activities and the demand for goods offered in the marketplace."

## 2. Readers

- **Hans-Joachim Voth**, review of *The Industrious Revolution*, EH.Net (May 2009). **Read in full.** "This is an impossible book"; "staggeringly erudite, insightful, stimulating, and on all the main points, convincing"; "The intellectual starting point is emphatically Beckerian"; "It is the striking difference between largely stagnant day wages on the one hand, and rising consumption as reflected in probate inventories on the other, that is one of the best bits of evidence in favor of the 'Industrious Revolution.'" On the breadwinner household: "De Vries begs to differ. Far from a sign of male suppression, the male breadwinner household gave ample power to women" (Voth's summary). Reservation: "the almost complete disconnect with behavioral economics."
- **Elise van Nederveen Meerkerk**, *TSEG* (2009) (existing, read in full): consumption as "een nieuwe 'deus ex machina'" (p. 176); de Vries attends to family power "vooral om machtsverschillen tussen de seksen te bagatelliseren" (p. 177).
- **Leonard Rosenband** (2016, abstract; existing): papermaking raises "fundamental challenges to this model".
- **Kenneth Pomeranz** (2000, p. 17, whose n. 28 cites "DeVries 1994b"; existing `pomeranz_divergence`, read): Chinese family labour "seems to closely resemble the reorientation of labor, leisure, and consumption toward the market that Jan DeVries has called Europe's 'industrious revolution'"; the contents list a section "Family Labor in China and Europe: 'Involution' and the 'Industrious Revolution'"; p. 3 n. 2 on DeVries 1976.
- **Philip Huang** (*JAS* 2002, pp. 520–521; existing `huang_pomeranz_review`, read in 1.144): "it is surprising that Pomeranz would have tried to equate the Yangzi delta with de Vries' industrious revolution … But that is what Pomeranz decided to do (2000, 17), along the same lines as R. Bin Wong's brief discussion earlier (1997, 30–31)"; "he tries to take the revolution out of de Vries' industrious revolution."
- **Jan Luiten van Zanden**, "The 'revolt of the early modernists' and the 'first modern economy': an assessment," *EHR* 55:4 (2002), 619–641. **Abstract only** (Economic History Society site): "De Vries and Van der Woude maintain that the Dutch economy was exceptional in its process of 'modern economic growth' in 1500-1815. This article argues that economic growth in the Netherlands was probably not much faster than in England, as is clear from the development of real wages. The modernity of the Dutch economy in this period appears to be a product of economic and institutional changes in the middle ages."
- **Metadata only:** Herman Van der Wee, "Was the Dutch economy during its golden age really modern?", *European Review* 7:4 (1999), review of *The First Modern Economy* (Cambridge Core; no abstract). The title is not summarised and no source record is made.
- **Mediated, not used for any edge:** de Vries's *Historically Speaking* essay on Pomeranz (2011), known through Peer Vries (2013); its title is not confirmed (wave-1 pass 2).
