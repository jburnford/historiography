L66@P4: Abstract: This Element provides a pedagogical overview of the history
L67@P4: of knowledge, including its main currents, distinguishing ideas, and key
L68@P4: concepts. However, it is not primarily a state-of-the-art overview but
L69@P4: rather an argumentative contribution that seeks to push the field in
L70@P4: a certain direction – towards studying knowledge in society and
L71@P4: knowledge in people’s lives. Hence, the history of knowledge
L72@P4: envisioned by the authors is not a rebranding of the history of science
L73@P4: and intellectual history, but rather a reinvigoration of social and cultural
L74@P4: history. This implies that many different forms of knowledge should be
L75@P4: objects of study. By drawing on ongoing research from all across the
L76@P4: world dealing with different time periods and problems, the authors
L77@P4: demonstrate that the history of knowledge can enrich our
L78@P4: understanding of past societies. This title is also available as Open
L79@P4: Access on Cambridge Core.

L93@P6: On 9 August 1995, Netscape Communications Corporation was floated on the
L94@P6: American NASDAQ stock exchange. The company was then only sixteen
L95@P6: months old and did not turn a profit. But its browser – Netscape Navigator –
L96@P6: dominated the Internet, the hottest technology of the day. The interest from the
L97@P6: media and financial markets was enormous. When the first trading day was over,
L98@P6: the introductory share price had more than doubled and Netscape’s market value
L99@P6: was $2.9 billion. Was this the beginning of a new era, and if so, what would it
L100@P6: bring with it? Would the Internet disrupt our entire existence, or was it simply
L101@P6: a fad?1
L102@P6: In the mid-1990s, questions like these were highly topical. But uncertainty
L103@P6: was great, even in Silicon Valley. There, business executives and entrepreneurs were used to rapid technical development, big promises, and radical
L104@P6: visions of the future. Sometimes these materialised, but often they did not.
L105@P6: Would it be possible to navigate through this? Someone who thought long and
L106@P6: hard about this was Andrew Grove, a long-time managing director of the
L107@P6: market-leading semiconductor manufacturer Intel. He was there at the company’s founding in 1968 and had lived through several radical changes,
L108@P6: including Intel’s shift in the mid-1980s of its production from memory chips
L109@P6: to microprocessors.
L110@P6: At the time of Netscape’s flotation on the stock exchange, Grove was writing
L111@P6: a book that would eventually be titled Only the Paranoid Survive. Initially published in 1996, it is still in print, in spite of its examples now being obsolete. The
L112@P6: reason the book continues to find new readers is that the principal challenge
L113@P6: discussed by Grove remains: how should companies and individuals behave in
L114@P6: a world that is in a constant state of flux? And above all: what should one do when
L115@P6: the world’s dynamics are upended, when new technologies, laws, competitors, or
L116@P6: world events make that which once functioned no longer able to do so?
L117@P6: The final chapter in Only the Paranoid Survive is about the Internet. In this
L118@P6: chapter Grove perceptively, almost prophetically, discusses the potential significance of this technology for telecom operators, software developers, retailers, and media companies. His discussion indicates that the Internet will
L119@P6: probably rock the very foundations of many companies and businesses – but
L120@P6: not of Intel. At the same time, his gut tells him otherwise. He realises that the
L121@P6: interconnections of the world’s computers will affect his company at
L122@P6: a foundational level. It is high time to prepare for a different world.2
L123@P6: Retrospectively, this course of action may seem obvious. Surely, it was
L124@P6: evident to anyone – in particular to a director at one of the leading tech

L128@P7: But it is not quite that simple. It is difficult for people to appreciate the extent of
L129@P7: changes they experience, even for actors who have real power to steer development in a particular direction. The future is always more unsure and elusive in
L130@P7: real-time than retrospectively.
L131@P7: Andrew Grove can therefore hardly be blamed for not having discussed
L132@P7: the future significance of the Internet for the conditions, effects, and forms
L133@P7: of our shared knowledge. He was of course aware of Microsoft’s attempt to
L134@P7: create a digital encyclopaedia called Encarta, but he had no idea that within
L135@P7: a decade we would all be googling online and seeking knowledge on
L136@P7: Wikipedia. Smartphones, streaming services, and social media were not on
L137@P7: the map, let alone on the horizon. A few decades further on it is, however,
L138@P7: clear that in just a short time the foundations were profoundly shaken. This
L139@P7: happened at a societal level, where the prerequisites for communication, the
L140@P7: circulation of knowledge, and political mobilisation were repeatedly disrupted. But it also occurred at an individual level, where today our everyday
L141@P7: lives in principle demand access to a mobile phone and an Internet connection. When we need to learn something new, the first step is a search engine,
L142@P7: and the next is a freely accessible text, instruction film, or podcast. Media
L143@P7: infrastructure, the composition of the knowledge society, and the preconditions for global knowledge work are completely different today than they
L144@P7: were in the mid-1990s. Put briefly: the world before the Internet is now
L145@P7: history. And we who are around after this inflection point should – as
L146@P7: Andrew Grove did in 1996 – have the good sense to realise that the time
L147@P7: of revolutionary change is not past. Momentous things will continue to
L148@P7: happen, even if we cannot know what, when, how, or what consequences
L149@P7: they will have.
L150@P7: But can history give us any guidance? We believe so. If nothing else, it may
L151@P7: give us insights from other times when the basic preconditions of knowledge
L152@P7: changed. For instance, this happened with the invention of the first written
L153@P7: languages in ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt, India, and China. Writing permitted
L154@P7: the transfer of knowledge between generations and different places without the
L155@P7: direct interaction of people. It made possible civilisations, world religions,
L156@P7: cultural specialisation, and the concentration of wealth and power. Another
L157@P7: example is the impact of the printing press in fifteenth-century Europe. It
L158@P7: created completely new opportunities for writing down, disseminating, storing,
L159@P7: and using knowledge. The sixteenth-century Reformation can hardly be
L160@P7: imagined without such technical and media changes. This is true even though
L161@P7: social movements, protests, and power struggles – then and later – were also

L164@P8: If we look further ahead, towards the end of the nineteenth century, we can
L165@P8: see how modern journalism and the political party system in the Western world
L166@P8: developed symbiotically with the spread of universal literacy. The great breakthrough occurred when cheaper production technologies and subsidised mail
L167@P8: services had made it economically profitable to print newspapers in mass
L168@P8: editions. Simultaneously, conflict levels increased in societies that had industrialised. Workers organised, as did employers. The struggle centred on democracy, universal suffrage, and economic distribution, but also on what the state
L169@P8: and the authorities, the armed forces, and the educational system should be. At
L170@P8: universities, polytechnics, and growing businesses ever greater resources were
L171@P8: allocated to the natural sciences, technology, and medicine. At the same time, an
L172@P8: established culture of learning with humanistic overtones remained strong,
L173@P8: influencing art, culture, and public discussions of ideas. What was the place
L174@P8: of knowledge between tradition and modernity, ideology and idealism?4
L175@P8: If we turn to the initial decades of the post-war era, we again see different
L176@P8: patterns. At this time, the state and the military emerged as the major researchfunding bodies. From here, there is a direct line to Andrew Grove and the
L177@P8: Internet. Without enormous federal investments in military technology after the
L178@P8: Sputnik Shock of 1957, Silicon Valley would never have existed. Many other
L179@P8: wealthy nations invested a lot of money in higher education, which grew
L180@P8: substantially. The old elite university was replaced by a more democratic
L181@P8: mass university. In parallel with this, television, press, and radio flourished. In
L182@P8: the United Kingdom, the BBC dominated the airwaves and became the model
L183@P8: for public service channels in other countries. In the mid-1960s, many countries
L184@P8: had a single or only a few TV channels whose social reach and importance are
L185@P8: difficult to imagine today. Prosperity increased, and the vehicle-borne consumption society took shape alongside political investments in longer schooling for
L186@P8: boys and girls, regardless of their backgrounds. The large baby boom cohorts
L187@P8: born after the end of World War II had completely different preconditions than
L188@P8: their parents.5

L191@P8: their time. Those of us who have lived through the digital revolution have
L192@P8: witnessed changes in media and knowledge systems that have increased our
L193@P8: sensitivity for detecting similar phenomena in the past. Simple historical parallels are rarely easy to draw. But contemporary experiences enable us to notice

L201@P9: Questions like these are fundamental to the writers of this Element. We are two
L202@P9: historians at Lund University in Sweden who, together with our colleagues, have
L203@P9: endeavoured to introduce, establish, and develop a new field of research: the
L204@P9: history of knowledge. At an early stage, we were inspired by discussions in the
L205@P9: German-speaking part of the world. It was there, just after the turn of the millennium, that arguments emerged for replacing or complementing the traditional
L206@P9: history of science (Wissenschaftsgeschichte) with a new history of knowledge
L207@P9: (Wissensgeschichte). Its focus would lie on broader societal contexts rather than
L208@P9: on institutions of learning and elite actors. A watchword of the time was circulation. This created associations to globalisation and the Internet, and to the movement of people, capital, knowledge, and ideas. Researchers did not uncritically
L209@P9: embrace the concept of circulation, but they felt it could be used in order to study
L210@P9: and analyse the past in new ways.6
L211@P9: In parallel with our initial work on the history of knowledge, the field has
L212@P9: attracted ever greater attention in the English-speaking world. One can say that
L213@P9: this research field entered a new and more international phase in the mid-2010s.
L214@P9: This intensity has increased markedly over the last few years, and American,
L215@P9: Australian, British, Dutch, and Nordic researchers have begun to make contributions that deal explicitly with the history of knowledge.7
L216@P9: What we are dealing with is, fundamentally, a shift in perspective. By making
L217@P9: knowledge – not science, culture, politics, or ideas – central to historical study,
L218@P9: new questions, methods, research objects, and source materials become significant. But what does this mean in practice? What is the history of knowledge, and
L219@P9: how is it done? In this Element, we want to provide our answers, and to
L220@P9: encourage our readers to apply their own history of knowledge perspectives
L221@P9: on the past.

L240@P10: 1.1 The Concept of Knowledge
L241@P10: Historians of knowledge regard knowledge as a socially and historically determined phenomenon. The focus is on what people in various societies and times
L242@P10: have believed they know about themselves and the world. Consequently,
L243@P10: anything perceived, dealt with, or acted on as knowledge becomes potentially
L244@P10: interesting to study. Whether the knowledge claims of past generations are
L245@P10: today considered well-founded or true is, as a general rule, not interesting to
L246@P10: a historian of knowledge. What we study instead is how something became
L247@P10: knowledge for people and what the consequences of this were for different
L248@P10: societies and cultures.
L249@P10: This foundational way of looking at knowledge is something historians of
L250@P10: knowledge share with anthropologists and sociologists. We all emphasise that
L251@P10: knowledge is always embedded in larger cultural and social contexts. In this
L252@P10: context, Norwegian anthropologist Fredrik Barth has spoken about traditions of
L253@P10: knowledge as consisting of a collection of statements about the world, conveyed
L254@P10: through words and symbols, within some kind of institutionalised social relations. Various aspects are interwoven with one another. Knowledge can thus not
L255@P10: be something exclusively individual or particular. Instead, it is generated by
L256@P10: people sharing and jointly maintaining it.9
L257@P10: The philosophical reflections on what knowledge is stretch back to antiquity.
L258@P10: Plato defined knowledge as well-justified belief. But how can we know what
L259@P10: beliefs are well-justified? For his part, Aristotle underlined that there were
L260@P10: different forms of knowledge: episteme is rational or theoretical knowledge,
L261@P10: techne is artisanal or artistic knowledge, and phronesis is practical knowledge,
L262@P10: sagacity, or wisdom. Many historians of knowledge have favoured these
L263@P10: 9 Barth, ‘Anthropology’.

L265@P10-11: https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009047715 Published online by Cambridge University Pressdistinctions, not least because they enable a broader historical study of the types
L266@P11: of knowledge that exist and operate far beyond the worlds of learning.10
L267@P11: A complicated question is whether the truth of a knowledge claim actually
L268@P11: matters to a historian of knowledge. Are astrology, alchemy, and astronomy
L269@P11: equally viable objects of study? Yes, on one level they are. If it can be shown
L270@P11: that people have treated something as knowledge, then it is fruitful to investigate it as such. Historians of knowledge do not wish to judge past systems of
L271@P11: thought and knowledge. The history of knowledge includes various kinds of
L272@P11: scholarship – but these are not the only, or even necessarily the primary, objects
L273@P11: of study. At the same time, there are divisions within the field. Some researchers
L274@P11: and scholars, including Philipp Sarasin, have argued that knowledge historians
L275@P11: should concentrate on analysing systematic and rational forms of knowledge.
L276@P11: He makes an enquiry into the value of seeing, for example, conspiracy theories
L277@P11: as knowledge phenomena. Sarasin is aware, however, of the difficulties of
L278@P11: establishing such boundaries in practice. He also maintains that knowledgehistorical methods can be used to study phenomena that scholars themselves
L279@P11: believe are irrational or superstitious.11
L280@P11: Another aspect of this problem is whether our analysis is contingent on
L281@P11: historical actors themselves having referred to knowledge using the term
L282@P11: knowledge or one of its synonyms. If so, it is only conscious knowledge that
L283@P11: is studied. But people can be both unaware of – or stay silent about – that which
L284@P11: they employ as knowledge, perhaps because it appears so obviously true for
L285@P11: them that it does not need stating aloud or writing down. Or perhaps they lack
L286@P11: the necessary analytical concepts or live in a culture where these do not exist.
L287@P11: For this reason, Jürgen Renn feels that one should steer a middle course between
L288@P11: knowledge as a category used by historical actors (what people have believed to
L289@P11: be knowledge) and knowledge as a strictly analytical category (what
L290@P11: researchers define as knowledge after the fact). The former may lead to
L291@P11: a radically subjectivist or relativist position that makes it impossible to compare
L292@P11: phenomena across time and space. The latter risks becoming anachronistic,
L293@P11: awkward, and difficult to apply empirically in historical studies. Renn’s way out
L294@P11: of this dilemma is to see history of knowledge studies as explorations of both the
L295@P11: past and of the question of what knowledge really is.12
L296@P11: What we see here is tension between the history of knowledge as a science of
L297@P11: objects of research (where the main issue is what knowledge is and has been)
L298@P11: and the history of knowledge as a cluster of perspectives on the past (where the
L299@P11: value of knowledge is as an entry point which helps us investigate other things).
L300@P11: Like Jürgen Renn, we feel that the history of knowledge should be both of these

L304@P12: important thing about investigations in the history of knowledge is that they
L305@P12: provide new understanding of past societies, phenomena, and processes of
L306@P12: change. Therefore, we do not find it problematic that the history of knowledge
L307@P12: adopts a broad, imprecise concept of knowledge.
L308@P12: There are, however, critics who reason differently. What is not knowledge?
L309@P12: they ask. Other researchers have tried to turn this objection into a solution to the
L310@P12: problem. Lukas Verburgt and Peter Burke feel that it is especially fruitful to
L311@P12: focus on the boundaries of knowledge, on what is considered ignorance or what
L312@P12: is even ignored. It is then possible to indirectly make visible and analyse what
L313@P12: knowledge has been and how it has functioned.13 Another alternative is to
L314@P12: analyse hierarchies, conflicts, and aspects of power. The analyses will then
L315@P12: focus on how different forms of knowledge have been evaluated relative to each
L316@P12: other, and which people have had the authority to decide what knowledge is in
L317@P12: various historical contexts. Thereby it can be demonstrated how something is
L318@P12: elevated to being knowledge – or demoted to being ignorance.14
L319@P12: Our own basic position is that knowledge is a socially anchored form of
L320@P12: knowing. Such knowledge can be more or less changeable, dispersed, and used
L321@P12: in different places and at different times. But by focusing on the roles, meanings, prerequisites, and consequences of knowledge, a better understanding of
L322@P12: past societies is made possible. For us this exploration is a key driving force.
L323@P12: What we want to know more about is how human societies have functioned in
L324@P12: the past. The history of knowledge provides us with a fruitful approach to this
L325@P12: issue.15
