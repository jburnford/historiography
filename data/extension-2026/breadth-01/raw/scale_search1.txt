## Abstract

This Handbook brings together twenty-nine authors from around the world, each expert in a different area within the history of disability. This collection of new and original essays forms a benchmark in a field of historical inquiry that has been growing and maturing over the last thirty years. It is the first book to gather critical essays that incorporate studies from South and East Asia, eastern and western Europe, Australia, North America, and the Arab world. This Handbook is unique among other disability history texts in that it engages simultaneously in methodological and historiographic debates and in a further articulation and analysis of the lived experiences of disabled people.

## ABSTRACT

This article offers a definition of digital history that focuses on the core affordances of the personal computer and the process by which those properties come to be exploited. I begin by outlining the two properties of computers that I argue define digital history: they process data and (as Janet H. Murray noted) provide an immersive and interactive medium. I then examine how digital historians in the United States have employed those affordances. That process has proceeded unevenly, with computers having been used as a medium before they were employed to process data, and has produced additive forms—digital archives, digital public history, data analysis published in print—that rely on existing formats rather than on the affordances of computers. 8†5】 Digital history can be usefully understood on the same basis by exploring the extent to which the distinctive affordances of computers have been exploited in historical practice.6 In taking that approach, I develop a holistic view of digital history, not a comprehensive picture; there is scope to give only limited attention to the characteristics of different digital methods and the audience for whom digital history is created.

I begin by outlining the two properties of computers that I argue are central to defining digital history: computers process data and (as Murray noted) provide an immersive and interactive medium. I then examine how digital historians in the United States have used those affordances. That process has proceeded unevenly, with computers having been employed as a medium before they were used to process data, a disjunction that continues to shape how digital history is understood and how it relates to established historical practices. The digital history that has been created to date consists of additive forms that do not fully employ the affordances of computers. This situation is not unique to digital history. In an updated edition published nearly twenty years after Hamlet on the Holodeck first appeared in print, Murray noted that, in relation to computers as a medium, “legacy traditions continue to exert a powerful influence.”7 It is only recently that reminders of the time change takes have appeared in writing about digital history, with historians Andreas Fickers, Frédéric Clavert, and Ethan Kleinberg suggesting from different sides of the Atlantic that it will take another generation for a fully realized form of digital history to emerge.8 A definition that attends to the core properties of computers provides a useful reference point as digital history continues to develop. As Murray suggested, understanding the direction of that development requires focusing on the new affordances being used in additive forms. Doing so highlights new combinations of the two properties of computers in digital history.

## ADDITIVE DIGITAL HISTORY

Historians’ engagement with the two properties of computers that define digital history has proceeded unevenly. Spurred particularly by the connection of personal computers to the internet, digital historians initially focused on the medium offered by computers. Digital historians put content into the medium of the computer, briefly in the form of CD-ROMs, and then online, alongside the material digitized and shared online by libraries, archives, and museums. However, in the US, their audiences differed from those in other humanities fields: US audiences were in classrooms and were part of a wider public more often than they were other scholars, as I've argued elsewhere.57 When Daniel J. Cohen and Roy Rosenzweig classified history websites in 2005, the genres they identified followed “preexisting patterns and categories”; they were additive (in Murray's sense), older formats that had been recreated in the medium offered by the computer.58 The archive became the digital archive, the exhibit became the digital exhibit, the textbook became the digital textbook or e-book, and, later, the radio broadcast became the podcast. --------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Disability scholars study a wide range of people “who identified as, or who were described as mad, insane, disabled, infirm, deaf, deformed, lame, crippled, blind, unsound, and numerous other terms” (p. 1).
