L81: ## Abstract
L82: This state of the field article presents three questions for students and scholars of childhood: first, who is included in the history of childhood? Second, why does the history of childhood matter? Third, how should we do the history of childhood? It argues that all historians of childhood need to reflect upon modern developmental concepts of childhood which purport to tell us what children of different chronological ages can think, understand and feel.
L83: These limiting concepts of childhood originate from eighteenth-century Anglo-American concepts of liberal personhood that position children as non-people due to their lack of reason and their dependence on others. However, as ‘childhood studies’ scholars have shown, they still govern many of our instincts and assumptions about childhood today.
L84: Historians of childhood will have most to offer once we have interrogated our own concepts of childhood, but also when we fully consider how childhood has been historically constructed in relation to adulthood.
L85: ## I
L86: ‘The history of childhood is a nightmare from which we have only recently begun to awaken,’ wrote Lloyd deMause in 1974.cite15†1 DeMause was suggesting that premodern childhoods were marked by suffering, abuse and pain, and that only modern Western ideas about child welfare could save children from this horror.
L87: Despite the huge strides that historians of childhood have made over the last fifty years, this kind of assertion - that there is a ‘right’ kind of childhood that all children ought to experience - has been remarkably persistent. Paula Fass argued in 2013 that modern childhood is ‘a privileged state’ and that all children across the globe should have equal access to this model of protected innocence confined safely within the private sphere.cite16†2 This ideal is relatively new.
L88: Late eighteenth-century Anglo-American classical liberalism introduced new dividing lines between childhood and adulthood, based on children's supposed lack of ‘reason’, and, in the Global North, this led to the development of ideas about the necessity of childhood ‘freedom’ from adult concerns.cite17†3 This concept of ‘modern childhood’ is bolstered by the psy sciences and, more recently, neuroscience, which advance claims about the nature of children and what they require to develop into healthy adults.
L89: Here, I will contend that the dominant model of childhood in the Global North, like the concepts of childhood that preceded it and which exist alongside it, often silences, belittles and oppresses children, despite the familiar refrain that modern children are more fortunate than ever before.
L90: The ‘history of the history of childhood’ has already been the subject of a number of excellent ‘state of the field’ articles and survey texts, and this article will not replicate this previous work.cite18†4 The earliest histories, at least those written in English, tended to be centred on childhood in the Global North; later work looked at childhood across a much wider range of specific geographic areas; more recent work has considered childhood globally.cite19†5 While recognising that histories of childhood can be, and have been written, across the entire span of history, this article will focus on ‘the modern history of childhood’.
L91: Roughly speaking, this will cover the period from the late eighteenth century to the present day.cite20†6 This decision is partly logistical; in order to represent the diversity of global childhoods, I decided to restrict the time period to allow the broadest possible geographical scope.
L92: However, this article will also contend that, within the ‘modern history of childhood’, all historians, even those who focus on other historical periods, need to reckon with the normative ideas about modern childhood that dominate in the Global North.
L93: When challenging both the Global North conception of childhood and its implicit assumption that other kinds of childhoods are inadequate, we must be careful not simply to introduce new binaries.
L94: As Clovis Bergère states in a recent roundtable on childhood in the Global South, drawing on the work of the anthropologist Sarada Balagopalan, reproducing these divisions in academic work can frame childhood as either ‘Western and epistemiological or non-Western and empirical’.cite21†7 Afua Twum-Danso Imoh, Michael Bourdillon and Sylvia Meichsner point out in their recent edited collection that children in the Global North do not necessarily experience childhood as they are supposed to; meanwhile, it is clear that countries within the Global South have developed their own theoretical ideas about childhood that are not just imports from elsewhere.cite22†8 L95: This state of the field article is divided into three sections that put forward three questions: these questions address all students and scholars who are interested in childhood, and all historians who wish to critically integrate childhood as a category of historical analysis into their work.
L96: First, who is included in the history of childhood? Second, why does the history of childhood matter? Third, how should we do the history of childhood? These three questions are not addressed by all historians of childhood; indeed, the ‘state of the field’ is messy, with conflicting priorities that often relate to historians’ differing beliefs about what is good for children today.
L97: However, achieving clarity on these questions and engaging with them explicitly is, I contend, crucial for the progress of our field. Considering these questions should also enable us to systematically examine the failure of the Global North model of childhood to set adequate historical agendas.
L98: ## II
L99: Who is included in the history of childhood? The answer is much less straightforward than it might seem. Scholars of the modern Global North tend to use rigid definitions based on chronological age to separate young people into groups; for example, dividing childhood from adolescence.
L100: However, as Ishita Pande has argued, this mode of classification has its own history.cite23†9 Pande challenges historians to take age as a social construct as seriously as they take gender, pointing out that the modern ‘epistemic contract on age’ consists of ‘an implicit agreement that chronological age is a universal and natural measure of human capacity’.cite24†10 Pande's argument goes beyond recognising that chronological age has been defined differently and with varying levels of precision and significance in different societies at different times.
L101: She challenges the validity of chronological age as a neutral marker, asking us to question the assumptions we all make about the abilities of children to understand, to act and to feel.
L102: Where do these modern assumptions about chronological age and capacity come from? The legal division between childhood and adulthood that was established in the Anglo-American world in the eighteenth century expanded into more complex modes of age-differentiation across the Global North by the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
L103: The ‘child sciences’, especially developmental psychology and psychoanalysis, came to dominate thinking about childhood.cite25†11 These new ideas about childhood were expressed most prominently in the development of juvenile justice systems, child reformatories and other ‘child-saving’ institutions, and, perhaps most significantly, in compulsory schooling, where students were increasingly assigned to classes not by ability but by age, as comparative work by scholars such as Kathryn Anderson-Levitt and Stephen Lassonde has shown.cite26†12 In the twentieth century, ‘child-centred’ or ‘progressive’ education, influential across Britain, Western Europe and the United States, promoted the idea that children developed certain competencies only after they reached certain chronological ages.
L104: Progressive education claimed to extend children's freedom by tailoring education to their ‘natural’ needs and interests, but when translated into practice in large-scale national school systems, it entrenched stereotypes about age, race, class and gender.cite27†13 L105: Even as developmental psychological discourses were being challenged in the Global North in the later twentieth century, new ‘scientific’ ways of conceptualising the child-adult binary emerged, although they augmented rather than superseded these older ideas.cite28†14 It is now commonplace to be told in popular media that children and adolescents are fundamentally incapable of long-term thinking and impulse control because of the way that human brains develop.cite29†15 Even as feminist neuroscientists such as Cordelia Fine and Gina Rippon challenge studies that claim to show significant sex differences between male and female brains, these models of child and adult brains proceed unquestioned in popular culture.cite30†16 This reinscribes ‘common sense’ ideas about age-linked abilities and the difference between ‘irrational’ children and ‘rational’ adults in a language that, since the 1980s, has arguably been perceived publicly as even more authoritative than the knowledge produced by the psy sciences.cite31†17 ‘Critical neuroscience’, pioneered by Suparna Choudhury and Jan Slaby, challenges this kind of thinking by adopting an interdisciplinary approach, integrating neuroscientific concepts into understandings of selfhood developed by the humanities.
L106: It uses tools such as Ian Hacking's ‘feedback looping’, which suggests, very roughly, that what we believe about the way our brains work can affect our brains.cite32†18 Changes in brain structure and function during adolescence cannot, it is argued, be simply assumed to be the cause of certain stereotypically ‘adolescent’ behaviours, as behaviour, culture, and expectations shape brain structure in their turn.cite33†19 L107: Chronological age was not the only way of understanding differences between childhood and adulthood in the modern period.
L108: Historians of Africa have emphasised how ‘generation’ was more important in the structure of kinship and age-relations in gerontocratic African societies into the twentieth century.cite34†20 As Oyèrónké Oyěwùmí has argued, the major organising principle in Yoruba society prior to colonisation was seniority, derived by relative age, which meant that it was always a relational category rather than being rigidly tied to biological norms.cite35†21 Looking at late colonial Buganda [now Uganda], Carol Summers argued that radical Ganda activists in the Bataka movement of the 1940s drew on older traditions of youth and generation to propose an alliance between ‘grandfathers and grandsons’ against the ‘greed of fathers’, challenging hierarchical models of age.
L109: Ganda norms also rejected Western ideas of youth as an inevitably delinquent stage of life and reassessed it as a time of opportunity and creativity, with equal responsibilities to those of adults. Ganda youth ‘were junior adults, not different in kind from their seniors, simply not as far along the path.’cite36†22 L110: Young Africans could make instrumental use of colonial norms to challenge gerontocracy. Beverly Grier has explored how young African men in Southern Rhodesia [now Zimbabwe] used European definitions of ‘child’ and ‘adult’ to their advantage. While male elders were wary of migrant labour as it deprived them of labour resources in their own households, younger men could use new systems of labour contracts and passes to seek work elsewhere.
L111: The 1904 Native Pass and Native Tax Ordinances let any males over fourteen register with officials and carry passes, accelerating their entry into ‘manhood’, which traditionally might have been delayed a further ten or twenty years after puberty as they remained in ‘junior’ positions relative to their fathers.cite37†23 On the other hand, local adult elites might adopt colonial models of childhood as a way of of seizing power within colonial administrations by demonstrating claims to special expertise, as Satadru Sen argues in his examination of juvenile reform in India between 1850 and 1945.cite38†24 L112: Such groups often claimed that these models of childhood had a longer history within their own country, remodelling older knowledge as they decided how to make use of Global North discourses. Farida Makar points out that the centre-periphery model - which assumes that ideas are simply imported from colonial ‘centres’ to a non-Western ‘periphery’ - does not accurately describe how ideas actually spread.
L113: Instead, she argues, ‘ideas “transferred” from the metropole are actively altered, recycled, re-interpreted and worked with to produce new meanings in the periphery’, using the example of inter-war Egyptian pedagogues, who were engaged in transnational conversations about ‘progressive education’ not only with Europe and the United States but with Turkey and Iraq.cite39†25 The nineteenth-century Egyptian Islamic scholar Muhammad Abduh claimed that modern ideas about childhood had actually originated in the East, the original centre of the Enlightenment.cite40†26 Su Lin Lewis's work on the ‘modern girl’ in 1930s Penang, British Malaya [now Malaysia] shows that new discourses about age did not inevitably start in ‘the centre’ and gradually reach ‘the periphery’, but could have multiple centres, arising in different places at the same time.cite41†27 L114: So, who do we include in the history of childhood, if chronological age cannot be treated as a neutral norm? Here, I deliberately use ‘history of childhood’ as an umbrella term that has no strict age-limits, and so includes the histories of those labelled as ‘adolescent’, ‘youth’ or ‘teenage’.
L115: I have made this decision to highlight how age categories interlink with power inequalities; given how they are defined in modern models of childhood, children and adolescents have much more in common with each other than they do with adults.
L116: From a global historical perspective, using an expanded definition of the ‘history of childhood’ also makes sense; children are defined differently in different times and different places.cite42†28 Furthermore, terminology such as ‘young’ may be extended to particular groupings regardless of the chronological age of the actors, as Fabio Lanza has shown by separating the idea of ‘youth’ from the work of young activists in twentieth-century China.cite43†29 Setting universal age-limits on whom the history of childhood should or must include both does not make sense and artificially narrows its remit.
L117: ## III
L118: Why does the history of childhood matter? Recently, some brilliant defences of the field have been produced by scholars such as Steven Mintz, Ishita Pande and Bengt Sandin.cite44†30 Historians of childhood are often confronted with the assertion that children are only important insofar as they affect adult histories.
L119: Tobias Hecht writes that it is difficult to understand particular phenomena in Latin American history, such as the labour market, if children are taken out.cite45†31 If we take this view, including children in history is important because children are part of the world, but this reserves no special task for the historian of childhood and indeed questions why anyone would specialise solely in the history of children.
L120: Certain historical fields, such as the histories of emotions and of selfhood, invoke childhood for more particular reasons, perceiving natural links between childhood and ‘emotional socialisation’ within the family.cite46†32 But the risk here is that children's experiences are only ascribed historical meaning insofar as they affect the adults these children will become. In Karen Sánchez-Eppler's words, children are seen only as ‘objects of socialisation’ rather than as ‘forces of socialisation’.cite47†33 L121: Childhood experiences have also been used to analyse intersectional identities, such as gender and race.cite48†34 Undoubtedly, the white, male, able-bodied, middle-class and implicitly heterosexual and cisgendered child is often the ‘invisible norm’ in scholarship, and it is vital to understand how both discourses of childhood, and the actual experiences of children, have been shaped by these other axes of oppression.cite49†35 However, this kind of work can also lead historians to see children solely as adults-in-the-making.
L122: This is especially egregious in the history and sociology of education, which has a tendency to focus on social mobility and class privilege.
L123: For example, Diane Reay's Miseducation (2017) clearly exposes how British working-class children suffer in the twenty-first century education system but ascribes this solely to their class, rather than their age.cite50†36 This ignores the reason that children are subjected to compulsory education - their chronological age - and denies that age can be a category of oppression in its own right, as anthropologist Patrick Alexander explores in his ethnography of an English secondary school between 2007 and 2008.cite51†37 It also falls into the trap of assuming that children who are ‘successful’ in adult terms - for example, middle-class children who achieve good qualifications - are automatically benefited by schooling.
L124: Writing in the first issue of the Journal of the History of Childhood and Youth in 2008, Joseph M.
L125: Hawes and Ray Hiner argued that the ‘main issue’ for historians of childhood was to ‘to resist any historiographical trend or fashion that turns attention away from children or by implication denies them historical agency’.cite52†38 This implies that if children are not historical agents, they are not worthy of the historian's attention; the history of childhood matters because it is the search for children's agency.
L126: Useful theoretical contributions that nuance earlier claims about ‘childhood agency’ have been made by scholars such as Mona Gleason, Karen Vallgårda, Kristine Alexander and Stephanie Olsen.cite53†39 Gleason points out that agency has too often been understood as individual resistance to dominant societal norms and/or to adults themselves.
L127: Some children participate in such actions, but children can also exercise agency through collaboration with norms or employ more subtle strategies of negotiation and alliance with adults or peers.cite54†40 As Kathryn A.
L128: Sloan has shown, ‘minor’ teenage girls in the nineteenth-century Mexican state of Oaxaca used institutional structures to gain emancipation from their families, relying on sympathetic judges to allow them to marry without parental consent.cite55†41 Children's willing performance of childhood further contributes to the construction of age.cite56†42 L129: Pande and Sandin, amongst others, point out that employing modern developmental models of childhood has led to the assumption that children are simply incapable of being agentic, and questioning these models helps to reveal children's agency.cite57†43 However, as Lynn M.
L130: Thomas states in her consideration of ‘agency’ in research on African women, asserting that subjects had agency as a ‘concluding argument’ rather than a ‘starting point’ both positions the scholar as the ‘saviour’ of their oppressed subjects and ignores how agency itself is a historically contingent category.cite58†44 Particular attributes linked to Global North conceptions of what children ‘should’ be like at given ages can be used to ascribe agency only to children who meet these norms, as Annie McCarthy explores in her work on NGO policies intended to promote children's participation in a handwashing campaign in Delhi, which saw children as natural storytellers and valorised children who could tell creative narratives.cite59†45 L131: Some scholars, such as Sarah Maza, assert unquestioningly that the history of childhood is distinct from the histories of other oppressed and marginalised groups because children do not have shared political aims related to the category of childhood, and have ‘never set collective agendas for themselves’.cite60†46 This is straightforwardly not true.
L132: To take just one example, Owen Emmerson has researched the activity of the British Schools Action Union [SAU] between 1968–74, which organised national school strikes against corporal punishment and in support of its other demands, including universal co-educational comprehensive schools and better pay for teachers. SAU was led by adolescents but falsely suspected of being ‘controlled’ by hidden adult activists from far-left groups, which led to its surveillance by the Heath government.
L133: As a result of the SAU's activism, the Inner London Education Authority [ILEA] banned corporal punishment in all its schools in 1974, though it would not be until 1986 that corporal punishment was outlawed nationally.cite61†47 L134: The instinctive belief that children cannot organise collectively betrays both some historians’ adherence to the modern developmental model of childhood and their inability to think beyond adult perspectives on what unites an oppressed group.
L135: Children are, of course, differently positioned in this respect; everybody experiences being placed in the social relationship that constitutes ‘childhood’, and most – though not all – people ultimately move outside of this relationship.cite62†48 However, to assume that children think their experience in the here and now is not important because it will ultimately be over is to impose an adult understanding of the life-cycle.
L136: It also implies that children have no capacity for solidarity with the children who will come after them. Much adult activism would be rendered pointless if, for example, feminists and anti-racism activists took only actions that would directly benefit themselves, but such activists consider future generations.
L137: Why shouldn't children, albeit in a more limited timeframe, do the same? As seventeen-year-old Mexican-American climate activist Xiuhtezcatl Martinez, a political campaigner since the age of six, said in 2018: ‘Young people are fighting like hell now so that generations down the road will never have to struggle in the way we have’.cite63†49 L138: Nevertheless, it is clear that many children, perhaps especially younger children, cannot exercise agency in the way that many traditional modes of history-writing might require. Historians of childhood, therefore, need to go further, and question why we so constantly feel the need to say that children possess historical agency.
L139: Walter Johnson argues in his classic article ‘On Agency’ (2003) that the idea that the job of historians of African-American slavery is ‘to give the slaves back their agency’ is ultimately destructive, because the word ‘agency’ itself assumes the universality of ‘a liberal notion of selfhood’ which slavery was originally defined against.cite64†50 In this context, asserting that children possess ‘agency’ is a contradiction in terms, because the concept of agency has also been historically constructed in opposition to the figure of the irrational and dependent child.
L140: Instead, historians of childhood should ask why the only subjects who are deemed worthy of historical inquiry are those who are seen to meet the standards of classical liberal personhood.
L141: ## IV
L142: How should we do the history of childhood? Sources have often been seen as an especial problem for historians of childhood.cite65†51 Traditional histories have assumed that the child's perspective is not recoverable, and so have constructed histories of childhood ‘top-down’, using adult-produced materials.
L143: These assertions were sometimes made in the absence of evidence, with historians claiming that it was not possible to find children in the archive, especially children who experienced oppression along multiple axes. As Tammy Charelle-Owens writes:
L144: But, without fail, at the beginning stages of my research on black girlhood during the era of slavery, my questions and requests for materials were frequently met with blank stares or suggestions that black girlhood was lost.cite66†52 L145: More recently, it has been asserted by Kristine Moruzi, Nell Musgrove and Carla Pascoe Leahy that novel methodologies, including the use of material culture, make the child's perspective more accessible than was previously believed, and it is now more common to find criticisms of modern histories of childhood that lack the ‘child's voice’.cite67†53 L146: The division between the insights of adult-authored and child-authored sources is in some ways unhelpful; understanding adult constructions of childhood is necessary to understand how children navigated them. There are also methodological concerns. Frequently, the ‘child's voice’ is uncovered through the use of adult testimonies of childhood, such as self-narratives and oral histories.
L147: While these are invaluable sources for historians of childhood, they are too often used uncritically, with no thought given to how adult subjectivity shapes these accounts; adults, as Jay Mechling points out, can no longer narrate from the subaltern perspective of their childhood selves.cite68†54 Even when it is acknowledged that child-authored written sources exist, historians of childhood are often told that their sources are uniquely problematic while other historians using self-narrative sources remain unchallenged.
L148: Most historians of childhood will be familiar with the question: ‘Didn't children just write what adults told them to write?’cite69†55 Again, the artificial division between adults as independent moral agents, totally removed from the influence of others, and children as passive recipients of culture with no minds of their own, haunts the history of childhood.
L149: Calls have been made by historians such as Steven Mintz and Lynn Botelho to use ‘age as a category of historical analysis’, which could be seen as another way to do the history of childhood. At first, this seemed to amount to little more than a suggestion that historians should pay closer attention to the experiences of different age-groups across time.cite70†56 However, more sophisticated versions of this assertion have been developed by Corinne T. Field and Nicholas L.
L150: Syrett, demonstrating how the category of chronological age has been used as a tool of power.cite71†57 Age mediates inequalities beyond those which exist between adults and children.
L151: Uday Singh Mehta, Shirleene Robinson and Simon Sleight have examined how colonised states and subjects are often viewed as ‘immature’ and in need of ‘tutelage’ from the imperial metropole.cite72†58 Kate Fisher and Jana Funke have shown how homosexual men in late nineteenth and early twentieth-century Europe were conceptualised as in a state of arrested development, unable to move past the adolescent phase.cite73†59 While this work is hugely important, it sometimes poses problems of its own for historians of childhood, in short, it can revert to treating the subordination of children as ‘natural’, only finding ageist language problematic when it is extended to groups that are not ‘children’ as defined by chronological age.cite74†60 For example, Field's book The Struggle for Equal Adulthood adeptly shows how white women and black people demanded recognition as adults in the antebellum United States, but never questions the adult/child division as it was applied to those who were deemed ‘underage’.cite75†61 L152: It is sociologists, anthropologists and philosophers of childhood, rather than historians, who have often most thoughtfully considered age as an intersectional category of analysis.
L153: This work is sometimes grouped together under the label of ‘childhood studies’.cite76†62 Rachel Rosen and Katherine Twamley argue that modern feminism can often be ‘adultist’ in its unquestioning use of the category of ‘the child’, not examining the ‘ways in which certain humans are made into children and others into adults’.cite77†63 In the same edited collection, Ohad Zehavi suggests that we should move away from the liberal model of the independent self, recognising that children are not completely dependent on others and also that dependence is not automatically abandoned once we are legally defined as ‘adults’.cite78†64 This is perhaps especially relevant for disabled adults, who, as Patrick McKearney has shown, may have difficulty meeting traditional milestones of modern ‘adulthood’, but also highlights how all adults ultimately remain interdependent.cite79†65 Having said this, however, scholars in childhood studies - many of whom do historical work themselves - would also benefit from closer dialogue with historians of childhood.
L154: Historians have perhaps been reluctant to challenge the adult/child binary because they believe that, unlike other power relationships, this one is not inherently oppressive, and indeed, may be necessary for the protection of children.cite80†66 This taps into a twentieth- and twenty-first-century sociological and anthropological literature that argues that modern children now have too much power and privilege, although they are also endangered because they are being forced to grow up too fast.cite81†67 Anthropologist David Lancy argues that the modern United States is now a ‘neontocracy’ rather than a gerontocracy because ‘kids rule’, suggesting that ‘the child's wellbeing is paramount; its needs trump the desires of other family members’.cite82†68 This echoes Neil Postman's The Disappearance of Childhood (1982), where he argued that childhood was being eroded because children now have free access to information through new forms of media, which challenges the necessary authority of adults.cite83†69 Interestingly, in the preface to the 1994 edition, Postman quoted the reactions of some fifth and sixth graders [ten to twelve year olds] to this book.
L155: These readers were acutely aware that childhood is not so easy to escape or lose, with one writing ‘I think your essay wasn't very good. Childhood doesn't disappear – snap! – like that’.cite84†70 L156: The removal of childhood from certain groups has been seen by scholars as a form of oppression, which, again, frames childhood as a privilege.
L157: For example, the historian of slavery Wilma King explored how childhood was ‘stolen’ from enslaved black children.cite85†71 More recent analyses of slavery and childhood have nuanced this interpretation but still argue that slavery harmfully cut childhood short.cite86†72 In the modern United States, it has been convincingly shown that black children are ‘adultified’, denied the protections of juvenile justice and sentenced more harshly than their white counterparts.cite87†73 However, as Toby Rollo has recently argued, we can recognise the impact of adultification on black children without asserting that childhood itself is a privileged state that we should fight to preserve, as ‘in no era has childhood proven to be a genuine site of security or safety’.cite88†74 If historians of childhood fully adopt age as a category of historical analysis, we are well placed to explore these tensions, and to challenge simplistic, nostalgic assertions about how childhood ought to be.
L158: ## V
L159: To conclude, three linked but separate reflections. First, one thing that might help historians address our three questions is to consider how children themselves write and talk about age.
L160: According to black feminist ‘standpoint’ theory, oppressed groups can access unique insights into the way that their own oppression functions.cite89†75 Modern children are the social group most often asked to think explicitly about age-categories, as they were and are constantly asked ‘how old are you?’ or ‘what do you want to be when you grow up?’.cite90†76 Children have historically been socialised to regard those who are younger than them as inferior, and adulthood as an aspirational state, even if they may also anticipate it rather unwillingly.cite91†77 Therefore, children's testimony about age – whether that is written, spoken or expressed through their silences or through their play – should be a central part of making sense of this category of historical analysis.cite92†78 L161: Second, children are ‘future-orientated’ in that they are rarely seen as full humans in their own right, but as moving towards adulthood, when they will attain this status.
L162: They also serve as – often oppressive – symbols of the future in adult discourse.cite93†79 While childhood studies, in alliance with queer theory, has sought to challenge this understanding of children, it still often persists unquestioned in historical work on childhood.cite94†80 Using Stephen Kern's term, Nancy Lesko calls this ‘expectant time’, capturing the sense of being both continually progressing and suspended in place, like objects on a factory production line.cite95†81 Discussing the particular experience of black childhood in the United States, Jacob Breslow suggests that black children are ‘out of time’ in a way that white children are not.
L163: Dominant discourses do not see them as truly children, but because they have never had a childhood, they cannot progress to being adults.cite96†82 Ruby Lal identifies a similar kind of temporal suspension for ‘girl-children’ in nineteenth-century India, who were unable ever to achieve adulthood.cite97†83 In this context, children's own accounts of childhood are especially valuable, because they were composed while they still existed in this liminal space.
L164: Finally, I contend that as long as adulthood is still positioned historically as a neutral, static norm, we will not be able to fully understand childhood. Age, like gender, is a fundamentally relational category, but most current historical work does not consider how the experience of being ‘young’ is shaped in response to what it means to be ‘older’. While histories of adulthood are beginning to emerge, they often lack critical perspective.
L165: Some simply add another life-stage to the existing litany, thinking about ‘middle age’ or the ‘midlife crisis’, while not considering how these stages are also constructed against healthy adulthood.cite98†84 Others claim to historicise adulthood while actually reinscribing a sequence of modern stereotypes about what it means to be ‘grown up’, connecting adulthood to life-cycle markers such as marriage, paid employment and home ownership.cite99†85 Once again, it is other disciplines who have done the most useful theoretical work – this time, the literary scholars Sari Edelstein, Claudia Nelson and Teresa Michals – although historical analyses of modern adulthood are not entirely absent.cite100†86 L166: We began with Lloyd deMause's teleological statement about our progress away from the horrors of the past, but historians of childhood must not subscribe to such reductive ideas about how we as adults have made childhood better for children. Instead, we must acknowledge our own complicity in a fundamentally ageist society and challenge the ways in which children are structurally oppressed.
