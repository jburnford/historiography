L5@P0: Abstract
L6@P0: Historical studies of bodily and cognitive difference have flourished in the past decade.
L7@P0: This article surveys recent work in disability history to provide a sense of the state of the
L8@P0: field today. Concentrating on work published in English, the article outlines three main
L9@P0: pillars of the field: its political impetus; its commitment to a sociocultural approach to
L10@P0: disability, and its insistence that disability constitutes a powerful category of historical
L11@P0: analysis. Following this, the article discusses the sources and methods used by disability
L12@P0: historians, as well as some of the ethical issues their work raises. Major themes and areas of
L13@P0: strength in the field are also identified. The article concludes by suggesting how disability
L14@P0: history might develop in the future and encourages disability historians to push beyond a
L15@P0: Cartesian separation of body and mind when considering human difference.

L42@P1: is disability history and where is it heading? In this article, we survey
L43@P1: historical research on disability and offer some suggestions about how
L44@P1: the field might develop in the future. Our comments are necessarily
L45@P1: selective and focus on Anglophone work published over the last decade
L46@P1: or so. Despite this choice, we recognise that disability history is an
L47@P1: international endeavour. A lot of important work in the field does not
L48@P1: appear in English.2 Yet, this scholarship offers a crucial counterweight
L49@P1: to Anglo-American perspectives. Historians serious about developing
L50@P1: nuanced accounts of disability will learn a lot from this work, and we
L51@P1: urge colleagues to engage with it to the best of their abilities.
L52@P1: We begin with a brief history of the field that pays special attention to
L53@P1: its emergence, rationale and theoretical approach. We then discuss how
L54@P1: disability history has been conducted in recent years, concentrating on the
L55@P1: sources and methods used by disability historians and the ethical issues
L56@P1: they have encountered. In the final part of our survey, we identify major
L57@P1: themes and areas of strength in the field before concluding with some
L58@P1: proposals for future work in disability history.
L59@P1: II
L60@P1: The origins of disability history as a distinct field of study lie in the
L61@P1: 1980s. This was a decade in which, thanks to the efforts of the Disability
L62@P1: Rights Movement (DRM) and the United Nations’ International Year of
L63@P1: Disabled Persons (1981), disability started to receive significant attention
L64@P1: in public and political discourse. In this climate, Henri–Jacques Stiker
L65@P1: and Paul Longmore published what are now considered foundational
L66@P1: texts in disability history. Inspired by the work of Michel Foucault,
L67@P1: Stiker focussed on Europe, whereas Longmore, who was informed by his
L68@P1: personal experience of disability and a significant figure in US disability
L69@P1: activism, concentrated on America. Both identified the twentieth century
L70@P1: as a key period in the history of disability, proposing broad schemas that
L71@P1: others have since refined.3 However, it was not until the 1990s that the field
L72@P1: really began to take off, particularly in the United States. Inspired by the
L73@P1: successes of disability activists and the enactment of the Americans with
L74@P1: Disabilities Act in 1990, increasing numbers of scholars began excavating
L75@P1: the ‘hidden history’ of disability. Most considered themselves members
L76@P1: or allies of the DRM and aimed to create a ‘usable past’ to further the
L77@P1: movement’s goals.4 Consequently, from its earliest days, disability history

L90@P2: ‘voices’ within the historiography of disability.5 Such politically engaged
L91@P2: scholarship, informed by activism, remains a hallmark of disability

L93@P2: The field was also conspicuous in its critical approach to the so-called
L94@P2: ‘medical model’, which informed earlier historical work on disability and
L95@P2: continues to be influential. Shorthand for ideas about bodily and cognitive
L96@P2: non-normativity that became dominant in the late modern period, the
L97@P2: medical model sees disability as something ‘unchanging, pathological,
L98@P2: rooted in individual bodies, and always in need of cure, correction, or
L99@P2: elimination’.6 In contrast, disability historians generally advanced ‘social’
L100@P2: or ‘minority group’ models of disability. Although sometimes conflated,
L101@P2: these models are different, reflecting the different contexts in which they
L102@P2: arose.
L103@P2: Stemming from the deliberations of a group of British disability
L104@P2: activists in the 1970s and articulated most notably by Michael Oliver, the
L105@P2: social model rests on a ‘firm distinction between (biological) impairment
L106@P2: and (social) disability’. Such a distinction was not central to the minority
L107@P2: group model more popular with North American activists who drew
L108@P2: inspiration from the US civil rights tradition. This model emphasised
L109@P2: disabled people’s minority status and similarity to other marginalised
L110@P2: minorities in a deliberate attempt to link the DRM to a wider movement
L111@P2: for civil rights. Despite their differences, however, both theoretical
L112@P2: frameworks emphasise the ‘constructed’ nature of disability by shifting
L113@P2: attention away from the bodies and minds of ‘disabled’ people towards
L114@P2: the social and cultural contexts that shape perceptions and experiences
L115@P2: of human difference.7 It is this broad commitment to a socio-cultural
L116@P2: approach to bodily and cognitive non-normativity, rather than a strict
L117@P2: adherence to any particular ‘model’, that has underpinned disability
L118@P2: history from the outset. For disability historians, then, disability is
L119@P2: contingent, its meaning and existence dependent on time and place.

L137@P3: Moved by their critical perspective on the medical model and their
L138@P3: desire to effect social and political change, early disability historians often
L139@P3: distanced themselves from the social history of medicine. While both
L140@P3: fields shared similar sources, methods and themes, disability scholars
L141@P3: were suspicious of the social history of medicine’s medical focus and
L142@P3: preoccupation with ‘patients’, believing it insufficiently critical of the
L143@P3: medical model or wedded to the DRM to promote significant political
L144@P3: change.8 Disability historians consciously challenged the medical model
L145@P3: by moving beyond the realms of medicine to consider understandings
L146@P3: and experiences of bodily and cognitive differences in other contexts.
L147@P3: However, by positioning disabled people outside the medical gaze, early
L148@P3: disability historians did not seek to deny the significance of bodies
L149@P3: and minds to disabled people’s lived experiences. As Longmore and
L150@P3: Umansky put it: ‘At its best, the new disability history recognises the
L151@P3: corporeal dimension of human experience and its consequences for daily
L152@P3: functioning, while striving continually to understand the contingencies
L153@P3: that shape, reflect, express, and result from that dimension’.9
L154@P3: Since the publication of Longmore and Umansky’s observation in
L155@P3: 2001, historians have intensified their efforts to acknowledge and explore
L156@P3: the embodied aspects of disability. This has contributed to a significant
L157@P3: re-evaluation of the historical usefulness of the social model and the
L158@P3: ‘impairment/disability divide’ on which it rests.10 As a result, new
L159@P3: theoretical frameworks have emerged in the field, including the currently
L160@P3: popular ‘cultural model of disability’. This model explicitly blurs or erases
L161@P3: the distinction between impairment and disability to better recognise the
L162@P3: interconnectedness of the corporeal and social worlds. As the cultural
L163@P3: model’s name implies, however, despite innovations in the theoretical basis
L164@P3: of the field, disability history continues to stress a socio-cultural approach
L165@P3: and eschew essentialist understandings of disability.11

L167@P3: difference, disability appears similar in nature to other powerful social
L168@P3: categories, such as gender, race or class. As a result, disability historians

L197@P4: III
L198@P4: The main features of disability history can be characterised as: its political
L199@P4: impetus, its commitment to a socio-cultural approach to disability,
L200@P4: and its insistence that disability constitutes a powerful category of
L201@P4: analysis relevant to all areas of historical research. While these three
L202@P4: pillars continue to underpin the field, the practice of disability history
L203@P4: has become more varied. In recent years, as with historians of other
L204@P4: marginalised groups, disability historians have employed a widening range
L205@P4: of sources and methods and raised important ethical questions that need
L206@P4: addressing.
L207@P4: Elizabeth Bredberg has divided primary sources available to disability
L208@P4: historians into three broad types: the ‘institutional’, ‘vernacular’
L209@P4: and ‘experiential’. Institutional sources, such as asylum, school and
L210@P4: government records, generally foreground the views of ‘experts’ or
L211@P4: officials, or the priorities and concerns of the state or other organisations,
L212@P4: whereas vernacular sources, such as poems, newspapers and film, better
L213@P4: ‘reflect community or lay understandings of disability’. Experiential
L214@P4: sources, for example, diaries, memoirs and interviews, in contrast,
L215@P4: emphasise ‘personal’ or ‘first-hand accounts of disability’.13 Early
L216@P4: work in disability history drew heavily on institutional and vernacular
L217@P4: sources, and studies centring asylums, schools, social policies or cultural
L218@P4: representations were commonplace.14 Such sources remain popular
