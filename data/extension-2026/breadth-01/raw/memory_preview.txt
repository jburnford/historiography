L0@P1: Transcultural Memory
L1@P1: Memories are not static or frozen, remaining in particular sites or places, within and
L2@P1: belonging to particular groups, cultures or nations; rather, memory travels. Broadly
L3@P1: speaking, memory has travelled because of the demographic displacements brought
L4@P1: about by modernity’s extremes – slavery, colonialism, ethnic cleansing and genocide –
L5@P1: and also because of the trade, travel and migration made possible by globalisation.
L6@P1: Whether a social movement is violent, exilic, migratory, emancipatory or oppressive, it
L7@P1: is accompanied by memory. With the movement of people, memories of modernity’s
L8@P1: histories and postmodern legacies meet, correspond and often become mutually constitutive. Even where memories compete with each other for cultural dominance,
L9@P1: mutual dialogue and recognition is implicit if not explicit. Memories travel through and
L10@P1: across cultures and national boundaries, a process increasingly facilitated by mass
L11@P1: media technologies.
L12@P1: This collection explores a range of case studies of transcultural memory as well as
L13@P1: theorising the mobility of memory as it travels.
L14@P1: This book was originally published as a special issue of the journal parallax.

L120@P9: As Astrid Erll, whose essay begins this collection, argues, memory needs to travel to
L121@P9: stay alive. How and why does it travel, and how does memory studies keep pace? These
L122@P9: are the questions that contributors to this edited volume engage with and to whose
L123@P9: work this short introduction can only begin to frame.1 In recent years, memory studies
L124@P9: has travelled from the collective to the cultural to the transcultural. It is a commonplace but nonetheless useful orientation in tracing the path of memory studies to cite
L125@P9: Maurice Halbwachs’s conception of the social frames in which individual memory
L126@P9: work takes place, and of the difficulty of prizing apart individual and collective
L127@P9: remembrance. Despite the particularity of individual experience of past events, that
L128@P9: experience of the event and subsequent remembrance of it will be socially encoded,
L129@P9: and, as Halbwachs puts it, ‘the framework of collective memory confines and binds our
L130@P9: most intimate remembrances to each other’.
L131@P9: 2 The inherently social nature of remembrance means that memories can be shared, no matter our proximity to, or distance
L132@P9: from, the events remembered. As Aleida Assmann has pointed out, it is the concept of
L133@P9: ‘cultural memory’ rather than ‘collective memory’ that perhaps best explains how this
L134@P9: sharing takes place. Cultural memory describes the artefacts (texts, objects and symbols) by which memory is socialised, or, rather, by which memory is further socialised
L135@P9: and mobilised. When individual memory, social by nature, is represented artefactually,
L136@P9: it has the potential to be unmoored from social groups, and no longer necessarily
L137@P9: identifiable with or affiliated to those groups, their experiences, values, ideas and temporal horizons.3 In describing the materialisation and mobilisation of memory, the
L138@P9: concept of cultural memory illuminates the dynamic nature of the artefacts of memory,
L139@P9: their actual and potential itineraries.
L140@P9: To complicate this picture, the materials of memory may move, but, as Alon Confino
L141@P9: and Peter Fritzsche argue, memory also moves with and through them: memory is a
L142@P9: process ‘embedded in social networks’ rather than solely and statically in institutions,
L143@P9: sites, objects, texts or people.4 Memory does not inhere in its material representations
L144@P9: and traces of the past but rather flows through them and constellates them, and such
L145@P9: materials must be subject to a continual symbolic investment for them to retain their
L146@P9: memorative value, and that may entail their substitution or modification by, or convergence with, other materials and media of memory.5 Another way of putting this is
L147@P9: that the concept of cultural memory recognises the agency that mobilises and remobilises memories rather than just the trajectories, materials and media of remembrance. Wulf Kansteiner has reminded us that no matter the intentions of the makers
L148@P9-10: of the material of memory, and no matter the ostensible significance of the materialTRANSCULTURAL MEMORY 2
L149@P10: itself, and the forces behind its distribution, its significance will ultimately rest on those
L150@P10: who receive and consume it.6 Susannah Radstone, though, contextualises agency,
L151@P10: arguing elsewhere that the public articulation of memory is informed by the discourses
L152@P10: and institutions of the public sphere that mediate and authorise recollection.7 Conversely, James Young’s seminal work on Holocaust memorials, monuments and
L153@P10: museum artefacts, and the exhibitionary and commemorative narratives they constitute, suggests the ways in which institutionalised public memory is subject to a continual process of negotiation by the constituencies whose acts of memory are focused
L154@P10: but not over-determined by such objects, places and spaces – by which the meaning of
L155@P10: commemoration is dependent on such visitors and how they mobilise memory across
L156@P10: cultures.8 Two recent and related theoretical paradigms, ‘postmemory’ and ‘prosthetic
L157@P10: memory’, would account in part for such mobilisation.
L158@P10: Marianne Hirsch’s ‘postmemory’ is a self-reflexive mode of remembering trauma
L159@P10: that foregrounds the relationship of those who remember to their act of recall. Postmemory typically describes ‘the relationship of children of survivors of cultural or
L160@P10: collective trauma to the experiences of their parents, experiences that they “remember”
L161@P10: only as the narratives and images with which they grew up, but that are so powerful, so
L162@P10: monumental, as to constitute memories in their own right’. Having defined the concept
L163@P10: of postmemory in terms of ‘familial inheritance’, Hirsch has broadened its application
L164@P10: to a more general, cultural inheritance (culturally affiliative rather than familial) that
L165@P10: can transcend ethnic or national boundaries and need not necessarily be confined to
L166@P10: the Holocaust. Postmemory, therefore, is ‘defined through an identification with the
L167@P10: victim or witness of trauma, modulated by an unbridgeable distance that separates the
L168@P10: participant from the one born after ... . Postmemory would thus be retrospective witnessing by adoption. It is a question of adopting the traumatic experiences – and thus
L169@P10: also the memories – of others as experiences one might oneself have had, and of
L170@P10: inscribing them into one’s own life story’. It is the belated nature of traumatic memory
L171@P10: that fuels its transmission and adoption. If the traumatic nature of the event defies its
L172@P10: own witnessing, cognition and remembrance, then, for Hirsch, it makes sense that the
L173@P10: next generation is in a position to work through traumatic experience and its symptoms, narratives and images bequeathed but not fully remembered or known by the
L174@P10: previous one. A highly or hyper- mediated form of memory, it is reconstructed from the
L175@P10: textual traces of the past (because the witness generation often bequeaths silence). The
L176@P10: act of reconstruction affords the opportunity for a potentially culturally dislocated,
L177@P10: critical distance on the past – its working through – rather than just an acting out of a
L178@P10: perceived trauma.9
L179@P10: In Alison Landsberg’s influential concept ‘prosthetic’ memories are enabled by the
L180@P10: modern technologies of mass cultural communication and by the ways that mass culture is commodified; remembrance takes place in ‘experiential sites’ produced by commodification and technologisation, such as the museum and cinema. The interface with
L181@P10: historical narratives made possible at these sites generates memory in consumers of
L182@P10: events not directly experienced: experiences with which they would not necessarily
L183@P10: identify, or experiences that are culturally, socially and historically associated with
L184@P10: particularities of identities not their own. The particularities of identity (gendered,

L187@P11: The idea of prosthetic memory, then, rejects the notion that all memories – and, by
L188@P11: extension, the identities that those memories sustain – are necessarily and substantively shaped by lived social context. Prosthetic memories are not ‘socially
L189@P11: constructed’ in that they do not emerge as the result of living and being raised in
L190@P11: particular social frameworks.
L191@P11: Given their mass distribution and circulation in a commodified culture, prosthetic
L192@P11: memories thereby resist ownership and naturalisation as belonging to (or as part of the
L193@P11: heritage) of one particular individual or group. Prosthetic remembrance, then, resists
L194@P11: what are in effect biological or essentialist claims over memory made by individuals or
L195@P11: groups in the course of their self-identification. Prosthetic memory builds on Benedict
L196@P11: Anderson’s concept of ‘imagined communities’ that were enabled by mass cultural
L197@P11: technologies of the nineteenth century, and, as discussed above, on Halbwachs theorisation that social frameworks are the instruments by which collective memory is
L198@P11: formed and expressed. Where the models of Anderson and Halbwachs are centripetal,
L199@P11: describing the way in which group or national identity is shored up by the collective
L200@P11: memory of events not directly experienced. Landsberg’s, in a realisation of the potential
L201@P11: of cultural memory akin to that identified by Assmann, is centrifugal in its dispersal of
L202@P11: memory beyond group ownership.10
L203@P11: Memories may be appropriated across cultural boundaries, or cultures may correspond or dialogue over matters of memory, but to what extent are cultures reconstituted in the process? Halbwachs, Assmann, Hirsch, and Landsberg account for the
L204@P11: centrifugal movement of memory, but we should not forget that the dynamism of cultural memory, its artefacts, context or mise-en-scène of remembrance, and memorative
L205@P11: agents makes it potentially centripetal as well, instrumental in shoring up group
L206@P11: memory. In such cases, wider participation in memory, the appropriation of memory,
L207@P11: suggests a kind of additive and assimilationist model. The powerful political realities of
L208@P11: such theories and practices of cultural memory cannot be ignored – as in Pierre Nora’s
L209@P11: formulation of lieux de mémoire by which national memory is hypostatised and imagined as preserved and crystallised in particular texts, sites or realms.11 However, the
L210@P11: idea of the transcultural is useful here in that it resists ideologies and politics that
L211@P11: would homogenise cultural memory and naturalise it as such, revealing instead not just
L212@P11: the ideological and political contingencies at play, but the inherently transcultural
L213@P11: nature of cultural memory.

L216@P11: mémoire’. The idea of knotted memory is useful here in that it points to a fundamental
L217@P11: restructuring of so-called national memory, and moves beyond Nora’s idea that under
L218@P11: the umbrella of ‘nation’ there may be a series of localised, coherent group memories.
L219@P11: This logic lends itself to, for example, a core-periphery structure in which metropolitan
L220@P11: memory stands impermeable to, and aloof from, the memories of the colonised.
L221@P11-12: Instead ‘“knotted”’ remembrances in ‘all places and acts of memory are rhizomaticTRANSCULTURAL MEMORY 4

L223@P12: (whether at the local or the national level) and identitarian reduction. Peformances of
L224@P12: memory may well be territorializing or identity-forming effects, but those effects will be
L225@P12: contingent and open to re-signification.’

L227@P12: Gilroy’s work in his reformulation of memory, and it is worth dwelling on Gilroy’s

L230@P12: Gilroy’s conception of diaspora allows a mapping and tracking of memory where
L231@P12: models of national memory would otherwise contain and homogenise remembrance
L232@P12: within impermeable cultural boundaries. In short, for Gilroy diasporic thinking opposes and problematizes the alignment of race, culture and nation, or put another way,
L233@P12: the racialization of national culture in which territory and territorial origins determine
L234@P12: (emplaced) identity and the consciousness of belonging, in which the boundaries of
L235@P12: culture are those of ethnicity, and in which a ‘common’ memory is invoked, as if it
L236@P12: were not politically contingent, to buttress this particularisation.13 The concept of diaspora instead helps identify and analyse transcultural ‘relational networks’ that are
L237@P12: formed through the forced dispersal and relocation of peoples. Though that memory
L238@P12: work may trace origins, and the violence that took place there and the violence of exile,
L239@P12: the turbulent and highly contingent cultural hybridisation, fraternisation and creolisation produced by the conditions of diaspora means that memory work is truly deterritorised; remembrance can neither be assimilated to a homogenising national culture,
L240@P12: nor can it be practised outside of it, as if an impermeable cultural boundary separated
L241@P12: and homogenised margin and centre. Furthermore by this deterritorialising logic the
L242@P12: remembrance of an originary loss cannot produce a kind of cultural reversal, a resort
L243@P12: of origins, no matter how traumatic, that essentialises identity and group memory and

L245@P12: evolution of diasporic identity – ‘beyond the stark dualism of genealogy and geography’ – and expressed through and informed by that mutable identity is memory

L247@P12: movement and complex, dynamic variations’.

L254@P12: with Michael Rothberg’s paradigm-shifting intervention in (trans)cultural memory

L259@P12: predominant memory at the expense of others. The competitive version of memory is
L260@P12: underpinned by a belief in a direct correspondence between memory and identity, as if
L261@P12: memories own identities and identities own memories. In contrast, the public sphere
L262@P12: must be rethought as an infinitely malleable and productive space that can accommodate the proliferation of memories and identities, and which can accommodate the
L263@P12-13: inherent nature of memory itself. Just as ‘memory captures simultaneously theTRANSCULTURAL MEMORY 5

L293@P13: That asymmetry of memory is key to thinking transculturally about memory’s
L294@P13: movements – for memory studies has been cautious about the slippage between the
