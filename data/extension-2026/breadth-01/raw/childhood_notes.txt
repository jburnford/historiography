L152: It is sociologists, anthropologists and philosophers of childhood, rather than historians, who have often most thoughtfully considered age as an intersectional category of analysis.
L153: This work is sometimes grouped together under the label of ‘childhood studies’.cite76†62 Rachel Rosen and Katherine Twamley argue that modern feminism can often be ‘adultist’ in its unquestioning use of the category of ‘the child’, not examining the ‘ways in which certain humans are made into children and others into adults’.cite77†63 In the same edited collection, Ohad Zehavi suggests that we should move away from the liberal model of the independent self, recognising that children are not completely dependent on others and also that dependence is not automatically abandoned once we are legally defined as ‘adults’.cite78†64 This is perhaps especially relevant for disabled adults, who, as Patrick McKearney has shown, may have difficulty meeting traditional milestones of modern ‘adulthood’, but also highlights how all adults ultimately remain interdependent.cite79†65 Having said this, however, scholars in childhood studies - many of whom do historical work themselves - would also benefit from closer dialogue with historians of childhood.
L154: Historians have perhaps been reluctant to challenge the adult/child binary because they believe that, unlike other power relationships, this one is not inherently oppressive, and indeed, may be necessary for the protection of children.cite80†66 This taps into a twentieth- and twenty-first-century sociological and anthropological literature that argues that modern children now have too much power and privilege, although they are also endangered because they are being forced to grow up too fast.cite81†67 Anthropologist David Lancy argues that the modern United States is now a ‘neontocracy’ rather than a gerontocracy because ‘kids rule’, suggesting that ‘the child's wellbeing is paramount; its needs trump the desires of other family members’.cite82†68 This echoes Neil Postman's The Disappearance of Childhood (1982), where he argued that childhood was being eroded because children now have free access to information through new forms of media, which challenges the necessary authority of adults.cite83†69 Interestingly, in the preface to the 1994 edition, Postman quoted the reactions of some fifth and sixth graders [ten to twelve year olds] to this book.
L155: These readers were acutely aware that childhood is not so easy to escape or lose, with one writing ‘I think your essay wasn't very good. Childhood doesn't disappear – snap! – like that’.cite84†70 L156: The removal of childhood from certain groups has been seen by scholars as a form of oppression, which, again, frames childhood as a privilege.
L157: For example, the historian of slavery Wilma King explored how childhood was ‘stolen’ from enslaved black children.cite85†71 More recent analyses of slavery and childhood have nuanced this interpretation but still argue that slavery harmfully cut childhood short.cite86†72 In the modern United States, it has been convincingly shown that black children are ‘adultified’, denied the protections of juvenile justice and sentenced more harshly than their white counterparts.cite87†73 However, as Toby Rollo has recently argued, we can recognise the impact of adultification on black children without asserting that childhood itself is a privileged state that we should fight to preserve, as ‘in no era has childhood proven to be a genuine site of security or safety’.cite88†74 If historians of childhood fully adopt age as a category of historical analysis, we are well placed to explore these tensions, and to challenge simplistic, nostalgic assertions about how childhood ought to be.
L158: ## V
L159: To conclude, three linked but separate reflections. First, one thing that might help historians address our three questions is to consider how children themselves write and talk about age.
L160: According to black feminist ‘standpoint’ theory, oppressed groups can access unique insights into the way that their own oppression functions.cite89†75 Modern children are the social group most often asked to think explicitly about age-categories, as they were and are constantly asked ‘how old are you?’ or ‘what do you want to be when you grow up?’.cite90†76 Children have historically been socialised to regard those who are younger than them as inferior, and adulthood as an aspirational state, even if they may also anticipate it rather unwillingly.cite91†77 Therefore, children's testimony about age – whether that is written, spoken or expressed through their silences or through their play – should be a central part of making sense of this category of historical analysis.cite92†78 L161: Second, children are ‘future-orientated’ in that they are rarely seen as full humans in their own right, but as moving towards adulthood, when they will attain this status.
L162: They also serve as – often oppressive – symbols of the future in adult discourse.cite93†79 While childhood studies, in alliance with queer theory, has sought to challenge this understanding of children, it still often persists unquestioned in historical work on childhood.cite94†80 Using Stephen Kern's term, Nancy Lesko calls this ‘expectant time’, capturing the sense of being both continually progressing and suspended in place, like objects on a factory production line.cite95†81 Discussing the particular experience of black childhood in the United States, Jacob Breslow suggests that black children are ‘out of time’ in a way that white children are not.
L163: Dominant discourses do not see them as truly children, but because they have never had a childhood, they cannot progress to being adults.cite96†82 Ruby Lal identifies a similar kind of temporal suspension for ‘girl-children’ in nineteenth-century India, who were unable ever to achieve adulthood.cite97†83 In this context, children's own accounts of childhood are especially valuable, because they were composed while they still existed in this liminal space.
L164: Finally, I contend that as long as adulthood is still positioned historically as a neutral, static norm, we will not be able to fully understand childhood. Age, like gender, is a fundamentally relational category, but most current historical work does not consider how the experience of being ‘young’ is shaped in response to what it means to be ‘older’. While histories of adulthood are beginning to emerge, they often lack critical perspective.
L165: Some simply add another life-stage to the existing litany, thinking about ‘middle age’ or the ‘midlife crisis’, while not considering how these stages are also constructed against healthy adulthood.cite98†84 Others claim to historicise adulthood while actually reinscribing a sequence of modern stereotypes about what it means to be ‘grown up’, connecting adulthood to life-cycle markers such as marriage, paid employment and home ownership.cite99†85 Once again, it is other disciplines who have done the most useful theoretical work – this time, the literary scholars Sari Edelstein, Claudia Nelson and Teresa Michals – although historical analyses of modern adulthood are not entirely absent.cite100†86 L166: We began with Lloyd deMause's teleological statement about our progress away from the horrors of the past, but historians of childhood must not subscribe to such reductive ideas about how we as adults have made childhood better for children. Instead, we must acknowledge our own complicity in a fundamentally ageist society and challenge the ways in which children are structurally oppressed.

L266:   * Laura Tisdall is a NUAcT Fellow in History at Newcastle University. Her publications include A Progressive Education? How Childhood Changed in Mid-Twentieth-Century English and Welsh Schools (Manchester, 2020) alongside journal articles on children in care in inter-war England, post-war education and parenting, and depictions of childhood in British horror and science fiction.
L267: Her current project, funded by the Leverhulme Trust, uses British young people's contemporary writing from c.1945 to c.1989 to examine how children and teenagers understood adulthood, youth and chronological age, and how this reflected a wider reconceptualization of both childhood and adulthood in Cold War Britain.
