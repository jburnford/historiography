## 1. John Foster, "The Declassing of Language", *New Left Review* I/150 (March–April 1985)

**Read status: read in full** on NLR's own site (https://newleftreview.org/issues/i150/articles/john-foster-the-declassing-of-language; full text open; no page numbers in the web edition, so passages are located by the article's section headings: untitled opening, "A New Agenda", "The Language of Chartism", "Post-war Changes", "Political Consequences", "Saussure and Volosinov", "The 1842 General Strike", "A Declassing of Language"). Epstein (1986, n. 14) cites it as NLR 150, pp. 29–45 [the "38–41" he cites for the linguistic-theory critique; the article's page range is not shown on the NLR page]. Foster's footnotes give the *Languages of Class* page numbers for every quotation, which are reused below as "LoC p. x (as quoted by Foster)".

**Author.** John Foster, author of *Class Struggle and the Industrial Revolution* (1974), the book Stedman Jones reviewed in NLR I/90 (1975), reprinted as chapter 1 of *Languages of Class*. The 1985 review is therefore an answer by a former subject of Stedman Jones's criticism; Foster does not say so in the text.

**What Foster takes the book to claim (opening; "A New Agenda").**
- "The novelty of Gareth Stedman Jones's Languages of Class lies, as its title suggests, in its contention that the study of language must be the starting point for any understanding of political activity."
- Historians have set fragments of speech "into direct relation to a putative experiential reality of which they were assumed to be the expression" (LoC p. 21, as quoted by Foster). "A reductionist history has bred a reductionist political practice."
- The programme, quoted from the introduction (LoC p. 22, as quoted by Foster): "What we must do is study the production of interest, identification, grievance and aspiration within political languages themselves. We need to map out these successive languages of radicalism, liberalism, socialism, etc., both in relation to the political languages they replace and laterally in relation to rival political languages with which they are in conflict … To peer straight through these languages into structural changes to which they may be notionally referred is no substitute for such an investigation, not because there is not a relationship of some kind, but because such connection can never be established with a satisfying degree of finality."
- "Rethinking Chartism" "proposes that Chartism was no more nor less than what it claimed to be: an alliance of forces with strictly political and constitutional objectives"; radicalism's "vocabulary of exclusion" (LoC p. 104) "was specifically cross-class and socially inclusive"; "There was no great betrayal. Chartism was what it was: a movement for parliamentary reform." Chartism's collapse is dated to Peel's administration, "1841–43", when liberalism "became the language of government".
- On the Labour Party essay: the party as "a vacant centre" (LoC p. 22); class consciousness in Britain "a 'conservative rather than revolutionary phenomenon'" (LoC p. 246).

**Stedman Jones on Thompson, as Foster reports it ("Political Consequences").** "Stedman Jones sees himself as taking forward the redefinition of class initiated by Thompson, whose 'great achievement' is to have 'freed the concept of class consciousness from any simple reduction to the development of productive forces … and to have linked it to the development of a political movement which cannot be reduced to the terminology of incoherent protest'. However, Thompson's approach still assumes a 'relatively direct relationship between "social being" and "social consciousness" which leaves little independent space to the ideological context within which the coherence of a particular language of class can be reconstituted'" (LoC p. 101, as quoted by Foster). "According to Stedman Jones, precedence must be given to 'the interposition of a particular language which organizes the understanding of experience'." Foster also quotes the introduction's Saussurean passage: "the materiality of language itself, the impossibility of referring it back to some primal anterior reality, 'social being', the impossibility of abstracting experience from the language which structures its articulation" (LoC p. 20); and the phrase "non-referential conception of language" (LoC p. 22).

**Foster's objections (own words).**
- Theory: "the book's very undeveloped theoretical introduction"; "we are told rather grandly, and without the aid of any reference, that Ferdinand de Saussure is the man to know"; no engagement with Labov, Bernstein, Wittgenstein, or "the Soviet school: Vygotsky, Volosinov, Luria and Leontiev". Against Saussure he sets Volosinov's "multi-accentuality of the sign" and Luria on concepts changing with social relations, and Marx's *Capital* on technology and "the mental conceptions that flow from those relations".
- Method: "The great danger of this approach is, of course, subjectivism. The 'language' of radicalism, as defined by Stedman Jones, is purely a historian's construct … reductionism may take an idealist as well as economic form." The radical/economic–political distinction "depends for its validity on a sharp conceptual distinction between the 'economic' and 'political' spheres"; Foster turns Stedman Jones's 1975 essay against him: "it is difficult to separate political and economic demands in the 1830s and '40s because working-class leaders themselves rarely did so" (LoC pp. 58–9, as quoted by Foster).
- Evidence: on the 1842 general strike (Jenkins), Stedman Jones "should have ignored the most important and relevant material"; the 1843 trial shows liberalism's victory "derived from a calculated and specific use of state power", "not simply because of some organic flaw in the language of radicalism".
- Politics: "Languages of Class does directly involve an intellectual declassing of our understanding of language"; "Stedman Jones, entirely logically, dismisses the trade union movement"; "Today, it would lead socialists to withdraw from the social practice that generates class consciousness." He aligns the book's politics with *Marxism Today* and Stuart Hall ("It is unclear whether Stedman Jones takes exactly this position").
- Verdict: "Languages of Class is a book which demands discussion. It is eloquent, plausible and, as with all Stedman Jones's work, enriched with an enviable depth of historical erudition. But its assumptions … are quite unlike those of Stedman Jones's previous work or of the more general tradition of Marxist historiography in Britain. The great problem is that these new assumptions are not argued."

**Edge use.** A named critique, in the critic's own words and read in full: supports `languages_of_class → gareth_stedman_jones` (critique, Foster named) and the strand `first_critics_1985_87`. Also supplies, second-hand but with pages, the introduction's statements on Thompson (p. 101 is in "Rethinking Chartism", not the introduction) and on "experience" (pp. 20–22), to be confirmed against the Google Books preview (§3). Foster's remark that "the debate on Chartism's class content was initiated by the publication of Thompson's Making" dates the lineage.

## 2. James Epstein, "Rethinking the Categories of Working-Class History", *Labour/Le Travail* 18 (Fall 1986): 195–208

**Read status: read in full** in the journal's open PDF on Érudit (https://www.erudit.org/fr/revues/llt/1986-v18-llt_18/llt18re01.pdf; https://id.erudit.org/iderudit/llt18re01; printed page numbers 195–208 confirmed from the running heads). Review essay on *Languages of Class* (cited "Cambridge 1983"). Epstein thanks Cynthia Herrup, Paul Krause, William Reddy and Joseph White (p. 208). He co-edited *The Chartist Experience* (1982) with Dorothy Thompson and wrote *The Lion of Freedom* (1982), both cited in his notes.

**Reading of the book (pp. 195–196).** "The author's increasing dissatisfaction with the explanatory force of the Marxist notion of class provides a unifying theme to the book" (p. 195). "What the concepts 'consciousness' and 'experience,' often employed to mediate the relationship between the social and the political, conceal is 'the problematic character of language.' Language cannot be understood as merely providing the medium through which 'experience' finds expression. Invoking the authority of Saussure, Stedman Jones notes the materiality of language itself … it is impossible to abstract 'experience' 'from the language that structures its articulation.' (19–20) This volume charts the author's progress towards dissociating 'the ambition of a theoretically informed history from any simple prejudgement about the determining role of the "social" as something outside of, and logically prior to its articulation through language.' (7)" (pp. 195–196). Only the two 1982 essays ("Rethinking Chartism" and "Why Is the Labour Party in a Mess?") rest on the new approach; the volume "underscores a more general crisis in meaning that pervades cultural and historical studies" (p. 196), with Sewell, Hunt and Wilentz cited in n. 2.

**On "Rethinking Chartism" (pp. 196–199).** Stedman Jones "registers his surprise that historians have continued to adopt an essentially 'social' perspective"; the cost is "the general neglect of the specific political and ideological form within which this mass discontent was expressed and the consequent tendency to elide the Chartist language of class with a range of Marxist or sociological notions of class consciousness" (LoC 93–4, as quoted by Epstein, p. 196). Epstein calls the essay "the most sustained and coherent descriptive analysis yet of formal Chartist ideology" (p. 196), notes its consonance with "recent revisionist work, particularly that of Dorothy Thompson and Iorwerth Prothero" (p. 197), and summarises: radicalism was "a 'vocabulary of political exclusion whatever the social character of those excluded,' and as such 'it could never be the ideology of a specific class'" (LoC 104, p. 197); "there was no independent rationale within radical ideology for antagonism towards the middle class as such" (LoC 107, p. 198); "it should not … be assumed that the radical analysis that lay behind the Charter was in the course of displacement by a different and more class conscious mode of thought" (LoC 153, p. 198); the Petition and Convention "were not premised upon proletarian politics" (LoC 161, p. 199); the state "as the tyrannical harbinger of a dictatorship over the producers … As a conjunctural phenomenon, Chartism represented the rapid upsurge and gradual ebbing away of this specific vision of the state" (LoC 173–4, p. 199). "From the long-term perspective, Chartism appears less as the culmination of the 'making of the English working class' and more as the impressive last manifestation of a radical political tradition stretching back to the eighteenth century" (p. 199).

**Epstein's objections (pp. 199–204).**
- "the argument never really confronts the question of what constitutes Chartist 'language.'" Stedman Jones restricts himself: "'What is examined here is only the public political language of the movement.' (95) Such a restricted concept of language, however, deprives us of certain meanings, particularly meanings imparted through symbolic action and contest" — the mass platform, torchlight meetings, the Ashton mill fire of December 1838 (pp. 199–200).
- "Most significantly, Stedman Jones fails to acknowledge fully the essential instability of the terms of public political discourse" — "the people" contested between Chartists and Brougham: "Surely here is a struggle to appropriate … the meaning of a key sign … There seems little reason not to regard this as a form of class struggle" (p. 200). "Stedman Jones' emphasis on the relatively autonomous force of language, and the continuities of linguistic forms, underestimates the intense conflict that often surrounds the social construction of language. Whether this conflict merits the preface 'class' requires an approach that goes beyond the formalism of 'Rethinking Chartism'" (p. 201). Pocock's "multivalency" and Cunningham's "language of patriotism" (HWJ 12) are cited (n. 11–12).
- The term "false consciousness": to move from the absence of socialist theory "to the notion that the movement was not a 'class' movement … seems to whisper gently 'false consciousness, comrades.' Presumably this is not Stedman Jones' intention" (p. 202). He records Stedman Jones's own caveat: "His intent is not to replace a social interpretation with a linguistic interpretation, 'rather it is how the two relate, that must be rethought.' (95) It is unclear, however, how his 'non-referential conception of language' (22) can accommodate the dynamic interplay between changing social relations and political language" (p. 202; n. 14 refers to Foster 1985, pp. 38–41, "for a critique of Stedman Jones' use of linguistic theory"). He points to Behagg on Birmingham and to Stedman Jones's own 1974/75 essay on unequal exchange and the London tailor (pp. 202–203).
- On the state: "By directing attention to the changing visage of the state, Stedman Jones makes a major advance towards understanding the erosion of this integrated vision" (p. 203); but "by treating the state more or less autonomously, Stedman Jones ignores the range of accommodative strategies pursued within civil society" (p. 204); 1848 showed a "Janus-faced" liberalized state (p. 204).

**On the London essay and the Labour essay (pp. 204–208).** The 1974 London essay is "justly influential" and points to "the late nineteenth century as a founding moment"; he quotes "impermeability … no longer threatening or subversive, but conservative and defensive" (LoC 215) and "Capitalism had become an immovable horizon" (LoC 237) (pp. 204–205). Of "Why Is the Labour Party in a Mess?": class consciousness in Britain "a conservative rather than a revolutionary phenomenon … the consciousness of the separateness of a caste rather than that of the hegemonic potentialities of a particular position in production" (LoC 246–7, p. 205); "The terms 'conservative' and 'revolutionary' are blunt instruments of analysis" (p. 205). Hobsbawm's "forward march" called "an optical illusion … part of the social democratic mythology of Labour in the 1940s" and Labour's history "a series of 'discontinuous conjunctures'" (LoC 243, p. 206); 1945–51 as "the last and most glorious flowering of late Victorian liberal philanthropy" (LoC 246), which Epstein finds "in danger of being reduced to a platitude of new left revisionism" (p. 207). He places the essay in "a more general appeal associated with History Workshop for a new kind of history of the Labour Party and socialism" (p. 207, n. 22: the HWJ 12 editorial, and Samuel and Stedman Jones in *Culture, Ideology and Politics*, 1982).

**Verdict (p. 208).** "Stedman Jones is among the most gifted intellectuals on the British left … One of the great strengths of these essays is the sustained critique of the teleological aspects of Marxist theory … Stedman Jones also poses in most powerful terms the difficult question of the ordering of the relationship between 'social being' and 'social consciousness.' The exact role of language within this relationship (both determining and determined, producing and produced) remains problematic."

**Edge use.** A named critique with agreement stated: supports `languages_of_class → gareth_stedman_jones` (Epstein named) and roster row for Epstein (critic, with the Dorothy Thompson collection as context). It also confirms, with pages, the introduction's "experience" passage (LoC 19–20), the p. 7 statement, and p. 95 ("how the two relate … must be rethought"), which the atlas's existing `stedman_chartism_primary` note already records as "Page 95 explicitly rejects replacing social with linguistic interpretation".
