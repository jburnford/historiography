### A4. *Visions of the People: Industrial England and the Question of Class, 1848–1914* (CUP, 1991)
**Read status: publisher page and contents read (Cambridge Core); chapter openings read for the Introduction (pp. 1–24), ch. 3, ch. 4 and ch. 14 (Cambridge chapter summaries via OpenAlex); body unread; further passages known with page numbers as quoted by Tilly (1994).**
- Cambridge Core (read 2026-10-22): "a study of how the labouring poor of nineteenth-century industrial England saw the social order"; the book "attacks orthodoxies and sets up new questions" across politics, work, language and art. Print date shown 25 January 1991 (paperback ISBN 9780521447973 listed; digital 5 March 2012). Contents: 1 "Introduction: beyond class?"; Part I "Power and the People: Politics and the Social Order" (2 "The languages of popular politics: from radicalism to Liberalism"; 3 "Class, populism and socialism: Liberalism and after"); Part II "Moralising the Market: Work and the Social Order" (4 "Civilising capital: class and the moral discourses of labour"; 5 "Building the union"); Part III "Custom, History, Language: Popular Culture and the Social Order" (6–8, incl. "The people's English"); Part IV "Kingdoms of the Mind: The Imaginary Constitution of the Social Order" (9–13, broadside ballads, dialect literature, popular theatre); 14 "Summary and conclusion: the making of the English working class before 1914?" (pp. 329–342); appendices of documents (Stephens, Grime, Ernest Jones, Bright, Gladstone).
- Introduction opening (p. 1): "Until relatively recently, 'class' in British history was a settled matter … the work of E. P. Thompson and E. J. Hobsbawm remained, and remains, central, fixing the historical sequence of class development. Thompson's enormously influential *The Making of the English Working Class* presents a picture of class consciousness as substantially 'made' by around 1830 … Hobsbawm looks to the consolidation of industrial capitalism in the late nineteenth century … This has now taken the form of received wisdom. However, while there is no denying that class was a child of the nineteenth century, when it comes to how the social order was represented and understood, there were other children too who were every bit as lusty as class – indeed, in many respects stronger and more fully part of their time. Received wisdom has in fact become a dead weight, the fixation with class denying us sight of these other visions of the social order."
- Ch. 3 opening (p. 56 ff.; "Managing class"): Gladstone's "crusade of the 'masses' against the 'classes'"; "The 'negative' and divisive aspects of class were anathematised, by all classes. Popular Liberalism, and in fact popular Toryism as well, were about the union of the classes against class."
- Ch. 4 opening: "If class was a somewhat elusive presence in the political sphere then commonsense would tell us that in the world of work it found its true home … This was so, but only partly so. It represented one among many tendencies of development, and was not as single or overarching as it is so often taken to be."
- Ch. 14 opening (p. 329): "rather than a single populism, it is a variety or 'family' of populisms that should be considered"; in the industrial north "a radical populism conceiving of the true England as the industrial north in struggle with Privilege"; "The controlling narrative of popular politics appears to have concerned a righteous and dispossessed 'people' rather than a 'working class'"; "in this sense 'class' appropriations occurred, even though this was in practice rather limited."
- As quoted by Tilly (1994, pp. 263–265, with Joyce's pages): p. 4, "if class 'position' is not considered in the light of the very problematic nature of proletarianisation, then one is led to ask in what respect is the phenomenon to hand a matter of 'working-class consciousness' … rather than cultural and political traditions per se, or extra-proletarian identifications such as 'the people'"; p. 8, "Because manual workers chose to wear cloth caps and support football teams, it does not follow that they saw the social order in terms of class" (against Hobsbawm); p. 11, "the sense of class is defined in relation to, and usually over and against, other classes"; pp. 14, 96, 332, Jameson via James Epstein on "two opposed discourses fight it out within the general unity of a shared code"; p. 331, "it is better to talk of a 'master narrative' rather than of a 'master identity'"; p. 332, "an excluded and virtuous people doing battle in its pilgrim's progress against the forces of privilege, faction, darkness and ignorance."
- **Reception (dated).** Cannadine, "Cutting Classes", NYRB 17 Dec. 1992 (unread; known through the 8 April 1993 letters); Joyce's letter (read): Cannadine's review ended by calling for "a history of status" rather than "a history of class" or "a history of language"; Cannadine "fails to understand what is really behind the 'linguistic turn'"; the categories of populism "are important precisely for their plasticity not despite it"; populism is a way to "open up a neglected area, a history of social unities"; "A history of unities will at once be a history of difference"; he has "grown shy of the grand narratives modernism, especially liberalism, has so long and uncritically dealt in." Cannadine's reply (read): the letter is "disappointingly predictable"; "Status is merely a 'rhetorical construction'"; "Class is rejected because it is a 'grand concept'." Tilly, "Softcore Solipsism", L/LT 34 (Fall 1994): 259–268 (read in full, §A16). Kirk 1994 (metadata). Vernon 1993, p. 297: identities "are not done justice by Patrick Joyce's 'family of populisms'" (as quoted by Tilly, p. 266). Lawrence 1998 (contents read; the Introduction's treatment of Joyce unread). Eley and Nield 2007 (unread). The AHR (1992, 10.2307/2165572) and JIH (1992, 10.2307/205295) reviews: metadata.

### A5. *Democratic Subjects: The Self and the Social in Nineteenth-Century England* (CUP, 1994)
**Read status: publisher description and chapter openings read (Cambridge via OpenAlex); body unread.**
- Description: "explores critically the nature of class identity by looking at the formation and influence of two men (Edwin Waugh and John Bright) who might be taken as representative of what 'working class' and 'middle class' meant in England in the nineteenth century. The two studies of individuals are complemented by a further study on narrative … The book indicates the way forward to a new history of democracy as an imagined entity. It represents a deepening of Patrick Joyce's engagement with 'post-modernist' theory, seeking the relevance of this theory for the writing of history, and in the process offering a critique of the conservatism of much academic history, particularly in Britain."
- Chapters: "The sorrows of Edwin Waugh: a study in 'working-class' identity"; "John Bright and the English people: a study in 'middle-class' identity"; "Democratic romances: narrative as collective identity in nineteenth-century England" (opens with Linda Orr, *Headless History*: "le peuple is a metaphor … for the social referent itself"); "Narrative and history" (pp. 153–160) opens by quoting Somers and Gibson on "the shift from a focus on representational to ontological narrativity … social life is itself storied and … narrative is an ontological condition of social life".
- Reviews: AHR 101 (1996) (10.2307/2170457) and EcHR 49:2 (1996): 399 (reviewer surname Hall; 10.2307/2597941): metadata only.

### A6. Patrick Joyce, ed., *Class* (Oxford Readers, OUP, 1995; ISBN 9780192892522)
**Read status: contents read (Crossref ISBN listing, 60 items with pages); Joyce's Introduction (pp. 3–16) and section introductions (19–20, 71–73, 183–185, 233–235, 287–291) unread; the opening paragraphs of Joyce's two own extracts read.**
- The reader's architecture is itself evidence of Joyce's 1995 position: after Marx/Engels, Weber, Tönnies, Crompton, Wood ("An Inheritance Reaffirmed: Marx"), it moves through Bauman, Touraine, Baudrillard ("The End of the Social", pp. 90–94), Haraway, Bourdieu, Giddens, Castoriadis ("The Social Imaginary"), Thompson ("Class and Class Struggle", 133–141; "Culture and Structure: Social History Orthodoxy", 131–132), Katznelson ("Levels of Class Formation", 142–149), Stedman Jones ("The 'Linguistic Turn': Class, 'Experience', and Politics", 150–153), Scott ("Language, Gender, and Working-Class History", 154–160), Joyce ("A People and a Class", 161–166), Reid and Rancière, Sewell ("A Post-Materialist Rhetoric for Labour History", 174–180), Eley ("Habermas and the 'Public Sphere'"), **Foucault ("Governmentality", 201–203), Gordon and Burchell ("The Emergence of Liberal Governmentality"), Donzelot ("The Mobilization of Society"), Rose ("Towards a Critical Sociology of Freedom", 213–224), Riley ("Gendering 'the Social'", 225–229)**, Roberts, Hoggart, Martin, Moore, Cottereau, Burke, Wiener, Davidoff and Hall, Morris, Joyce ("Narratives of Class", 322–331), Sewell ("Meanings of Class"), Waites, McKibbin ("The State and the Language of Class", 343–347).
- "Narratives of Class" opening (p. 322): "Recent research on the vocabulary of class suggests that this did not emanate chiefly from the new economic conditions and outlook of rapid industrialisation. The political context seems to have been crucial … Dror Wahrman argues that the terms of class had by the 1810s become directed against radicalism … Class identities were, therefore, a product of arguments about meanings, arguments which were primarily political in character. Class does not seem to have been the collective cultural experience of new economic classes produced by the Industrial Revolution."
- Use: the inclusion of Foucault's "Governmentality", Gordon, Burchell, Rose and Donzelot in a 1995 reader on class, beside Baudrillard's "End of the Social" and Riley's "Gendering 'the Social'", is the earliest documentary sign of the governmentality turn that *The Rule of Freedom* (2003) completed. It supports `foucault → patrick_joyce` at moderate strength even without the introduction's text.

### A10. Patrick Joyce, ed., *The Social in Question: New Bearings in History and the Social Sciences* (Routledge, 2002)
**Read status: publisher description read (Manchester Research Explorer record via OpenAlex); Joyce's introduction unread; Latour's chapter already in the atlas (`w3_latour_tarde_2002`).**
- "With postmodernism has come the questioning of the very idea of 'the social'. Thinkers from across the social sciences and humanities now agree that this one foundational concept can no longer be taken for granted as an objective or real characteristic of the world … Drawn from sociology, cultural studies, history and theology, an international and eminent cast of contributors look at how the idea of 'the social' developed from its mediaeval foundations to its consolidation in the early twentieth century. The book then charts how the concept has been brought into question by critiques from science studies, cultural studies and postcolonial studies before going on to look at how new frameworks are being proposed".
- Latour's chapter (read for 1.151): ANT as "a deliberate attempt at terminating the use of the word 'social' in social theory to replace it with the word 'association'". Joyce chose to print it; that is editorial endorsement, not yet his own words.

### A11. *The Rule of Freedom: Liberalism and the Modern City* (Verso, 2003)
**Read status: publisher description read (Verso page, 2026-10-22); body unread.**
- "The liberal governance of the nineteenth-century state and city depended on the 'rule of freedom.' As a form of rule it relied on the production of certain kinds of citizens and patterns of social life, which in turn depended on transforming both the material form of the city (its layout, architecture, infrastructure) and the ways it was inhabited and imagined … Focusing mainly on London and Manchester … Patrick Joyce creatively and originally develops Foucauldian approaches to historiography to reflect on the nature of modern liberal society. His consideration of such artifacts as maps and censuses, sewers and markets, public libraries and parks … examine both the impersonal, often invisible forms of social direction and control built into the infrastructure of modern life and the ways in which these mechanisms both shape culture and social life and engender popular resistance." 292 pp.; April 2003 paperback date shown. Blurbs: Nikolas Rose ("amongst the most inventive and rigorous social historians writing today"), David Vincent.
- Reviews: Deborah Cohen, AHR 109:1 (2004): 151–152 (10.1086/ahr/109.1.151); Larry Frohman, CJH 39:2 (2004): 448–450 (10.3138/cjh.39.2.448): **metadata only**.

### A12. "What is the Social in Social History?", *Past & Present* 206 (Feb. 2010): 213–248 (10.1093/pastj/gtp030)
**Read status: opening paragraph read (publisher-exposed, p. 213); the rest unread (OUP and Edinburgh deposit both 403).**
