- Cambridge's book description (paraphrased): essays on how historians have conceived of "class" and how class has figured in English politics and culture since the 1830s; the English working class presented as the empirical basis of Marxist theories of industrial society and revolution and of later historians' answers to them; it separates the everyday sense that class is pervasive from the Marxist claim about its revolutionary importance, and questions standard accounts of "class consciousness" by looking at the political and discursive conditions in which particular languages arose and faded. Themes: a rethinking of "the making of the English working class", Chartism, the formation of working-class culture, and a new account of the Labour Party's history.
- Introduction summary on the chapter page (paraphrased): the essays concern the interaction of society and politics in nineteenth- and twentieth-century England, above all the changing place of "the working class" in political life; the subject has been not neglected but taken for granted, resting on utilitarian, evolutionist and Marxist assumptions; because the older beliefs about working-class politics are now in question, a critical re-examination is useful. The summary names Hobsbawm's metaphor of "the forward march of labour" halted, and asks whether "the working class" has any reality apart from the historical language in which it has been described.

## 4. Neville Kirk, "In Defence of Class: A Critique of Recent Revisionist Writing upon the Nineteenth-Century English Working Class", *IRSH* 32:1 (April 1987): 2–47

**Read status: pages read 2–5 (opening and statement of thesis) and 44–47 (close of the state section and conclusion), from the Cambridge Core PDF at the URL already stored in the atlas record `kirk_class_selected_body` (the Core page reports access; the file is a scan, so these eight pages were OCRed locally and checked against the running heads). Together with the atlas's earlier reads (pp. 6–7, 14–17, 46–47 in `kirk_class_selected_body`; opening and notes 1–22 in `kirk_defence_class`), roughly a quarter of the article is now read. DOI 10.1017/S0020859000008312.**

**How Kirk frames the revisionists (pp. 2–3).** The claim that class was central to nineteenth-century workers' lives is under criticism within labour and social history from Joyce (1980), Stedman Jones (1982 and 1983), Calhoun (1982) and Glen (1984), who hold that workers were less motivated by class than Edward and Dorothy Thompson and Hobsbawm claimed (p. 2, n. 1–2). Two novelties: the 1980s critiques have more momentum than the positivist critiques of the 1960s, a change Kirk ties in part to labour's defeats under Thatcherism (p. 3, n. 4: the halted "forward march of labour" is central to Stedman Jones's questioning of class, citing LoC p. 1 and the introduction); and they draw on wider theory — "Stedman Jones's great debts to Althusser and Saussure" (p. 3). Note 5 (p. 3): Stedman Jones, despite an acknowledged debt to Thompson's anti-reductionism, treats Thompson's "experience" as subjectivist and prefers Althusser's hidden structure (citing LoC pp. 12, 20); Kirk answers that Thompson is alive to structure and cites Thompson's "The Politics of Theory" (1981) and Ellen Wood's "E. P. Thompson and His Critics" (1982).

**Kirk's stated task (pp. 3–5).** Not a general review but a critique of Stedman Jones's "revisionist approach" as revealed in "The Language of Chartism" (1982, LCh) and "Rethinking Chartism" (1983, RCh), which replace the dominant "social" reading of Chartism with a political one: a movement in which politics reigned, exploitation was explained in political terms, and the appeal went to the politically excluded and economically productive of all strata (pp. 3–4). Reviewers have concentrated on four aspects — the study of words and language; language's part in constructing social meaning; the ideological character of Chartism; and the relation of (political) ideas to economic and social levels — and Kirk takes the last two (p. 4). On the first two he reports that "Robbie Gray and John Foster" found the "non-referential" approach a formalised, literal reading of words rather than a study of how social meaning is made; Foster's phrase about ignoring the nexus of social practice, language and consciousness is quoted (p. 4, n. 6, citing Foster 1985 esp. p. 43). Kirk's own view (n. 6): Stedman Jones offers no post-structuralist reading at all but a history of radicalism as a body of ideas "in the manner of conventional intellectual history", with too little context; he quotes Karen Sawislak's 1987 Yale discussion paper and Gray's observation (Social History 11, 1986, p. 367) that post-structuralist work on language is itself a field of debate rather than a settled method. Thesis (p. 5): following Pickering (P&P 112, 1986) and Epstein (L/LT 18, 1986), he will defend Chartism as a social, class-based movement and argue that Stedman Jones greatly underestimates economic and social influences on its ideas; the "awful dread of economic reductionism leads not to materialism, but to an unsatisfactory idealism" (p. 5). Note 8 (p. 5): the criticisms of Edward Thompson in LoC pp. 16–20 are "brief but unmistakable"; Dorothy Thompson's work is not directly addressed in either essay; the Thompsons, Epstein and Sykes are not economic reductionists, but Stedman Jones implies they treat language too unproblematically as the expression of "experience".

**The state and "liberalization" (pp. 44–46).** Kirk grants that the Factory Acts, public health, education, the Lancaster decision of 1843 and party competition show a new awareness of the need to give workers a "stake in society", but finds the "liberalization" thesis resting on three and five pages of the two essays, dependent on Richards (pp. 44, n. 134); he notes the irony of Stedman Jones's own mid-1970s warning (LoC p. 73) against exaggerating such concessions (p. 44, n. 135). Drawing on Saville's forthcoming *1848* and Dorothy Thompson's *The Chartists* (p. 295, cited n. 140, for 1842 as the year of most arrests and most people on the streets), he argues that coercion in 1839–40, 1842 and 1848 did more than concession to defeat Chartism, and that softened state practice followed effective repression (pp. 45–46).

**Conclusion (pp. 46–47).** Full support for Stedman Jones's opposition to economic reductionism, his stress on the complexity of social interaction, his refusal of preconceived notions of Chartist consciousness and his "refreshing aim" to study politics and language; but his non-referential approach and refusal to make "any simple prejudgement about the determining role of the social" (LoC p. 7 quoted) lead him to underestimate the pressures of economic and social factors, "especially the development of capitalist structures and practices", on Chartist thinking; language and politics have been "wrenched loose from their material determinations"; the class character of Chartism is "falsely denied"; the result is "an unconvincing idealism" (p. 47). Note 143 aligns Foster (1985, pp. 40–45) and Ellen Wood, *The Retreat from Class* (1986), pp. 102–15, as critics of the same "idealist thrust".

**Edge use.** The most fully argued critique of the first round, read in the critic's own words: supports `languages_of_class → gareth_stedman_jones` (critique; Kirk named), the strand `first_critics_1985_87`, and the existing `historyworkshop/chartist_language_class` strand text (confirmed: Kirk accepts attention to language but denies its independence from material determination). Kirk's p. 3 n. 5 and p. 5 n. 8 supply, with LoC pages 12, 16–20, the basis for an edge `gareth_stedman_jones → ep_thompson` (critique of "experience"). Kirk's p. 4 is the only lawful route this session to Gray's words (§5).

## 6. Gareth Stedman Jones, "Class Struggle and the Industrial Revolution", *New Left Review* I/90 (March–April 1975); reprinted as ch. 1 of *Languages of Class* (pp. 25–75)

**Read status: read in full on NLR's own site (https://newleftreview.org/issues/i90/articles/gareth-stedman-jones-class-struggle-and-the-industrial-revolution; open; web edition unpaginated, passages located by section headings: untitled opening, "New Emphasis", "The Case of Oldham", "Two Types of Consciousness", "Industrial Struggle", "Spinners' Fight", "Political Dimension", "Between Reform and Revolution", "Conceptual Problems", "Central Problem", "Labour Aristocracy", "Limitations of the Analysis", "Restabilization", "New Rules", "Balance Sheet"; footnote 1 gives Foster, Class Struggle and the Industrial Revolution: Early Industrial Capitalism in Three English Towns, Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1974).** DOI (NLR scheme) 10.64590/xdr.

**Findings (paraphrased).** A review essay on Foster's Oldham study. He calls the book a landmark whose questions and categories are explicitly Marxist and Leninist (a vanguard–mass dialectic) and heavily quantitative, and faults its isolation of Oldham from Peterloo, 1832, Owenism and Chartism ("New Emphasis", "The Case of Oldham"). He accepts that Oldham workers reached a new consciousness committed to overcoming sectional divisions, but doubts it was revolutionary in a socialist sense: Chartist aims were democratic and co-operative, not expropriation, and Foster's criterion for revolutionary consciousness is drawn from the period it is meant to prove ("Conceptual Problems", "Central Problem"). On the 1830s–40s he argues that economic and political discontent were closely connected and that after 1830 the line between industrial and political militancy blurred — the spinners fought for control of production as well as against immiseration ("Industrial Struggle", "Spinners' Fight", "Political Dimension"); Foster 1985 later turns this against him (first file, §1). From *Capital* he draws a distinction between struggles over the appropriation of the product (wage struggles) and over control of the labour process, the second tending to converge with political demands; Lenin's trade-union/class-consciousness pairing therefore fits Oldham badly, and the early Marx's alienation couplet is called idealist ("Industrial Struggle", "Conceptual Problems"). Against the labour-aristocracy explanation of post-1848 reformism he argues that employers won control of production before any political concession, that recovery came from railway building rather than capital export, and that the ebb of struggle should be explained by a new structure covering the whole workforce set against the apparent inevitability of capitalism — proposed as "experimental" ("Labour Aristocracy", "Restabilization", "Balance Sheet"). Verdict: the book reopens major debates and advances Marxist historical method.

**Edge use.** Establishes, in his own words, the pre-1983 position the Introduction later revises: Marxist categories applied critically, class consciousness tied to the labour process, reformism explained structurally. Supports `gareth_stedman_jones → labourhistory` (contribution 1974–75) and `gareth_stedman_jones → marx` (critique within the tradition; 1975, then 1983). Also the chronology event 1975 and the Foster–Stedman Jones reciprocal (Foster's 1985 reply).

## 7. Joan W. Scott, "On Language, Gender, and Working-Class History", *ILWCH* 31 (Spring 1987): 1–13, and the 1988 book version — for what they say about Stedman Jones

**Read status: Scott's text unread this session (Cambridge Core shows a first-page image only; "Get access"; the atlas already carries it as `pe134_palmer_scott_language_1987`, DOI 10.1017/S0147547900004063, with the Palmer, Stansell and Rabinbach responses and Scott's reply). Read in full: the issue's "Editor's Remarks" (ILWCH 31, pp. v–vi, DOI 10.1017/S0147547900004051, open PDF on Cambridge Core; two scanned pages OCRed locally), signed "H. G." [Helmut Gruber]. Scott's 1988 *Gender and the Politics of History* (Columbia UP; rev. edn 1999, Google Books id o2lApVRwxEoC, partial preview): a search inside the preview was blocked by Google's rate limit; unread.**

**What the editors say Scott argues (pp. v–vi, paraphrased).** Scott wants to show how language can serve the perception and definition of gender and so enrich working-class history; she objects to the conflation of language with words, which she sees as an obstacle to bringing gender into labour history. She "criticizes Gareth Stedman Jones for using theories of language in his 'Rethinking Chartism' in an essentially conservative manner" — a close study of the movement's vocabulary used to shift emphasis from the economic to the political sphere. The remarks quote her: he "stops short of opening up … important conceptual questions" because he treats language as a vehicle for communicating ideas rather than as a system of meaning or a process of signification (p. v). Thus, the editors summarise, he reduces Chartism to a formal political struggle and so flattens the relational meanings of class; her "rereading Chartism" shows that the Chartists' linguistic construction of class (rationalism, politics, producers as men) meant the working class would be represented by men and a gendered family division of labour would follow (pp. v–vi). The three critics: Palmer cautions Scott and Stedman Jones alike against exaggerating language and collapsing class into class consciousness, insisting on capitalist accumulation and the reproduction of labour; Stansell agrees that historians' wilful disinterest in post-structuralism missed opportunities but holds that only social analysis of real women's activities brings gender forward; Rabinbach welcomes the rereading, warns of the post-structuralists' "terrifying relativism", and asks that "working class" itself be put at the top of the concepts to be investigated (p. vi).

**Edge use.** Supports `joan_wallach_scott → gareth_stedman_jones` (critique, 1987): the attribution rests on the journal editors' explicit summary and their quotation of Scott, read in full; the strength should be marked as "editors' summary; Scott's text unread" until the article is read. Also supplies the Palmer position of 1987 in the editors' words, which is a lawful (if second-hand) basis for `bryan_d_palmer → gareth_stedman_jones` — see Proposals, where it is offered only as a roster/strand mention, not an edge, because the brief requires Palmer's own words.

## 9. Patrick Joyce, *Visions of the People* (CUP 1991), and Dorothy Thompson, *The Chartists* (1984) — on the point

**Joyce: publisher description and the Cambridge Core introduction summary only** ("1 – Introduction: beyond class?", pp. 1–24, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511560651.001; print 25 January 1991). ## P.3 Strands for `languages_of_class` (first round, 1983–87)

**`languages_of_class_1983` — "Languages of Class, 1982–83"**
focus (draft): Stedman Jones's "The Language of Chartism" appeared in 1982 in The Chartist Experience, edited by James Epstein and Dorothy Thompson, and in extended form as "Rethinking Chartism" in Languages of Class (1983). The introduction set the programme: historians had set fragments of speech "into direct relation to a putative experiential reality" (p. 21, as quoted by Foster); instead they should "study the production of interest, identification, grievance and aspiration within political languages themselves" (p. 22, as quoted by Foster), without any "simple prejudgement about the determining role of the 'social'" (p. 7, as quoted by Epstein). The Chartism essay examined "only the public political language of the movement" (p. 95, as quoted by Epstein) and found a radicalism that was "a vocabulary of political exclusion" (p. 104), not the ideology of a class; Chartism rose and ebbed with a particular vision of the state (pp. 173–4). He placed himself after Thompson, whose "great achievement" had freed class consciousness from economic reduction, while rejecting the "relatively direct relationship" Thompson assumed between social being and consciousness (p. 101, as quoted by Foster). The earlier essays reprinted in the book (1974, 1975) show the Marxist social history he was leaving.

**`first_critics_1985_87` — "The first critics, 1985–87"**
focus (draft): The answers came from labour historians who accepted the attack on economic reductionism and refused its conclusion. John Foster (New Left Review, 1985) — whose own Oldham study Stedman Jones had reviewed in 1975 — called the theory "undeveloped", set Volosinov's "multi-accentuality" against Saussure, and read the book as "an intellectual declassing of our understanding of language". Robert Gray (Social History, 1986) found post-structuralist work on language "a field of debate" rather than a method historians could adopt (p. 367, as quoted by Kirk). James Epstein (Labour/Le Travail, 1986) praised "the most sustained and coherent descriptive analysis yet of formal Chartist ideology" but argued that a public political language excludes symbolic action and that the fight over "the people" was itself "a form of class struggle". Neville Kirk (International Review of Social History, 1987) gave "full support" to the opposition to reductionism and concluded that language and politics had been "wrenched loose from their material determinations" — "an unconvincing idealism". Joan Scott (ILWCH, 1987) answered from the other side: he had used theories of language "in an essentially conservative manner", as a vehicle for ideas rather than a system of meaning, and so missed the gendered construction of class (as the journal's editors summarised her). Cronin (Journal of Social History, 1986) and Ellen Wood (The Retreat from Class, 1986) belong here but are unread.
