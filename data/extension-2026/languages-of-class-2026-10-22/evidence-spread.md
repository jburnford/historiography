### 0. Baseline: class as relationship, and the critique of "experience" inside Marxism

#### 0.1 E. P. Thompson, preface to *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963)
**Read status:** not re-read; the graph's `thompson_making_preface` (university-hosted transcription, read in full by the 1.139 agent) and edge_924's evidence note already quote the passage ("There is today an ever-present temptation to suppose that class is a thing … If we remember that class is a relationship and not a thing, we can not think in this way"), with the point that the preface's targets are reification in both the Marxist and the Smelser–Dahrendorf camps. **Not duplicated here.** What this file adds is how the later rounds read the preface: Eley and Nield (2000, p. 24) name "Edward Thompson and Sheila Rowbotham" for the belief "that a knowledge of past struggles itself contributed to resistance"; Cooper (2000, p. 68 n. 11) turns the preface against constructivists: Thompson "wanted to show how the working class made itself, but never doubted that what was made was the working class"; Sangster (2000, p. 158) sets "the Thompsonian subject whose consciousness emerged from the interplay of human agency with social, cultural and economic formations" against "the post-structuralist subject". See §§6.4–6.6 and §1.7.

3. **id `us_class_formation_1984_93` — "Class formation and exceptionalism in the United States (1984–93)".**
   focus: The American round began a year before Scott's, in the same journal. Sean Wilentz argued that "the Euro-centric concept of a mass-based socialist or communist party as the appropriate index of class consciousness diverts attention from the ways in which workers in the United States have battled the system of wage labor" (editors' remarks, ILWCH 26, 1984, p. v); Nick Salvatore replied that one must "use carefully three related but quite different terms: class consciousness, class conflict and class formation" and that Wilentz had not "travelled as far as he would wish from the position outlined by Louis Hartz" (pp. 26, 28); Michael Hanagan held that embedding in "a county's political structure" makes a difference (editors' remarks). Ira Katznelson and Aristide Zolberg's Working-Class Formation (1986) asked "how class, rather than other social bonds, became central" to working people's "ideologies, dispositions, and actions" across France, Germany and the United States, with chapters by Sewell, Perrot, Cottereau, Bridges, Shefter, Kocka and Nolan (publisher description); Eley and Nield note it "missed gender entirely" (2000, p. 4). Berlanstein's Rethinking Labor History (1993) carried Sewell's "post-materialist rhetoric for labor history" (as cited by Sangster), answered in Frader's "Dissent over Discourse" (1995).

4. **id `canadian_reception_1990_2000` — "Canada: materialist feminism and the discursive turn (1990–2000)".**
   focus: In Canada the debate arrived through Labour/Le Travail. Palmer's Descent into Discourse (1990) held that "historical materialism remains the foundation upon which studies of the past must be erected" (p. xiii, as quoted by Callinicos 1992); Mariana Valverde asked whether Scott "exaggerates the contribution Foucault and Derrida can make" (1990, p. 233); Joy Parr's The Gender of Breadwinners (1990) and "Gender History and Historical Practice" (1995) deconstructed skill while keeping "materialist conceptions of work and the economy" (Sangster 2000, p. 157). Existing strand `cdn_working_class/state_of_the_field_2000` covers the same ground from the Canadian field's side; this strand points to it rather than repeating it.

5. **id `french_language_of_labor_1980_89` — "France: the language of labor and the myth of the artisan (1980–89)".**
   focus: William Sewell's Work and Revolution in France (1980) treated "the 'language of labor' in the broadest sense" and argued that "The nineteenth-century labor movement was born in the craft workshop, not in the dark, satanic mill" (ch. 1 opening), with 1830 as "the emergence of class consciousness" (ch. 9 title). Jacques Rancière's "The Myth of the Artisan" (1983), the journal's first scholarly controversy, held that the skilled artisan's "culture as a seminal force in the development of the working-class and socialist movement is exaggerated and stems from later internal myth-making" (editors' remarks); answered by Sewell and Christopher Johnson, he replied that "the point at stake is the democratic meaning of worker militancy", that militants "were sons of the French Revolution rather than of corporative struggle; Julien Sorel's brothers rather than Poor Richard's", and that social history "in its new 'cultural' approach, rests on a far too restrictive understanding of 'social' struggle" (1984, pp. 42, 44, 45). William Reddy's Money and Liberty in Modern Europe (1987) found "an increasingly poor fit" between class concepts "and the reality they purport to explain" and proposed a new concept of exchange and authority (publisher description). Scott's 1988 chapter on Sewell is unread; in 2000 she returned to Rancière as "an important corrective to Eley and Nield".

6. **id `german_class_formation_1992_96` — "Germany: gender and the politics of class formation (1989–97)".**
   focus: German labour history met the debate through Alltagsgeschichte and gender. Geoff Eley's "Labor History, Social History, Alltagsgeschichte" (JMH 1989; unread) and Alf Lüdtke's Eigen-Sinn (1993; unread) carried the Thompsonian interest in experience into Bielefeld's territory, against Jürgen Kocka's class-formation programme (Lohnarbeit und Klassenbildung, 1983; "Traditionsbindung und Klassenbildung", 1986; unread). Stedman Jones restated his position in a Göttingen volume edited by Lüdtke (1997; Eley and Nield n. 18).

7. **id `south_asian_working_class_1989_98` — "South Asia: culture, capital and the making of a working class (1989–99)".**
   focus: Dipesh Chakrabarty's Rethinking Working-Class History (1989) read Bengal's jute workers against "an assumption regarding 'culture' built into Marx's category of 'capital'" and declared "Culture, one might say, is the 'unthought' of Indian Marxism" (pp. xi–xii; edge_916), separating his argument from Thompson's (p. 5 n. 9). Rajnarayan Chandavarkar's The Origins of Industrial Capitalism in India (1994) argued that both capital and labour were internally fragmented and asked when workers could build solidarity (publisher description); his "'The Making of the Working Class': E. P. Thompson and Indian History" (HWJ 1997) charged that Thompson's inheritance "turned in Dipesh Chakrabarty's hands into a static, timeless, indeed Orientalist characterization of a 'traditional' Indian, implicitly 'Hindu' culture" (p. 182; see provenance flag); Imperial Power and Popular Politics (1998) "rejects the 'Orientalist' view of Indian social and economic development" and "reasserts the critical role of the working classes" (publisher description), and was reviewed by Chakrabarty (AHR 1999; unread). Eley and Nield pointed to this exchange as where their discussion "could easily be carried" (2000, n. 2).

8. **id `africa_latin_america_1988_2000` — "Africa and Latin America: experience versus structures (1988–2000)".**
   focus: The journal's 1989 controversy was Emilia Viotti da Costa's "Experience versus Structures: New Tendencies in the History of Labor and the Working Class in Latin America — What Do We Gain? 9. **id `gender_race_and_class_1986_2007` — "Gender, race and the retrospective (1986–2007)".**
   focus: Carolyn Steedman's Landscape for a Good Woman (1986) set "two working-class childhoods" in "history, politics, psychoanalysis and feminism" (publisher); Sonya Rose's Limited Livelihoods (1992) made gender "a pivotal force" in "the massive reorganization of lives and livelihoods that accompanied industrial capitalism" (publisher); Catherine Hall's White, Male and Middle Class (1992) and Anna Clark's The Struggle for the Breeches (1995) followed; Eley and Nield judged that "the study of class has benefited powerfully from feminist revisions" (2000, p. 8). David Roediger's The Wages of Whiteness (1991) asked how "race and class came together" in the antebellum United States, treating white working-class racism as "psychological and ideological" rather than only economic (publisher) — the race turn, recorded here in one paragraph. In 2000 Eley and Nield asked "Farewell to the Working Class?", holding that "class discursively understood … is a better starting point for the study of class formation than the classical ones of economics and social structure" while wanting "to break this logic of disavowal" (pp. 16; as quoted by Scott p. 69); Scott answered that they "foreclose other possibilities" and quipped "Assent to Discourse" (pp. 69, 72); Cooper that they "did not think to ask Karl Marx" (p. 61). The Future of Class in History (2007) sought "collaborative conversation across the polarized differences between social and cultural historians" (preface, p. vii).
