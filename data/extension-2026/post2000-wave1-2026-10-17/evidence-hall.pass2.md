## 1. *Civilising Subjects: Metropole and Colony in the English Imagination, 1830–1867* (Polity / Chicago, 2002) and the *JBS* roundtable

The book is **UNREAD**. Its argument is taken from Hall's own statement in the 2003 roundtable.

**Catherine Hall, "A Response to the Commentators", *Journal of British Studies* 42:4 (October 2003): 530–538.** Roundtable on *Civilising Subjects*. Open-access copy at https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1407509/. **READ IN PART:** pp. 530–537 in full; p. 538 not reached.

**Thesis and position (p. 530).**
- "The central argument of Civilising Subjects is that being a colonizer was part of the construction of Englishness in the nineteenth century."
- "Given my location as a white English woman formed through socialist and feminist politics, my task as an historian, as I defined it at the end of the 1980s, was to explore Englishness as a racial formation."
- "Yet if, as Frantz Fanon argued, colonialism shaped the colonizers as much as the colonized then how was this expressed in everyday culture? How was it lived 'at home' as well as in the empire?"
- "I aimed to explore the construction of the colonizer and to break the binary of inside-outside through two case studies, one of Jamaica, the other of England."

**Gikandi's critique and her reply (pp. 531–532).**
- "When Simon Gikandi asks me, therefore, where are the black subjects whose narratives are essential to the construction of a new colonial history, my reply is that this was not the subject of my book."
- She concedes: "Of course he is entirely right to argue that 'a history of the wars of representations in Jamaica ... will remain incomplete until we make the accounts of these subjects ... constitutive of it.'"
- p. 531: "My contribution to this process of completion was to analyze the 'racing,' to use Toni Morrison's term, of white subjects, the ways in which Englishness needs to be understood not only as a formation of class and of gender, but also of race and ethnicity."
- p. 532: "Gikandi is concerned about my emphasis on the place of the discursive ... Yet colonial discourses shaped policy making and were constitutive of social worlds and lived experience."
- She cites Chakrabarty's "waiting room" of history (p. 532 n. 2, *Provincializing Europe*, p. 9) and *Defining the Victorian Nation* (2000), pp. 62–70 (n. 1).

**C. L. R. James and the single lens (p. 533).**
- "Colonizers were made both in the metropole and on colonial sites. And, as C. L. R. James's The Black Jacobins (1938) taught us, much can be gained through the analysis of metropole and colony in one lens."

**Holt and Levine on structure (pp. 533–534).**
- "Yet why, I am asked by Philippa Levine and Thomas Holt, did I decide to split the main narrative of the book between Jamaica, in the first half, and Birmingham in the second?"
- "Perhaps Holt is right when he remarks that this weakens the ending, for there is no single climax. But crucially, in making this split, I was reasserting the gap between metropole and colony as fundamental to the maintenance of metropolitan power."
- She cites Partha Chatterjee's "rule of colonial difference" (p. 534).
- n. 6 compares *Family Fortunes*: "Leonore Davidoff and I faced a similar problem ... We resolved this by integrating our material and telling a national story."

**Gender and men (pp. 536–537).**
- "Both Holt and Levine wonder, given the insistence on the gendering of my narratives, why men are so much more present than women? It is true that my primary focus is not on the relational formation of masculinities and femininities, but it is about men, masculinities, and the making of 'raced' subjects."

**Against the national story (p. 537).**
- "One of my self-appointed tasks was to demonstrate the ways in which the well-established narrative of British history, the national history, the one taught in schools and universities, needed to be rethought through the frame of empire. Civilising Subjects is not only cultural and social history, it is also political history."

**The other commentators' texts are UNREAD.** Simon Gikandi, Philippa Levine and Thomas C. Holt are in *JBS* 42:4 (2003); Cambridge abstract pages only were seen in search. Their critiques are known only as Hall states them.

**Other reviews of *Civilising Subjects*** (search listings only, unread):
- Hilary Beckles (*Albion*); Madhavi Kale (*Small Axe*, 2003); Jean Besson (*JRAI*, 2003); Sandra den Otter (*Histoire sociale/Social History*, 2005; the York OJS page was blocked by a verification wall).
- The AHA Forkosch Prize is recorded in the UCL REF case study (§3); the year is not stated there.

## 2. *Macaulay and Son: Architects of Imperial Britain* (Yale, 2012)

The book is **UNREAD**.

**Tao Wei, review, *Essays in History* 47 (2014)** (University of Virginia; PDF of the web page). **READ IN FULL.**
- Placement: "a follow-up to her excellent book Civilising Subjects". It is set among the "new imperial historians" Linda Colley, Kathleen Wilson and Antoinette Burton, who showed "how the British Empire was staged both at home and in the colonies".
- Hall quoted: writing new imperial history requires "a reconfiguration of the topography of history—a return to the center, but now from the margins, a process not of affirmation but of deconstruction" (p. xvi).
- On Thomas Babington Macaulay: Hall calls him "one of the most influential proponents of liberal imperial discourse" (p. xiii). His "vision of nationhood embraced Irish Catholics and Jews, yet excluded women and colonial subjects" (p. 153, reviewer's paraphrase).
- "Looking at Thomas as an historian in chapter six, Hall examines how Thomas' five-volume History of England from the Reign of James II promoted the Whig project of the British government for governing imperial subjects."
- On the Minute on Education (1835): the "space of difference" between British rulers and Indian subjects (p. 229).
- Criticism: "Hall overemphasizes the differences in their understandings of the British Empire."
- The reviewer's footnote adds *At Home with the Empire* (2006, ed. with Sonya Rose).

Other reviews are metadata only (Crossref): Stephen Auerbach (*EHR*, 2014), Wright (2015), and Theodore Koditschek (*JBS*), the last named in a search listing.

## 3. Legacies of British Slave-ownership (UCL, 2009–) and *Legacies of British Slave-Ownership* (2014)

**UCL REF 2014 impact case study, "Engaging with the legacies of British slave-ownership"** (https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=34487). **READ** via WebFetch excerpts.
- "the ESRC-funded project Legacies of British Slave-ownership (1/6/2009-31/5/2012)", run by "Professor Hall and her research associates, Dr Nicholas Draper and Dr Keith McClelland".
- The starting point is "the records of the £20m paid in compensation to slave-owners in 1833", documenting "the c.46,000 individual claims and awards".
- Hall is described as a prime mover in the "New Imperial History". *Civilising Subjects* won the AHA's Forkosch Prize.
- The online Encyclopaedia of British Slave-ownership was launched on 28 February 2013. It had 137,998 visits from 108,022 unique visitors between February and 31 July 2013. Workshops drew about 220 people, "mostly local and family historians from outside academia". Correspondents were "the great majority from descendants of slave-owners and the enslaved".
- Outputs listed: *Race, Nation and Empire* (Hall and McClelland, eds., 2010); exhibitions in 2008–9 and 2011; Hall's *Transactions of the Royal Historical Society* article (December 2011).
- The case study does not mention Eric Williams.

**Hall, McClelland, Draper, Donington and Lang, *Legacies of British Slave-Ownership: Colonial Slavery and the Formation of Victorian Britain* (Cambridge UP, 28 August 2014; doi:10.1017/CBO9781139626958).** Chapters, per Crossref:
- Introduction (Hall, Draper, McClelland), pp. 1–33;
- "Possessing people" and "Helping to make Britain great" (Draper);
- "Redefining the West India interest" (McClelland);
- "Reconfiguring race" (Hall);
- "Transforming capital" (Donington);
- Conclusion (Hall, Draper, McClelland);
- "Making history in a prosopography" (Draper, Lang);
- "A note on the database" (McClelland).

**Introduction READ IN PART** through WebFetch excerpts of the Cambridge Core HTML, without print page numbers, covering roughly the first 80 per cent of the chapter.
- "our project is to reinscribe slave-ownership onto modern British history." "The database digitises all the awards in the slave-compensation records of the 1830s."
- **Williams.** "Eric Williams' Capitalism and Slavery included four connected arguments that are critical for us." Their verdicts:
  - on the first thesis (slavery and industrialisation): "Our material cannot resolve these controversies over Williams' first thesis";
  - on the second (slave-wealth in British society and culture): "our findings are entirely supportive of Williams in the context of this second thesis";
  - on decline: their work "complicates Williams by qualifying his 'decline' thesis" ("'decline' is as much an ideological as a technical concept", after Christopher Leslie Brown);
  - on the fourth: "Williams relied on treating slave-owners as a whole"; "many West Indians were highly adaptive".
- **Drescher.** He "has largely been accepted outside the Caribbean as having fatally undermined Williams"; "Drescher did not point out that these were war years."
- Literatures: they list "the 'new imperial history'" among five that shaped them, and cite "the 'civilising subjects' of Catherine Hall's study".
- Publics: responsibilities "to the descendants of the enslaved people, to the descendants of the slave-owners and to other academic and non-academic constituencies"; the project "has no position on reparations and restitution".

**Katie Donington, "Eric Williams' Foundational Work on Slavery, Industry, and Wealth", *Black Perspectives* (AAIHS), 21 September 2020.** **READ** via WebFetch excerpts. Donington is a co-author of the 2014 book, so this is an insider's retrospect.
- "I spent many years working with the Legacies of British Slave-ownership project".
- "The project foregrounded Williams's work".
- The findings "supported a modified version of Williams's claims about the relationship between slavery and industrialization".
