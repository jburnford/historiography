## 1. Sources read this session

### 1a. AHA biography

Read via WebFetch at historians.org/person/lynn-hunt/. It is a celebratory presidential biography, used for dates.
- **Presidency:** "AHA President, 2002".
- ***The New Cultural History: Essays*** (University of California Press, 1989), grown "out of her 1987 conference".
- ***The Family Romance of the French Revolution:*** "Her book set off animated debate, spurred forums in multiple journals in the United States and abroad."
- ***Human Rights and Revolutions*** (co-edited, 2000). Then: "Lynn is currently working out her own interpretation of this paradox of human rights in its eighteenth-century context". This is the project that became *Inventing Human Rights*.

### 1b. AHA presidential address, "The World We Have Gained: The Future of the French Revolution"

Delivered 3 January 2003, 117th annual meeting, Chicago; published *AHR* 108:1 (Feb. 2003), 1–19 (OpenAlex DOI 10.1086/533043). Read via WebFetch extraction of historians.org/presidential-address/lynn-hunt/; no pagination.
- **Opening:** "The French Revolution has fallen out of favor."
- **The question:** "how could the French Revolution produce such contradictory consequences?"
- **The legacy:** "the French Revolution also gave birth to human rights and democracy."
- **Her definition of cultural revolution:** "the politicization of everyday life."
- **On Furet and Arendt:** "Arendt was even more Tocquevillian than Furet."
- **Close:** "the judgment of history depends on where the clock stops"; "the French Revolution prepared the way for many different futures, futures already past and futures yet to come."
- **Not covered:** the extraction reports that the address does not discuss global history or the linguistic turn.

### 1c. Samuel Moyn, "On the Genealogy of Morals," *The Nation*, 16 April 2007

Online 29 March 2007. Review of *Inventing Human Rights* (Norton, 2007). Read via WebFetch extraction of the complete text; no pagination.
- **Moyn's summary of Hunt:** she identifies "the Enlightenment and the age of democratic revolutions as the moment when the cause was born"; "the cascading logic of human rights."
- **Criticisms:**
  - the new human-rights history "distorts the past to suit the present";
  - "rights talk" had "historical lineages completely different from humanitarianism";
  - "The protagonists of her book are not people thinking and acting on their convictions but rights themselves";
  - "Hunt’s position is that nationalism sank the rights of man after their announcement", whereas the two overlapped from the start;
  - there is "little reason to locate the 'invention' of human rights as we now know them in the late eighteenth century".
- **His counter-thesis:** human rights as now understood are "an invention of recent date", of the 1970s.
- **Close:** the deep background is "of little use in responding to our confusion and dismay".

### 1d. David A. Bell, "Un dret egal," *London Review of Books* 29:22 (15 Nov. 2007)

Review of *Inventing Human Rights*. Read via WebFetch extraction of the complete text.
- **Bell's summary of her argument:** epistolary novels (*Pamela*, *Clarissa*, *Julie*) taught readers to identify with vulnerable characters; torture was condemned by the 1780s; a "logic of rights" followed the 1789 Declaration; the 1948 Universal Declaration is presented as later vindication.
- **Criticisms:**
  - 1948 to the present is compressed into about 38 pages; he notes Moyn's objection;
  - the book is novel-centred, neglecting theatre, painting and Plutarchan "emulation";
  - causation is only hinted at;
  - ordinary people who did not read novels also embraced rights;
  - **on method:** the "new cultural history" tends to flatten the difference between texts and actions, since reading about torture is not undergoing it.
- **On her earlier work:**
  - he calls her French Revolution studies "luminous";
  - he reads *The Family Romance of the French Revolution* (1992) as showing how family imagery shaped the demonisation of Marie-Antoinette and the move from paternal monarchy to fraternal republic, a thesis *Inventing Human Rights* "keeps";
  - he credits Hunt with popularising "the new cultural history".
- **Verdict:** "a rich, elegant and persuasive essay" that "raises more questions than it can answer".

### 1e. Julia McClure, review of *Writing History in the Global Era* (Norton, 2014)

*Reviews in History* no. 1732 (19 Feb. 2015). Read via WebFetch extraction of reviews.history.ac.uk/review/1732/print. This is the review the existing `hunt` source points to.
- **Her summary:** globalization has become a dominant "paradigm" filling the gap left by Marxism, modernization, the Annales school and identity politics. It privileges economics and macro trends and risks overshadowing "society" and "self". Hunt surveys the rise and fall of the cultural theories (Foucault, Derrida) she helped bring into history, defending their gains while acknowledging their limits.
- **Hunt quoted:** globalization "potentially threatens to wash away the gains of the last decades of cultural history" (p. 59, as McClure cites it).
- **McClure's criticisms:**
  - Hunt does not name proponents of the globalization paradigm;
  - she does not engage the historiographical debates on global history;
  - she treats world or global history as necessarily the history of globalization;
  - her own standpoint on universal values needs the scrutiny she applies to Mignolo.

### 1f. Gregory Jones-Katz, review in two parts (S-USIH blog, March–April 2015)

**Mediated** through the Toynbee Prize Foundation post of 2 April 2015 (WebFetch); the original is 403.
- "the globalization paradigm reinstates the very suppositions that cultural theories had criticized";
- globalization as a "Trojan horse" reviving modernization theory.
- **Limit:** these are the reviewer's words about Hunt, not hers.
