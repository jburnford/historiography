## 1. Sources read this session

### 1a. Daston, "The Moral Economy of Science," *Osiris* 10 (1995), 3–24

**Read in part, pp. 3–4.** Source: MPG PuRe public PDF, item_2276978. The OCR of the frontispiece is garbled; the text pages are clean.

- **p. 3, the thesis:** "I will nonetheless claim that not only does science have what I will call a moral economy (indeed, several); these moral economies are moreover constitutive of those features conventionally (and, to my mind, correctly) deemed most characteristic of science as a way of knowing. Put more sharply and specifically: certain forms of empiricism, quantification, and objectivity itself are not simply compatible with moral economies; they require moral economies."
- **p. 3 n. 1, the term and Thompson:** "I am grateful to John Carson and Nathan Reingold for pointing out that my use of the term moral economy diverges significantly from E. P. Thompson's in 'The Moral Economy of the English Crowd in the Eighteenth Century' … My appeal here to 'economies' of affects and values has little to do with Thompson's accounts of corn markets and the tradition of 'setting the price' by persuasion or riot, although it does appeal to a broader sense of 'legitimizing notion.'"
- **p. 3 n. 2, studies she gathers under the rubric:** Hannaway; Porter ("Objectivity as Standardization", 1992); Schaffer; Shapin.
- **p. 4, the definition:** "What I mean by a moral economy is a web of affect-saturated values that stand and function in well-defined relationship to one another." Also: "As Gaston Bachelard decades ago remarked, to imbue objects or actions with emotion is almost always thereby to valorize them, and vice versa."

### 1b. Daston, "Science Studies and the History of Science," *Critical Inquiry* 35:4 (Summer 2009), 798–813

**Read in full.** Source: MPG PuRe public PDF, item_2279417.

- **p. 799, author note:** "director of the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science in Berlin". Books: "*Classical Probability in the Enlightenment* (1988) and, with Katharine Park, *Wonders and the Order of Nature, 1150–1750* (1998), both of which were awarded the Pfizer Prize of the History of Science Society. *Objectivity*, coauthored with Peter Galison, was published in 2007."
- **p. 800, the thesis:** "I shall argue that despite intensive and fruitful exchanges between science studies and the history of science in the 1970s and 1980s the two fields came to diverge in their conceptions of what they initially had held in common, namely, the subject matter of science … [and] examine how their paths forked in the 1990s, as the history of science became ever more historical and science studies ever less."
- **p. 802, Kuhn, two readings:** "scholars in science studies interpreted Kuhn’s analysis as a manifesto of relativism … Historians of science drew a somewhat different lesson from Kuhn’s book, one probably more in tune with Kuhn’s own views, namely, that teleology must be repudiated as a narrative form in the history of science."
- **p. 809, microhistory and its effect:** "The call from science studies to heed context and practices has been followed with a vengeance by historians of science who apprenticed themselves to historians to do so—with the paradoxical result that the history of science and science studies have ever less to say to one another."
- **pp. 809–810, Foucault:** "Insofar as there has been a counterweight to these miniaturizing tendencies in recent work in the history of science, it has been supplied not by science studies but by a still more thoroughgoing form of historicism, namely, the philosophical history of Michel Foucault … Topics like proof, experience, and objectivity, which historians had previously assigned to the timeless contemplations of the philosophers, suddenly seemed fair game" (p. 810). On experience: "This is epistemology in the making" (p. 810).
- **p. 811, conclusion:** "the history of science (somewhat to its own surprise) has in the last decade or so become a discipline, with cultivated practices and an ethos, while science studies has remained interdisciplinary—and, even on the testimony of some insiders, undisciplined."
- **p. 811 n. 30, self-qualification:** "The current history of science is almost always readable, engaging and instructive even, but curiously inert—finely wrought but flat. The price of disciplinarity has been a convergence toward the mean; fewer clunkers, but also fewer meteors."
- **pp. 812–813, relativism:** "In contrast, to historicize the category of the fact, objectivity, or proof is not thereby to debunk it" (p. 812). "Analogously, the fact that scientific objectivity arose in a specific historical context neither supports nor undercuts its epistemological validity. 'If historical, then relative' is a non sequitur" (p. 813). She describes science studies' assumptions as "Manichean" (p. 812), and the essay ends: "A new form of interdisciplinarity must be forged. Philosophy, anyone?" (p. 813).
- **Latour:** quoted twice from "Why Has Critique Run out of Steam?" (2004): p. 799 n. 3 and p. 812 n. 32.

### 1c. Daston and Galison, *Objectivity* (Zone Books, 2007)

**Not read.** Quotations below are **mediated** by Adrian Switzer's review, *Foucault Studies* 6 (Feb. 2009), 96–104 (open PDF, **read in part, pp. 96–99**).

- **Objectivity p. 18, as Switzer quotes it (p. 96):** "[T]his history is one of innovation and proliferation rather than monarchic succession … imagine [the history of objectivity being akin to] new stars winking into existence, not replacing old ones but changing the geography of the heavens."
- **Objectivity p. 19, as Switzer quotes it (p. 97):** "In contrast to the static tableaux of paradigms and epistemes, this is a history of dynamic fields, in which newly introduced bodies reconfigure and reshape those already present, and vice versa."
- **Objectivity p. 376, as Switzer quotes it (p. 98):** "All history can do is to demonstrate the possibility of alternatives"; and, on epistemic virtues, history "clarif[ies] what they are, how they work, and how much hangs in the balance if one is obliged to choose among them."

**Switzer's own reading.**
- On Kuhn (p. 96): "Implicitly, it is Kuhnian history that Lorraine Daston and Peter Galison have in mind."
- His criticism (p. 98): "History as a social scientific discipline is thereby exempted from the questions that confront a science when its epistemology is historicized … the historian with one hand unsettles the seeming givenness of objectivity … while with the other hand recuperates into history itself a basic or founding objectivity."
- On method (p. 99): "though it is unacknowledged, it is by a kind of Foucauldian genealogy that Daston and Galison conduct their study of the virtues and practices of the biological and physical sciences."

- **date_label:** "Classical Probability 1988 · Moral Economy of Science 1995 · Objectivity 2007 · Science Studies and the History of Science 2009 · Rules 2022"
- **description (draft):**
  > "A historian of science who historicizes the categories of scientific knowing: probability (1988), wonders (with Park, 1998), the 'moral economies' of affect and value that empiricism, quantification and objectivity require (1995), and, with Peter Galison, objectivity itself as one epistemic virtue among others (1992, 2007).
