## 1. *The Peasant Economy and Social Change in North China* (Stanford, 1985)

Text from the Internet Archive item `PeasantEconomySocialChangeNorthChina` (1988 paperback, `_djvu.txt`; page numbers from the running heads).

**READ IN PART:** chapter 1, "The Issues", pp. 3–17. The rest was searched by keyword only.

**Chayanov, Polanyi and Scott as the substantivist resources (pp. 4–5).**
- "The classic theoretical analysis is presented by A. V. Chayanov in his studies of the Russian peasants in the 1920's (1966a, b). Chayanov argued convincingly that peasant economies cannot be understood in the conventional terms of a discipline developed for the study of capitalist economies." (p. 4)
- Polanyi's "substantive" economics (p. 5).
- "James Scott has elaborated on the implications of Chayanov's and Polanyi's analyses" (p. 5).
- Against these he sets Schultz's and Popkin's formalist "rational peasant" (pp. 3–4).

**Geertz acknowledged for the term (p. 8).**
- "Labor input could be pushed to levels well beyond the point at which diminishing marginal returns set in. Chayanov pointed out the existence of this phenomenon in the agriculture of prerevolutionary Russia (1966b: 113–16). Clifford Geertz has given the same phenomenon, in rice farming in Java, the catchy term 'agricultural involution' (1963)."
- Footnote, p. 8: "'involution' occurs when the line showing the relationship between output and labor input begins to level off—i.e. when the marginal output of labor diminishes."

**Geertz's model as a frame for earlier China scholarship (p. 11).**
- On Perkins and Mark Elvin: "As Chinese agriculture intensified to a higher and higher degree with the population growth of the late imperial period, the marginal productivity of labor steadily declined ... (Elvin 1973: esp. Chap. 17). The peasant economy, in other words, involuted along the lines suggested by Geertz's analytical model."

**The 1985 thesis (pp. 15–16).**
- p. 15: "This combination of high land productivity and low labor productivity documents the fact of agricultural involution in recent centuries."
- p. 16: "The North China countryside underwent neither simply involution nor simply the beginnings of a transition to capitalism. What happened was differentiation within the context of a tenacious and involuting small peasant economy."

He later called this a "syncretic approach that emphasized the partial validity of both generations of scholarship and both classical models" (1991, p. 314).

**Prize.** The Fairbank Prize appears in Huang's institute book listing ("The John K. Fairbank Prize 1985") and in Wikidata (award received: John K. Fairbank Prize). The AHA prize list itself was not checked. Treat the year as "1985 per the author's institute".

## 2. *The Peasant Family and Rural Development in the Yangzi Delta, 1350–1988* (Stanford, 1990)

**UNREAD** (Internet Archive lending copy, restricted; full-text search blocked). The institute lists it with the "Joseph R. Levenson China Research Book Award"; the year was not checked.

Its definitions are known through Huang's own later citations:
- **2002, p. 503 (read).** "what I termed in my work development (involving increased labor productivity), to distinguish it from involution (involving diminishing marginal returns per unit of labor) and intensification (added labor input per unit of land) in the Yangzi delta (1990, 11)."
- **1991, p. 314 (read).** "It was only in my 1990 book that I grasped clearly how empirical paradoxes challenged the unspoken assumptions shared by both generations and models."
  - "Shrinking farm sizes drove peasants to accept involution, or intensification of labor input per unit area at the cost of diminishing marginal returns per workday. In the Yangzi delta, this involution took principally the form of commercialized crop production, especially of cotton, and the sideline production that came with it."
  - "It was a strategy of survival against population pressure, more than of capitalistic profit-maximizing, and it resulted in little capital accumulation."
- **2002, p. 514.** The household as the "backbone" of involution, with spinning at one-third to one-half of farming returns.

The contemporary critique: Ramon H. Myers, "How Did the Modern Chinese Economy Develop?—A Review Article", JAS 50:3 (1991): 604–628 (doi:10.2307/2057563), and Huang, "A Reply to Ramon Myers", JAS 50:3 (1991): 629–633 (doi:10.2307/2057564).
- **Metadata only** (Crossref; Duke UP page blocked, HTTP 403).
- Huang himself names this as the main exchange his books "provoked" (1998, p. 188).
- He also lists Myers among the "neo-Smithian American scholars" (1991, p. 313).
- The content of Myers's critique is **not known here**, so no Myers edge is proposed.

## 3. "The Paradigmatic Crisis in Chinese Studies: Paradoxes in Social and Economic History", *Modern China* 17:3 (July 1991): 299–341

From Huang's site (article/download/12; JSTOR wrapper; text layer with spaces dropped, so spaces are restored below). PDF page = printed − 297.

**READ IN PART:** pp. 299–300, 310–314 and 317–318.

**Kuhn (p. 299).**
- "The field of Chinese social and economic history is in the midst of a paradigmatic crisis ... I am referring to the crisis of confidence in underlying, yet unspoken, assumptions that are shared by opposing constructs. Those have had a greater influence in our profession than the articulated constructs themselves, and have carried the true force of paradigmatic assumptions, in the original sense intended by Thomas Kuhn (1970 [1962])."
- Author's note thanks "my students ... Christopher Isett, Bradly Reed, and Matthew Sommer" and "Kathryn Bernhardt".

**Against both classical models (pp. 310–311).**
- p. 310: "We have, in short, the paradoxical concurrence of vigorous commercialization and subsistence-level peasant farming. This throws into question both the Marxist and Smithian models' assumption of a necessary connection between commercialization and modern development. Those models were based mainly on the exceptional empirical experience of England."
- p. 311: "The distinction between growth and development is crucially important for comprehending China's rural social-economic history ... Labor productivity development lies at the core of the meaning of modern development, and it did not happen in Ming-Qing China."

**Against counterfactuals on both sides (p. 313).**
- "Chinese incipient capitalist scholars maintained that if Western imperialism had not intruded into China, the capitalist sprouts of the Ming and Qing would have led to transformative capitalist development".
- "neo-Smithian American scholars argue explicitly or implicitly that if there had not been war and revolution, the market-driven development of rural China ... would have led to transformative rural modernization (Myers, 1970; Rawski, 1989; Brandt, 1989) ... Theory is maintained at the expense of history."
- "Scholarly inquiry should proceed from history to theory, rather than starting with theory and forcing history to fit it."

**The acknowledgement (p. 317).** "In attempting to rethink intuitive assumptions, I have found theoretical constructs generated from non-Western societies more instructive than the Western-derived models. Thus, my notion of involution has drawn much from the theories of A. V. Chayanov and Clifford Geertz, both based on micro-social studies of non-Western societies."

**Answer to the brief's question.** Yes. Huang acknowledges Geertz's "agricultural involution" explicitly in text read here: 1985, pp. 8 and 11; 1991, p. 317. In 2002 he cites Geertz 1963 only on Japan (p. 515). He builds the economic definition on Chayanov's diminishing returns more than on Geertz's cultural account. The 1985 wording, "the catchy term", credits Geertz with the name for a phenomenon that Chayanov had analysed.

## 4. Retrospects: "Theory and the Study of Modern Chinese History: Four Traps and a Question", *Modern China* 24:2 (April 1998): 183–208

From Huang's site (article/download/13). **READ IN PART:** pp. 183–188 and 193–195. A retrospective source.

- p. 183: "I think of myself as an empirical historian above all else ... Epistemologically, I have consciously tried to proceed from empirical research to theory and back to empirical findings rather than the reverse."
- p. 184: his University of Washington training was "entirely empirical", with "no exposure to theory"; he took to reading theory after his first book, on Liang Qichao (1972).
- p. 186, on resources: "sophisticated academic theorists such as Charles Tilly (1975a, 1975b, 1979) and Jeffrey Paige (1975)"; "A. V. Chayanov's (1986 [1925]) insights into peasant family farms, James Scott's (1976) into community and the moral dimensions of economy, as well as E. P. Thompson's (1966) into the process and the nonmaterial dimensions of class and community formation".
  - "The alliance between Marxist and substantivist ideas that was forged in Tilly's ... work certainly added to the appeal that both sets of ideas held for me."
- p. 188, reception of his books: "A quick look over the various exchanges and symposia that my North China and Yangzi delta (Huang, 1990) books provoked, most especially those with Ramon Myers in the Journal of Asian Studies (Huang, 1991a) and with others in the symposium in Republican China (Huang, 1992), should illustrate the point." In China, his books "were honored by two conferences and a series of symposia discussions" but were criticised by those "committed to the paradigm of 'incipient capitalism'".
  - "contrary to the predictions of Marxist theory, I simply could not find in 'managerial farming' substantial advances in productivity ... (Huang, 1985: esp. chap. 8)."
- pp. 193–195, **critique of Said and "culturalism"**:
  - "While culturalist theories are surely correct to emphasize that facts come with constructed representations, I believe they are wrong to jump from there to the conclusion that facts are no more than representations ... But that is the conclusion that is drawn by Said, on the theoretical inspiration of Michel Foucault". He then quotes *Orientalism*, pp. 272–273.
  - p. 195: "Said's (1978) book, to be sure, makes effective and telling points, especially in the first part ... But the discussions in the second and third parts are much less convincing. The connections drawn become more and more tenuous."

## 6. Reception and critiques of involution

**Pomeranz** (2000, p. 17; 2002; 2003): see the Pomeranz note §§1, 4. Key read lines:
- "I will show that there is little reason to believe this" (2000, p. 17);
- Huang's involution "internally inconsistent ... repeatedly confused marginal and average returns" (2003, p. 167).

**Huang's own concession and restatement** (2003, pp. 157–158): see the Pomeranz note §4.

**Robert C. Allen** (EHR 2009; abstract only): delta labour productivity near the English level, little change 1620–1820, and falling incomes. This measures against both sides: see the Pomeranz note §6.

**Brenner and Isett** (2002): an ally on the delta's outcome but not on its cause. See the Pomeranz note §3.

**Bianco (1996, read; graph source `pe130_bianco_1996`, p. 243)** shows that Pomeranz's 1993 book "ne sépare jamais l'enquête régionale du débat théorique". It sets Huang's involution model beside the "breakthrough" theses of Myers, Rawski and Brandt. This is in-period evidence that Huang's model framed the field's debate by 1993.

**Popular afterlife: *neijuan*.** Emily Feng, NPR, "Feeling burned out? There's a word for that in Mandarin Chinese", 10 December 2025 (WLRN republication, read through WebFetch excerpts).
- "Involution" "first appeared in English with its modern connotation of futility in a 1963 academic tract" by Geertz.
- "Scholar Philip Huang used it in a seminal study ... trying to explain why capitalism did not organically develop in the 20th century".
- The Chinese coinage *neijuan* came from translators of Prasenjit Duara's *Culture, Power, and the State* ("administrative involution").
- The present usage spread after Xiang Biao's 2020 Sixth Tone interview; by 2024–25 it had entered official language ("anti-involution" campaign).
- Journalism, cited only for the term's path. It does not credit Huang with the 2020 sense.
- `prasenjit_duara` is already on the global roster. Duara's book is unread.

**description** (about 280 words):
"A historian of rural China and Chinese law who founded the journal Modern China in 1975 and gave Chinese economic history its concept of 'involution'. Studying North China villages through Japanese field surveys (1985), he drew on Chayanov's analysis of family farms. He took the name from Clifford Geertz, who had given the phenomenon, in Javanese rice farming, 'the catchy term "agricultural involution"'. Family farms that could not dismiss their own members pushed labour 'well beyond the point at which diminishing marginal returns set in'. His Yangzi delta study (1990) separated 'development', a rise in labour productivity, from 'involution', growth in output per unit of land at 'diminishing marginal returns per unit of labor'. In 'The Paradigmatic Crisis in Chinese Studies' (1991) he argued that Marxist 'incipient capitalism' and neo-Smithian market models shared an assumption that commercialization brings development. The Chinese evidence of 'vigorous commercialization and subsistence-level peasant farming' contradicted it, and inquiry 'should proceed from history to theory'. He acknowledged that 'my notion of involution has drawn much from the theories of A. V. Chayanov and Clifford Geertz'. From the 1990s he turned to legal history. He studied a 'third realm' between informal mediation and courts, and the practice of civil justice, taking 'practice' from Bourdieu but preferring 'the study of practice as historical process'. He also rejected culturalist claims that 'facts are no more than representations', which he attributed to Said. In 2002 he challenged Kenneth Pomeranz's claim that England and the Yangzi delta were comparable before 1800, and their exchange became a centre of the Great Divergence debate. 'Involution' later passed, through other translations, into Chinese public speech as neijuan."
