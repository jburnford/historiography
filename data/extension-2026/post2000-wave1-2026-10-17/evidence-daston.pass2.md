## 1. "The Moral Economy of Science", *Osiris* 2nd ser. 10 (1995): 2–24 (JSTOR 301910)

Hosted scan: https://www.ias.edu/sites/default/files/sss/pdfs/Moral-Daston.pdf. **READ IN PART:** pp. 3–5 and 21–24 (section I and the conclusion). The middle sections, on quantification and empiricism, are unread. The text layer has dropped spaces, restored below.

**Definition and thesis.**
- p. 3: "I will nonetheless claim that not only does science have what I will call a moral economy (indeed, several); these moral economies are moreover constitutive of those features conventionally (and, to my mind, correctly) deemed most characteristic of science as a way of knowing ... certain forms of empiricism, quantification, and objectivity itself are not simply compatible with moral economies; they require moral economies."
- p. 4: "What I mean by a moral economy is a web of affect-saturated values that stand and function in well-defined relationship to one another." "economy also has a deliberately old-fashioned ring: it refers not to money, markets, labor, production, and distribution of material resources, but rather to an organized system that displays certain regularities".

**E. P. Thompson: the term borrowed, the sense distinguished** (p. 3 n. 1, the author's note).
- "I am grateful to John Carson and Nathan Reingold for pointing out that my use of the term moral economy diverges significantly from E. P. Thompson's in 'The Moral Economy of the English Crowd in the Eighteenth Century,' Past and Present, Feb. 1971".
- "My appeal here to 'economies' of affects and values has little to do with Thompson's accounts of corn markets and the tradition of 'setting the price' by persuasion or riot, although it does appeal to a broader sense of 'legitimizing notion.'"

**Bachelard** (p. 4 and n. 4). "As Gaston Bachelard decades ago remarked, to imbue objects or actions with emotion is almost always thereby to valorize them, and vice versa." Note 4 cites "Libido et connaissance objective", *La Formation de l'esprit scientifique* (1938).

**Fleck and Elias** (p. 5, nn. 6–7; p. 22).
- "To extend Ludwik Fleck's terminology, what is meant here is a Gefühls- as well as a Denkkollektiv."
- n. 7: "I have in mind a gradual shaping of a collective personality akin (and, as will be seen below, sometimes identical) to Norbert Elias's 'civilizing process'".
- p. 22: "Norbert Elias argued that all complex coordinations of human activity partake of the 'civilizing process,' that is, the 'permanent self-control' demanded by 'the lengthening of the chains of social action and interdependence,' and science is no exception here."

**Merton** (p. 5). "Moral economies belong to the interior of Merton's black box."

**Mechanical and aperspectival objectivity** (pp. 21–22).
- "If the emblem of mechanical objectivity is the photograph, the motto of aperspectival objectivity could be 'the view from nowhere,' in Nagel's brilliant oxymoron."
- "Aperspectival objectivity was the ethos of the interchangeable and therefore featureless observer".

**Conclusion: historical epistemology** (pp. 23–24).
- "I do not mean to suggest that moral economies in science are the Universal Key to all Mythologies."
- "And because moral economies are part of what one might call historical epistemology—a history of the categories of facticity, evidence, objectivity, and so forth—they should interest the philosopher as well as the historian."
- p. 24: "Here there is ample room for a revival of Weberian-style historical sociology that carries the study of the cultural origins and characters of scientific rationality beyond a few mournful sighs over the disenchantment of the world."

**Bearing.**
- This is her own statement, in 1995, of a programme she names "historical epistemology", with Bachelard cited.
- She defines it as a history of epistemic categories, which differs from the atlas entry's Bachelard–Canguilhem sense.
- Elias is an explicit resource.
- Thompson's term is borrowed in a deliberately different sense.

## 2. Historical epistemology at the MPIWG: Sturm (2008)

Thomas Sturm, "What (Good) is Historical Epistemology? Reflections on a Conference at the MPIWG, July 2008", *Research Topics*, Max Planck Institute for the History of Science, September 2008 (https://mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/sites/default/files/2017-07/fs1_sturm_en_web.pdf). **READ IN FULL** (4 pp.; text column).

- "One such challenge, which has recently attracted many historians of science, has been named 'historical epistemology'. There are several different versions of this approach".
- "The way the program was organized reflected three versions of historical epistemology, as they are practiced by researchers at the MPIWG."
- "(1) According to Lorraine Daston, historical epistemology raises the Kantian question about the preconditions that make thinking this or that idea possible, but views these preconditions as thoroughly historical. Thus, not only our knowledge and evidence changes or grows throughout history, but our understanding of what can count as knowledge can be historicized as well."
- "(2) Hans-Jörg Rheinberger's version ... focuses strongly upon the material – especially experimental and technological – conditions". "(3) Jürgen Renn views historical epistemology as an historically founded theory of long-term developments of scientific knowledge."
- "There must also be a parallel program of reconstructing what the agents thought were permissible or recommendable steps, or how they understood such concepts as knowledge, evidence, observation, probability, objectivity, and proof."
- The conference led to a special issue of *Erkenntnis* and a volume edited by Feest and Sturm (2011). That is metadata from the MPIWG news item seen in search; it was not opened.

This is a reader's explicit statement that Daston's is one of three named versions of historical epistemology at her institute. Sturm was then an MPIWG fellow, so this is an institutional account.

## 4. *Objectivity* (with Peter Galison; Zone Books, 2007) and its critics

The book is **UNREAD**. It is known here through one review read in full.

**Michelle Henning, "A Picture Of Virtue", *New Formations* 64 (2008).** Pp. 154–159 appear in the extracted text; the first page is inferred as 154 from the running heads 155–159. **READ IN FULL** (10 pp. extracted).
- p. 154: "Scientific objectivity, Lorraine Daston and Peter Galison claim, is best conceived as a virtue. Their use of a concept of virtue is persuasive". "Virtues are aimed at through 'techniques of the self' in Foucault's sense, though this is not a straightforwardly Foucaultian argument."
- p. 154, quoting the book (p. 197): "'the emergence of scientific objectivity … goes hand in glove with the emergence of scientific subjectivity'".
- pp. 154–155: "mechanical objectivity" and "structural objectivity" are distinguished from "'truth-to-nature' and 'trained judgment', which exist (respectively) before and after objectivity, but also coexist with it."
- p. 157: "they dismiss the rise of photography, along with various other social and technical changes as only of minor relevance". "By seeing the different 'epistemic virtues' and the scientific selves they entail as co-existent, they also distance themselves from Foucault's theory of historical rupture and Kuhn's account of paradigm shifts in science."
- p. 157, criticism: "Though Daston and Galison are right to distrust the notion of historical explanation as the search for deep causes, the book loses something in its inattention to the social." The example is Carnap and Neurath's Unity of Science movement, a "depoliticised concept of communicability" (p. 158).

### 5.2 Node text revisions: `lorraine_daston`

**date_label:** "Classical Probability in the Enlightenment 1988 · ‘The Image of Objectivity’ 1992 · ‘The Moral Economy of Science’ 1995 · Max Planck Institute director 1995–2019 · Wonders and the Order of Nature 1998 · Objectivity 2007 · Rules 2022"

**description** (replacement, about 200 words):
"A historian of science who treats the categories of knowing as having histories. After Classical Probability in the Enlightenment (1988), she and Peter Galison distinguished 'mechanical objectivity' in scientific images and atlases from other components of objectivity (1992). In 'The Moral Economy of Science' (1995) she argued that empiricism, quantification and objectivity 'require moral economies', which she called 'a web of affect-saturated values', and named the enterprise 'historical epistemology—a history of the categories of facticity, evidence, objectivity'. She drew on Norbert Elias's civilizing process for the self-restraint of scientific observers, and took the phrase 'moral economy' from E. P. Thompson only in 'a broader sense'. With Katharine Park she traced Wonders and the Order of Nature, 1150–1750 (1998). As a director of the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science (1995–2019) she led one of the versions of historical epistemology practised there. Objectivity (2007, with Galison) made objectivity one 'epistemic virtue' beside truth-to-nature and trained judgment, coexisting rather than succeeding one another. A reviewer found it inattentive 'to the social'. Rules (2022) turned to the algorithms, laws and models that govern conduct."

**representative_figures_and_works:**
"Lorraine Daston; Classical Probability in the Enlightenment (1988); with Peter Galison, ‘The Image of Objectivity’ (Representations, 1992); ‘The Moral Economy of Science’ (Osiris, 1995); with Katharine Park, Wonders and the Order of Nature, 1150–1750 (1998); ed., Biographies of Scientific Objects (2000); ed., Things That Talk (2004); with Peter Galison, Objectivity (2007); Rules: A Short History of What We Live By (Lawrence Stone Lectures, 2022). "Read: 'The Image of Objectivity' (1992) at pp. 81–82; 'The Moral Economy of Science' (1995) at pp. 3–5 and 21–24; Sturm's 2008 MPIWG report in full; Henning's 2008 review of Objectivity in full.
