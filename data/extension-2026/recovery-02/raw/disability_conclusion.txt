L926@P19: As Esme Cleall suggests, examining disability in imperial or colonial
L927@P19: contexts can unsettle popular views about disabled people. Most histories
L928@P19: of disability and empire tend to consider the impact of colonialism on
L929@P19: disabled people. Yet, Cleall takes a radically different approach. In her
L930@P19: study of an emigration scheme devised and promoted by Jane Groom

L932@P19: how disabled people were oppressed by colonial endeavour, but also
L933@P19: how they participated and benefited from the practice of Empire’.80
L934@P19: Groom’s plan proposed and facilitated the settlement of deaf British
L935@P19: migrants on land taken from First Nations people in North-West Canada.
L936@P19: In highlighting this, Cleall complicates our understanding of disabled
L937@P19: people’s relationship to colonialism, reminding us that they too have been
L938@P19: agents of empire, complicit in the colonial project.
L939@P19: Like recent work on medicalisation, Cleall’s research indicates that
L940@P19: disability history is maturing. While disability histories continue to
L941@P19: explore the impact of momentous historical processes on disabled people,
L942@P19: there is now a growing recognition of disabled people’s contribution to
L943@P19: those processes. Colonisation, medicalisation, and industrialisation, for

L945@P19: disabled people helped to propel them. This shift in interpretative balance

L948@P19: or ‘discriminatory’ forces. Sometimes disabled people have embraced or
