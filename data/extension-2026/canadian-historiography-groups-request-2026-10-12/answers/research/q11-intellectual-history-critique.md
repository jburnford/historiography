# Q11: published critiques of English-Canadian intellectual & cultural history

Prepared 2026-10-12 for `cdn_intellectual_cultural`. Research only: nothing has been applied to the draft or the atlas. Structured version: `q11-intellectual-history-critique.json`. Counts script: `q11_citation_counts.py`.

## Answer
Yes, published critiques of the field as a whole exist, and the two strongest were read in full.

- **Clarence Karr, "What Happened to Canadian Intellectual History?", *Acadiensis* 18:2 (Spring 1989), 158–74** (full text read). This is the direct critique.
  - Karr says the field is "in eclipse", while social history "has attracted the brightest of the young scholars".
  - He analyses three attributes of the field: its "preoccupation with delineating the national mind", its "propensity to place everything in an atmosphere of crisis", and "its narrow, elitist, biographical methodology" (p. 160).
  - The group-biography method that followed Berger's *Sense of Power* focuses on "an elite group composed primarily of academics, journalists and clergymen". As a result, little is known about what the wider population thought (p. 169).
  - His named targets are Berger, McKillop, Cook, Shortt, Levitt, Allen, and Francis's *Underhill*.
  - He calls the individual books "excellent studies within their self-imposed context".
- **Susan Mann Trofimenkoff, "Nationalism, Feminism & Canadian Intellectual History", *Canadian Literature* 83 (Winter 1979), 7–20** (full text read; special issue on intellectual history). This is a feminist critique.
  - The field dates from the Quiet Revolution and is anchored to nationalism and presentism.
  - Its practitioners "have implied that women are virtually non-existent". "Most intellectual historians have been men", and women's history grew up "in isolation from intellectual history".
- **Donald Wright, "Discourse, Power and Tradition", *Acadiensis* 24:2 (Spring 1995), 122–34** (full text read). Wright names the charges against the field, partly in order to answer them.
  - "After the dismissal of ideas as epiphenominal to demographic and economic forces, the most common charge levelled against intellectual history is that of representation" (p. 132).
  - He concedes that the history of political thought "tends to focus on elites".
- **Michael Gauvreau, "Beyond the Search for Intellectuals: On the Paucity of Paradigms in the Writing of Canadian Intellectual History"**, in Friesen and Owram, eds., *Thinkers and Dreamers* (UTP, 2010), 53–90, DOI 10.3138/9781442690165-004.
  - The metadata was checked. The argument comes only from Denis McKim's H-Canada review (Sept. 2011), which I read.
  - According to McKim, Gauvreau takes intellectual historians "to task for their abiding interest in articulate elites" and for a national frame. These kept the field from engaging with social history and transnational approaches. He credits recent work on churches and governments for moving beyond this.

### The candidates you suggested
- **Social historians' charges of elitism.** Karr (1989) frames the charge and Wright (1995) reports it as "the most common charge". I did not find and check a named social historian's own published attack, for example in *Labour/Le Travail*.
- **Palmer, *Descent into Discourse* (Temple UP, 1990).** The book record and six reviews were checked; none of the reviews was read.
  - It is a general critique of poststructuralism in social history ("language is not life").
  - Whether it discusses Canadian intellectual or cultural historians is **not verified**.
  - Its only checked link to this field is Wright's phrase that Valverde avoids a "descent into discourse".
  - It should not anchor the critique sentence.
- **Reviews of *Quest of the Folk*.** *Acadiensis* 35:1 (Autumn 2005) ran a forum on the book (Creelman, Dutcher, Marquis, M. Wright, and McKay's reply).
  - **Marquis** (read in full) challenges the book's "selectivity and applicability for the rest of the region". He points to "a countervailing discourse of modernity", and asks how much impact Creighton and Black actually had, citing Conrad (AHR 1996) and Archie Green (CHR 1996).
  - **McKay's reply** reports that some readers found the book "theoretically simplistic" or "pretentious and overbearing". He accepts that antimodernism may hold "more elements of resistance" than he allowed in 1994.
  - Shore's 1996 review in *L/LT* praised the book, according to Marquis.
  - Tye (*Ethnologies* 1995) was metadata only; Érudit was behind a bot check.
- **Owram, "Intellectual History in the Land of Limited Identities".** Confirmed as *Journal of Canadian Studies* 24:3 (Fall 1989), 114–28, DOI 10.3138/jcs.24.3.114. The abstract was read.
  - It is a self-assessment, not a critique. The field's roots "tend to be nationalist". Regional differences matter less than in other subfields, and are sharpest between francophone and anglophone writers.
  - It is useful for the draft's scope note on Lamonde, not for a critique sentence.
  - The same JCS special issue includes McKillop's "Culture, Intellect, and Context" (7–31; abstract read). In it he says few mainstream historians write cultural history, and that provincial and regional framing weakens the field.
- **Other sources checked**
  - Tippett, "The Writing of English-Canadian Cultural History, 1970–85", *CHR* 67:4 (1986), DOI 10.3138/chr-067-04-04. Metadata only; Litt (2009) reports that she found no synthetic overviews by historians.
  - Bumsted, *Acadiensis* 7:1 (1977), read in full. Sympathetic, not a critique.
  - McKillop, "Nationalism, Identity and Canadian Intellectual History" (*QQ* 1974; *Contours*, 1987). Metadata only; not read.

## Recommendation
Anchor the sentence on **Karr 1989**, with **Trofimenkoff 1979** as the second source. Both were read in full and both criticize the field as a whole. Gauvreau 2010 would be the best later voice, but the chapter itself should be read first. Proposed wording:

> Critics charged the field with what Clarence Karr (1989) called a "narrow, elitist, biographical methodology": group biographies of academics, journalists and clergy, organized around a national mind and moments of intellectual crisis, said little about what most Canadians thought. Susan Mann Trofimenkoff (1979) had already argued that a field anchored to nationalism had left women almost invisible.

Optional addition, only after Gauvreau's chapter has been read: "Michael Gauvreau (2010) later faulted its focus on articulate elites and its national frame."

For the cultural strand, Marquis (2005) could support a short line in McKay's context row, such as "critics questioned how far its Nova Scotia findings applied to the wider region". It should not serve as the critique of the field. A critic row for Karr is possible. Trofimenkoff was CHA president in 1984–85; check whether she is already in the atlas before adding her.
