# Environment family: what the field's own self-accounts say about founders, precursors and frameworks

Prepared 2026-10-13 for the editor's question: who should be front and centre in the Environment family timeline (environmental history; geography and environmental inquiry), and should Braudel or the Annales be shown as a major influence?

Method: read the field's own historiographical self-accounts, using full text wherever I could get it, and record verbatim quotes with page numbers. I did not use Wikipedia or memory as evidence. Every quote below comes from text I actually opened. Where I saw only a translation, an excerpt, an abstract, or a quotation inside another author's text, I say so. Page numbers come from the running heads in the copies I read. Where a copy has its own pagination (a course reader, a working paper), I say which pagination I am using.

Date labels: **[pre-2000]**, **[2000–2005]**, **[post-2005 retrospective]**.

## A. Cronon, "The Uses of Environmental History" (1993) [pre-2000]

This was the ASEH presidential address. It is a self-account of what the field is for, not a genealogy. It names exemplary practitioners in a list of "interventions" (pp. 2–3) and footnotes a list of "lessons" (pp. 8–10) to the books that taught them. No precursor figure is named except Leopold, whom Cronon uses as a moral and literary voice.

- **Origins:** "Like the several other “new” histories born or reenergized in the wake of the 1960s—women’s history, African-American history, Chicano history, gay and lesbian history, and the new social history generally—environmental history has always had an undeniable relation to the political movement that helped spawn it." (p. 2)
- **Nash:** "Roderick Nash’s Wilderness and the American Mind has played a significant role in helping frame debates about wilderness protection in the three decades since its publication." (pp. 2–3). A note criticises him: "environmentalists can also tell linear narratives about heroic environmentalism: Nash’s whig history of wilderness consciousness and the rights of nature is probably the most obvious case in point." (n. 27, p. 22)
- **Hays:** "Samuel Hays’s Conservation and the Gospel of Efficiency and Beauty, Health, and Permanence though less obviously partisan in their politics than Nash’s book, speak just as powerfully to major trends in conservation and environmental politics in the twentieth century." (p. 3)
- **Worster:** "Among the most consistently interventionist of environmental historians has been Donald Worster, whose unflinching moral vision has never failed to produce works of history that are also passionately committed to change." (p. 3). Also: "Dust Bowl and Rivers of Empire located the origins of environmental degradation in capitalist world-views and modes of production" (p. 3)
- **Merchant:** "Carolyn Merchant joined Worster in bringing an environmentalist perspective to the history of science, but combined it with a more feminist approach" (p. 3). The lesson footnoted to her (n. 18): "Men and women often experience the world in very different ways, so that one cannot hope to understand the way a culture relates to an environment without examining the ways it engenders the natural world." (p. 9)
- **Pyne:** "Steven Pyne’s epic histories of fire have consistently tried to persuade present-day resource managers of the complexity of their task. Pyne has even gone so far as to author a textbook on fire management practices" (p. 3). Also the fire lesson (n. 17), pp. 8–9.
- **Crosby:** lessons footnoted to *Columbian Exchange* and *Ecological Imperialism* (nn. 16, 25): "When people migrate from one ecosystem to another, they carry with them other organisms—plants, animals, microbes—whose success or failure in the new location is often crucial in determining the success or failure of the migration." (p. 8), and "Beware of bugs that come from afar." (p. 10)
- **Richard White:** cited for *Land Use…Island County* and *Roots of Dependency* (nn. 15, 23). His lesson is the horse as "a complex cultural entity" (p. 9)
- **Leopold:** "It is the history recorded in Aldo Leopold’s tree rings, the history recorded by the marks of his saw upon the good oak as he cut it down" (p. 19). Also: "At this and several other points in this essay, I trust that readers will hear my echoes of Aldo Leopold's Sand County Almanac" (n. 11, p. 21)
- **Braudel:** "Perhaps this is why we feel some kinship with a Braudelian vision of history in which the different time scales of la longue durée, la vie matérielle, and l’histoire événementielle weave together to form the tapestry of the past. Although our general bias is often toward the longue durée, we understand that the interactions of environment, economy, political institutions, social norms, cultural values, and natural processes are endlessly complex." (p. 14). Note 29 (p. 22) cites *The Mediterranean*.
- **Field-level framework claim:** "One important contribution of environmental history, then, has been to reintroduce materialist styles of analysis to the study of past human-environment interactions while trying to finesse a full-blown determinism." (p. 13)
- **Also named:** Joel Tarr (p. 3), Arthur McEvoy (n. 22), Martin Melosi (n. 24), Calvin Martin (n. 15), Raymond Williams, whose "Ideas of Nature" is called "a most wise and profound essay" (n. 19), and Emerson's Transcendentalism, as a root of romantic values that environmental history historicises (p. 12)
- **Absent:** Thoreau, Marsh, Carson, Webb, Malin, Sauer, Mumford, Febvre, Bloch, Le Roy Ladurie, Guha and Grove are not named.

## B. Worster, "Doing Environmental History" (1988), abridged reprint [pre-2000]

Page numbers below are the Merchant reader's (ch. 1, pp. 2–9). Original pages are given where Isenberg 2013 cites them.

- **Origins:** "The idea of environmental history first appeared in the 1970s, as conferences on the global predicament were taking place and popular environmentalist movements were gathering momentum in several countries." (reader p. 3; original p. 290 per Isenberg n. 2). Also: "Environmental history was, therefore, born out of a moral purpose, with strong political commitments behind it, but also became, as it matured, a scholarly enterprise that had neither any simple, nor any single, moral or political agenda to promote." (reader pp. 3–4)
- **The framework:** "There are three levels on which the new history proceeds, three clusters of issues it addresses … The first deals with understanding nature itself … The second level in this history brings in the socioeconomic realm as it interacts with the environment … Then, forming a third level for the historian is that more intangible and uniquely human type of encounter—the purely mental or intellectual" (reader p. 4; original p. 293 per Isenberg n. 19)
- **Leopold, the non-historian originator of the idea:** "Some years back the scientist and conservationist Aldo Leopold projected such an alliance when he spoke of “an ecological interpretation of history.”" (reader p. 6)
- **Darwin:** "ever since Charles Darwin, ecology has been concerned with past as well as present interactions" (reader p. 5)
- **Absent from the excerpt:** no other precursor or historian is named. Because the excerpt omits original pp. 299–301, 303–305 and 306–307, absence here is weaker evidence than absence in a full text.

## C. Worster, "Transformations of the Earth" (JAH 1990), Portuguese translation [pre-2000]

- **Leopold** opens the field's charter essay (p. 23): "Há quarenta anos atrás, um homem sábio e visionário, o conservacionista e biólogo da vida selvagem, Aldo Leopold, de Wisconsin, reivindicou uma “interpretação ecológica da História” … Levou algum tempo para os historiadores darem atenção ao conselho de Leopold, mas finalmente o campo da história ambiental começou a ganhar forma". My gloss: forty years ago Leopold called for an "ecological interpretation of history"; it took historians some time to heed him, but the field finally began to take shape.
- **White 1985 and the Annales**, in note 5 (p. 42). This is the most important pre-2000 statement on the Annales question that I found: "O melhor esforço para traçar a emergência do campo, pelo menos em uma parte influente do mundo, é o de WHITE, R. “American Environmental History: The Development of a New Historical Field” … White argumenta que o estudo da fronteira e da história do oeste tem sido a influência formativa neste campo. Uma outra fonte de idéias importante, realmente retirada da influência de Frederick Jackson Turner, tem sido a dos historiadores e geógrafos franceses, particularmente Fernand Braudel, Lucien Febvre e Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, todos associados à revista dos Annales."
  - My gloss: White's essay is the best effort to trace the field's emergence, at least in one influential part of the world. White argues that frontier and western history has been the formative influence. Another important source of ideas, well removed from Turner's influence, has been the French historians and geographers, particularly Braudel, Febvre and Le Roy Ladurie, all associated with *Annales*.
  - **Caveat:** this is a translation. The English wording, and the JAH page of n. 5, should be checked against the original before it is quoted in the atlas.
- **Crosby:** "Tomo a expressão do seguinte texto de CROSBY, A. Ecological Imperialism" (n. 4, p. 42). My gloss: Worster takes the phrase "ecological imperialism" from Crosby.
- **Webb and Malin** are good introductions to Plains ecology and settlement (n. 28, p. 44). **Pyne**'s *Fire in America* is cited for fire (n. 13, p. 43).
- **Translator's note (not Worster's words):** "Donald Worster … é considerado um dos fundadores da “História Ambiental”." (p. 23). My gloss: Worster is considered one of the founders of environmental history.

## F. McNeill, "Observations on the Nature and Culture of Environmental History" (2003) [2000–2005 retrospective; explicitly international]

**Precursors (§IV, "The Prehistory of Environmental History")**

- **Annales:** "It was against this often dry and staid political and intellectual history that Lucien Febvre and Marc Bloch rebelled, c. 1915–1930, founding Annales and gradually legitimizing, for professional historians, new fields of inquiry that had cattle, trees, diseases, and bodily functions (or at least their results) in them. But the Annales historians were, many of them, strangely blind to evolving environments. Fernand Braudel saw in Mediterranean landscapes a powerful influence of geography, to which he devoted the first third of La méditerranée, but he normally imagined it as an unchanging force. With few exceptions his writing on environments conformed to the spirit of his phrase l’histoire immobile. His pupil Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, however, embraced environmental change in two of his major books, starting in the late 1950s. But Braudel himself never outgrew his immersion in the static French human geography of his youth, in which environment served as a constraint on possible paths of history. To a very limited extent, then, Annales historians were engaged in environmental history avant la lettre, although they never adopted the term before 1974 nor, so far as I can tell, thought of themselves in such terms. Their general approach however proved inspirational for many who would become environmental historians, as it did for practitioners in so many genres within the discipline of history." (p. 14)
- **Turner, Webb, Malin:** "An additional source of inspiration, mainly for U.S. environmental historians, was and to some extent remains, the frontier history of Frederick Jackson Turner and Walter Prescott Webb, and the explicitly ecological history of the Great Plains of James Malin." (p. 14). And: "Turner and Webb were mainly interested in social aspects of the frontier story, but Malin engaged directly with ecological change. They were all prominent historians whom no one educated in U.S. history was likely to miss." (p. 15)
- **Marsh:** "Equally inspirational to U.S. environmental historians, if much less so elsewhere, was the book of the diplomat and polymath George Perkins Marsh, Man and Nature, first published in 1864." (p. 15)
- **The decisive stimulus:** "While intellectual influences from Turner and Febvre to Malin and Le Roy Ladurie helped inspire and shape environmental history, its strongest stimulus came from outside the academy. The popular ecology movement of the late 1960s and 1970s was decisive in the emergence of environmental history as a self-conscious genre in Europe and North America" (p. 15)

**Annales as an institution (§III)**

- "Surprising for its neglect of environmental history is the most admired and influential history journal of the twentieth century, Annales … In 1974 Le Roy Ladurie presented a collection of articles by various authors entitled “Histoire et Environnement,” … This was self-conscious environmental history (mixed with pieces of contemporary commentary), but it did not herald anything new in Annales." (p. 12)
- On France: "perhaps the stature of the Annales group was such that there seemed, for a long time, no point in trying new approaches to geographical and environmental themes. The masters had spoken." (p. 29)

**Founders and frameworks**

- **Hays:** "Among the American pioneers in this field was Samuel Hays, who wrote about U.S. conservation politics as early as the 1950s." (p. 8)
- **Nash:** "Roderick Nash, who in 1970 offered one of the very first courses in environmental history" (n. 39, p. 15). McNeill quotes Nash's 1972 PHR article: "I was responding to cries for environmental responsibility which reached a crescendo in the first months of [1970]." McNeill cites it as "Pacific Historical Review 363 (1974)", which looks like a citation slip. Note 42 (p. 16) calls *Wilderness and the American Mind* "The most important book in this vein".
- **Cronon:** "in what is probably the most widely read environmental history book yet written, Changes in the Land, William Cronon took on southern New England c. 1600–1800 and showed more interest in pigs than in Puritanism." (p. 16). Also: "Cronon’s Changes in the Land inspired direct imitation." (p. 37)
- **The core trio, read internationally:** "the American historians, especially Worster, Cronon, and Crosby, were read almost wherever environmental history aroused interest." (p. 19)
- **Worster:** his call to internationalize the field (n. 50) went largely unheeded: "Worster and others have called for an internationalization of environmental history, but Americanists are among the least responsive to this cry." (p. 18)
- **Crosby:** *The Columbian Exchange* is "one of the landmark works in environmental history" (p. 24). *Ecological Imperialism* "contains one of the most elegant arguments found anywhere in environmental history" (p. 33). The common critique is recorded: "Some readers felt that Crosby had relocated historical agency … from humans to wheat and whooping cough" (p. 33)
- **Theory (§IX):** "The most cogent was probably the one elaborated in slightly different versions by both Donald Worster and Arthur McEvoy, in which nature itself, the economy, and human ideas and images of nature form the three building blocks of environmental history. … they have sparked no groundswells of enthusiasm nor attracted sizable schools of followers. More influential have been mere models of how to do environmental history." (p. 37)
- **White:** "Richard White’s concept of “middle ground,” … struck historians of Japan and medieval Austria as useful for their own studies." (p. 37)
- **Merchant:** "Carolyn Merchant has offered a more formal theory that tries to take gender into account, and which avoids the notion that women are inherently closer to nature. It adds another variable to that of the Worster-McEvoy trio, inserting reproduction" (p. 38). Then: "Whatever the merits of this scheme, it has not attracted wide imitation within environmental history. The signal contribution, I think, of Merchant (and others) is to insist that historians consider the variable experiences of males and females" (p. 39)
- **Pyne:** cited in n. 112 (p. 32) for the global-scale fire histories *World Fire* and *Vestal Fire*. McNeill sets Goudsblom against "less flamboyant prose than Pyne prefers".
- **Guha:** "Perhaps the crucial book here was Ramachandra Guha, The Unquiet Woods" (n. 64, p. 21). His harmony thesis is criticised: "This view was beguilingly expressed by Madhav Gadgil and Ramachandra Guha in their accessible survey, This Fissured Land" (p. 21)
- **Grove:** cited for imperial environmental history, El Niño history and *Green Imperialism* (nn. 22, 63, 95; pp. 10, 21, 27)
- **Thoreau, Leopold and Carson appear only as subjects that cultural historians study:** "The conservation movement, the establishment of parks, concepts of nature and wilderness, the writings of Thoreau, Aldo Leopold, and Rachel Carson all received new analytic treatment" (p. 17)
- **Radkau and Hughes** wrote the first world environmental histories by professional historians (p. 31). Also named: Mark Elvin (p. 13), Tarr and Melosi (n. 45), Pfister (n. 19), Glacken (n. 31), and the Thomas volume *Man's Role in Changing the Face of the Earth* (n. 106)

## I. Winiwarter et al., Europe survey (2004) [2000–2005; non-US]

- **France section (Massard-Guilbaud):** "it was not before the end of the 1990s that the phrase ‘history of the environment’ appeared in this country, with a very small number of earlier exceptions. This assertion challenges the vision a number of non-French historians have of Braudel and other historians of the École des Annales as pioneers in the field – which they were not, in spite of the misleading translation of the title of one of Braudel’s major works." (p. 513)
- Note 52 (p. 527) explains the mistranslation: the first volume of *The Identity of France*, "which in French is ‘Space and History’ was rendered as ‘Environment and History’ in the English translation, which is unfortunate, as Braudel clearly was not working with the concept of ‘environment’". Note 51 lists the Annales special issue on environment and history as one of the "main exceptions". The note dates it vol. 29, 1973; McNeill gives 1974.
- **Britain:** "Chris Smout is one of the founding fathers of environmental history in Britain in the modern era, inheriting the legacy of the earliest pioneers (W.G. Hoskins, Oliver Rackham and Peter Brimblecombe)" (p. 505). Grove is named among "the main contributors over the past decade" (p. 505), and as *Environment and History*'s "founding editor" who "quickly globalised its scope" (p. 502).
- Worster's "second level" is used as an organising category (n. near p. 519).

## L. Sörlin and Warde, working paper (2005) [2000–2005; European]

Page numbers are the working paper's own.

- **Nash coined the term:** "The expression ‘environmental history’ was coined, at least within the genealogy that has led to a recognizable field of academic endeavour, by American historian Roderick Nash in 1972." (p. 1)
- **Glacken:** "The standard reference here remains Clarence Glacken and his superb bird’s eye view of geographical ideas, Traces on the Rhodian Shore (1967)" (p. 1)
- **Leading reflective practitioners:** "There have been quite a number of reflective essays by leading practitioners in the field over the past fifteen years or more: Donald Worster, Alfred Crosby, William Cronon, to name a few." (p. 2)
- **US dominance:** "The most influential work in the field has been done in the United States, which is also where the first teaching programs emerged and where the large majority of environmental history specialists are active." (p. 2)
- **The Annales as a strand, not a founder:** in their list of the field's disciplinary strands, "‘Annales’ school-type regional studies" sits beside historical geography, historical ecology, history of science, pollution and urban studies, and "American environmental / frontier history" (p. 9). Also: "Strands of historical geography have of course been highly influential on environmental historians – from Vidal de la Blache in France, through H.C. Darby in England, to contemporary practitioners such as Graeme Wynn in Canada." (p. 9). And: "The influence of geography on history, which was clearly more prominent in the heyday of Annales history, seems to have channelled into more specialised areas." (p. 10). They also note that Delort and Walter's synthesis "self-consciously treads along the path set out by an edition of the journal Annales in 1974" (p. 7)
- **Braudel:** "Within Europe, the environment all too frequently ceases to have an active role in history from around 1500, and even in the seminal writings of Braudel it remains the backcloth, the stage for history, rather than an active force." (p. 16)
- **Seminal works:** "Carolyn Merchant’s The Death of Nature (1980), Alfred Crosby’s Ecological Imperialism (1986), William Cronon’s Nature’s Metropolis (1991), Richard Grove’s Green Imperialism (1995), John McNeill’s Something New Under the Sun (2000). Apart from making the plain observation that the large majority of these authors are American, one may perhaps more interestingly note that they are all works of synthesis." (p. 13)
- **Grove's reception** (relevant to the existing Pomeranz entry): "Grove has been quoted in C. A. Bayly’s recent The Birth of the Modern World, 1780-1914 (2004), and in Kenneth Pomeranz’s influential, if controversial, The Great Divergence (2000)." (p. 14)
- **Radkau:** among the national syntheses, "Radkau for Germany" (p. 7)

## M. AHR Conversation (Dec. 2008) [post-2005 retrospective; global panel]

- **Sedrez on the canonical reviews of the field:** "This first question reminds me of some very classic “reviews of the field,” such as the roundtable with Don Worster, Richard White, Carolyn Merchant, William Cronon, Stephen Pyne, and Al Crosby in 1990, and the two pieces by Richard White in the Pacific Historical Review in 1985 and 2001." She adds: "The first and most obvious change, in my point of view, is the expansion of environmental history to non-U.S. areas" (p. 1432)
- **Sedrez (Latin Americanist):** "I have long maintained that all environmental historians owe a debt of gratitude to Fernand Braudel (a geographer) and his three movements of historical time—individual time, social time, and geographical time—even if this debt requires them to challenge his formulation in that wonderful masterpiece. (I admit, I am a big Braudel’s fan; sue me.)" (p. 1433). Later: "the growing literature on environmental justice and “environmentalism of the poor” (to use the concept of Ramachandra Guha and Juan Martínez-Alier) has been fundamental for the development of environmental history in Latin America and India" (p. 1463)
- **Crosby 1995, quoted by Sedrez:** environmental historians had founded “their own sect, the American Society for Environmental History, with its own journal.” Also: “the last ten years of the AHR includes more pages on film than on environmental history. Environmental history exists as an independent subdivision of the discipline, but it remains a minor one.” (both Crosby p. 1188, quoted at p. 1433)
- **Hoffmann (medievalist):** "Deep into the 1980s, self-conscious environmental history was largely an affair of Americanists and a few modernists in other fields. Even a pioneer of the long-term and broad vision, Al Crosby, in his 1995 AHR overview of environmental history still assumed that state records, scientific reports, policy debates, and other attributes of modernity were essential to research in it." (p. 1433)
- **Perdue (China historian):** "The two foundational traditions of the Annales school and the American frontier school have both contributed to environmental history in Asia, in different ways. … The Bloch-Braudel tradition of attention to agrarian structures, the longue durée, and constraints of nature inspired me and others to use local gazetteers and official sources to reconstruct long-term agrarian change in the early modern period. But the Annales tradition emphasized the limits of human activity faced with natural phenomena, and the relative unimportance of great political events." (p. 1437). Perdue goes on: "The American tradition stresses much more the transformative power of capitalism and frontier settlement, in its agrarian (early Cronon) and industrial (later Cronon, Worster, etc.) modes. Unexpectedly, I found this perspective more useful than the Annales for my second book" (p. 1437). Also: "(Braudel’s “structures” were in fact not static constructions, but also included human and natural dynamics.)" (p. 1443)
- **McCann (Africanist):** "I think one of the failures of the Annales (though I have always been a fan) has been the difficulty faced by their innovative use of mundane sources, including climate records." (p. 1444)
- **AHR Editor:** "I am struck by the mention of Fernand Braudel several times in the course of this conversation, a historian who has, I fear, dropped off the lists of many graduate programs." (p. 1464)

## N. Isenberg, North American survey (2013) [post-2005 retrospective]

- **Challenges the 1970s creation story:** "Yet American environmental history did not emerge sui generis in the 1970s. Nor was the field spawned by the environmental movement … Rather, the field’s roots are in the nineteenth century; indeed, its earliest articulation, by the frontier historian Frederick Jackson Turner, came not long after the historical profession itself emerged" (pp. 80–81)
- **Carson and the ASEH founders:** "most of the founders of the ASEH read R. Carson’s Silent Spring" (n. 1, p. 80)
- **Webb:** "“I know in my bones”, wrote the American environmental historian Donald Worster in 1987, “that Webb was right”" (p. 84)
- **Sauer:** among "Webb’s intellectual allies … not fellow historians but geographers, anthropologists, and human ecologists … the geographer Carl Ortwin Sauer" (p. 84). But: "the work of Sauer and Speck had little influence on historians at the time" (p. 85)
- **Crosby:** the materialist pole. "Crosby’s analysis devolved into little more than ecological determinism" (p. 86)
- **Thoreau** appears only as one of the literate Americans whose ideas of land Henry Nash Smith studied (p. 86)
- **Nash:** "A yet more significant study was Roderick Nash’s Wilderness and the American Mind" (pp. 86–87)
- **The synthesizers:** "Environmental history emerged as a distinct field in the 1970s and early 1980s when a group of historians, including Wiliam Cronon, Thomas Dunlap, Carolyn Merchant, Arthur McEvoy, Richard White, and Donald Worster, synthesized the material and cultural approaches to the environment." (p. 87)
- **The 1990 split:** Worster (materialist, "the new history") against White and Cronon (cultural). McEvoy and Merchant offered "The way out of this impasse" (pp. 88–90)
- **Absent:** Braudel and the Annales are not mentioned.

## O. Massard-Guilbaud, *Áreas* (2016) [post-2005; French specialist]

- **English abstract (author's own):** "Between the two world wars, historians of the Annals School Lucien Febvre and Marc Bloch or the geographer-historian Roger Dion and others publish books who grant a large place to environment and seem, today, pioneering. After WWII, the second generation of Annals, on the other hand, if they grant a large space to the milieu, see it like something fixed, immobile. In Braudel’s work, nature has to be tamed, improved, “be drawn into modernity”. … In the same generation, Le Roy Ladurie has a more critical view, and launches a climate history — “without man” in a first time. Pleading for the writing of an environmental history, he rapidly turns, however, towards other fields of research and has no immediate disciples." (p. 15)
- **American assumptions:** "Los investigadores americanos de la Environmental History (los más numerosos e influyentes en la comunidad internacional de historiadores ambientales) y otros con frecuencia dan por asumido que los historiadores de la Escuela de los Annales fueron los pioneros" (p. 18). My gloss: American environmental historians and others often take for granted that the Annales historians were the pioneers.
- **Braudel:** "a pesar de sus cualidades, la visión del medio braudeliana no puede ser considerada pionera" (p. 20). My gloss: despite its qualities, Braudel's view of the milieu cannot be considered pioneering. Also: "el medio braudeliano, inmóvil, no hizo escuela" (p. 24). My gloss: Braudel's immobile milieu founded no school.

## Who is missing: women and non-US scholarship

- **Women.** Merchant is the only woman historian named consistently, in 9 texts. Carson appears as an inspiration, not as a historian. Susan Flader appears at founding (H). Scharff and Langston appear only in Cronon's teaching lists (E, G). McNeill (F) treats ecofeminism as a weak current. Cronon 1993 says broadening to "women, multicultural people of color, poor people, and workers" risks "white-hat-black-hat narratives" (n. 10, p. 21).
- **Non-US scholarship.** It is nearly absent from the US self-accounts (A, B, C, E, G, H). It appears in McNeill and in the 2004–05 international surveys, and the omission is itself argued over. Grove contested US-origin narratives (J p. 383); McNeill says Americanists were "among the least responsive" to internationalization (F p. 18); Sörlin and Warde note that the seminal works are mostly American (L p. 13). Guha and Grove are the two non-US figures with the strongest claims. Neither appears in the US texts. Canada is noted as neglected: "Canadianists have almost entirely ignored the genre" (F p. 18).

## Disagreements recorded in the texts

1. **When and why the field began.** Most accounts say the 1970s environmental movement (B: original p. 290; A p. 2; F p. 15). Isenberg says the roots are nineteenth-century, beginning with Turner (N pp. 80–81). Grove, via Carruthers, says colonial and imperial origins (J p. 383).
2. **Materialist versus cultural.** Worster stands against White and Cronon in 1990. McEvoy and Merchant are presented as the synthesis (N pp. 88–90; F p. 37).
3. **Nash.** He is honoured as the coiner and pioneer (J, K, L), but Cronon calls his history "whig" (A n. 27).
4. **Crosby.** He is a landmark (F) and also an ecological determinist (N p. 86; F p. 33).
5. **The Annales.** See below.

## What the evidence says about Braudel, Le Roy Ladurie and the Annales

**For influence:**

- Worster 1990, the field's charter essay, names "Fernand Braudel, Lucien Febvre e Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie" as "Uma outra fonte de idéias importante" ("another important source of ideas"; C n. 5, translation only).
- Cronon 1993: "we feel some kinship with a Braudelian vision of history" (A p. 14).
- McNeill 2003: the Annales "general approach however proved inspirational for many who would become environmental historians" (F p. 14). Also: "intellectual influences from Turner and Febvre to Malin and Le Roy Ladurie helped inspire and shape environmental history" (F p. 15).
- Sedrez 2008: "all environmental historians owe a debt of gratitude to Fernand Braudel" (M p. 1433).
- Perdue 2008: "The two foundational traditions of the Annales school and the American frontier school" (M p. 1437).

**Against treating the Annales as founders:**

- McNeill: "strangely blind to evolving environments … To a very limited extent, then, Annales historians were engaged in environmental history avant la lettre" (F p. 14). On the 1974 issue: "it did not herald anything new in Annales" (F p. 12). On France: "The masters had spoken." (F p. 29)
- Massard-Guilbaud 2004 rejects "the vision a number of non-French historians have of Braudel and other historians of the École des Annales as pioneers in the field – which they were not" (I p. 513). In 2016 she wrote that Braudel's milieu "no hizo escuela" ("founded no school"; O p. 24).
- Sörlin and Warde: "even in the seminal writings of Braudel it remains the backcloth, the stage for history, rather than an active force" (L p. 16).
- Perdue finds the American capitalism-and-frontier tradition "more useful than the Annales" for imperial expansion (M p. 1437).

**Absent:** none of the US teaching documents assign or list any Annales text: the 1994 syllabus (E), the c. 2004 reading list (G) and the ASEH canon (H).

**Reading the pattern.** The texts agree that the Annales were an influence on approach: the longue durée, geography, total history, and legitimizing non-political subjects. They do not agree that the Annales were a genealogy of environmental history proper, and the French specialist explicitly says they were not. Le Roy Ladurie has the strongest individual claim, through climate history and the 1974 "Histoire et Environnement" issue. Every account that discusses that issue says it had no follow-through.

## Judgement (my assessment, not a finding of the texts)

**Front-and-centre framework figures (historians).** The evidence most strongly supports three: **Worster** (three levels; agroecology and modes of production), **Cronon** (*Changes in the Land* as the model book; nature and narrative), and **Crosby** (Columbian Exchange and ecological imperialism). McNeill pairs exactly these three as the Americans "read almost wherever environmental history aroused interest" (F p. 19). They are also the "leading practitioners" of Sörlin and Warde (L p. 2). Worster and Crosby are named ASEH "founding members" (H). Cronon is the field's most-assigned author in his own teaching documents (E, G), which is weaker evidence because he wrote them.

**Next tier.** **Richard White** was the field's first historiographer (1985); his "reciprocal" and middle-ground models travelled, and he was the cultural pole in 1990. **Merchant** brought gender and reproduction, and *Death of Nature* is "seminal" (L), though McNeill says her theory was "not … widely imitated". On present evidence both could justify person entries; White's is the weaker case for Environment specifically, because much of his reception is as a western and Native American historian.

**Non-US framework figures.** **Grove** (green imperialism and colonial origins) and **Guha** (*Unquiet Woods*; environmentalism of the poor) are the founders the international texts name. If the atlas wants to avoid repeating the US-centrism that these very texts criticise, they belong on the Environment timeline. The evidence for them is concentrated in the non-US texts.

**Pioneers and institution-builders, roster-level or a smaller marker.** **Nash** coined the term and taught the first course; *Wilderness* is the key intellectual-history book. He is honoured but criticised, and is more a founder than a framework. **Hays** is "among the American pioneers" of political and conservation history. **Pyne** is a respected specialist who is never framed as a framework or founder in any text; roster level. **McEvoy** is co-author of the field's "most cogent" theory but had no following; roster.

**Precursors: inspiration figures who were not historians.** **Leopold** has the strongest claim of the editor's list. Worster's two programmatic essays (B, C) explicitly ground the field's idea in Leopold's "ecological interpretation of history", Cronon uses him in A, E and G, and he is in the 1980 shared canon. **Carson** is a catalyst, with good evidence (E, G, H, J, N). **Marsh** is a precursor with modest evidence: McNeill and Cronon's list, and McNeill notes "if much less so elsewhere". Also in this group: **Sauer, Glacken, Mumford and the Thomas 1956 volume**, Sauer and Glacken as precursor geographers.

**Thoreau.** No self-account I read names him as a founder, precursor or framework. He appears only as an object of cultural history. Putting him front and centre would not be supported by the field's own accounts.

**Historian precursors.** In the US: **Turner, Webb, Malin**. In France: **Febvre and Bloch** (legitimizing the subject matter), **Braudel** (time scales and longue durée, as inspiration), **Le Roy Ladurie** (climate history; 1974). In Britain: **Hoskins**.

**On the Annales question specifically.** The evidence supports showing Annales and Braudel as an influence, labelled as an influence on method and time-scale. It does not support showing them as a founding lineage. The existing Lucien Febvre entry is defensible as a precursor who legitimized new subjects (F p. 14). On this evidence it should not be the only, or the main, person anchoring the Environment family. If one Annales figure is added specifically for environment, the texts point to **Le Roy Ladurie** rather than Braudel, with the caveat that his programme "led nowhere" in France (F p. 29; O p. 21).

**Gaps that limit these judgements.**

- I could not read **White 1985**, **Crosby 1995**, **Nash 1972**, the **JAH 1990 responses** or **Worster 1990 in English**.
- Worster's Annales footnote should be checked against the English original before it is quoted in the atlas.
- White 1985 needs checking for what it says about Annales, Malin and Webb. Worster reports only that White saw frontier and western history as "formative".
