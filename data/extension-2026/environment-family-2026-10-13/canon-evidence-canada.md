# How Canadian environmental history accounts for its own origins: canon evidence

Prepared 2026-10-13 for the Environment family person-entry decision. The editor's proposed names (Thoreau, Marsh, Leopold, Carson, Worster, Cronon, Richard White, Pyne, Merchant), the Braudel/Annales question, and the existing Innis entry are tested against what Canadian (and Quebec) environmental historians say about where their field came from.

## Method and conventions

- Only texts actually read are quoted. Quotes are verbatim. Where the source was an OCR'd PDF, only OCR spacing artifacts were normalized (for example "H . A ." to "H.A."). No words were changed. Original misspellings are marked [sic].
- Page numbers come from printed folios or running heads in the PDFs. Where the pagination could not be confirmed, this is said.
- Each text is labelled by date:
  - **contemporaneous**: 2004 or earlier, written while the field was forming in Canada.
  - **early-field**: 2005–2009.
  - **retrospective**: 2012 or later. Use these only as labelled retrospective readings.
- The atlas frame is 1920–2000. Only two texts below fall inside it: Cook 1990 and Forkey 1998. The rest come from the decade in which, by these texts' own account, Canadian environmental history became a named field (c. 1998–2009), or later.

## 1. Text-by-text evidence

### C1. Cook, "Cabbages Not Kings" (1990–91), the W.L. Morton Lecture, Trent, October 1990

Organising figure: **W.L. Morton**, not any American. Cook builds on Morton's ecology and the plough, then turns to Cartier, Sagard, Champlain and Denys.

- **Morton (precursor / organising insight)**
  - p. 5: "'History is concerned with more than ecology,' he contended in 1946, 'but it is concerned with ecology too.'"
  - p. 6: "It is an insight which I would like to try to build upon."
- **Earlier Canadian historians as precursors**
  - p. 6: "this generation of historians has no more claim to the discovery of ecological history than Columbus had to the discovery of America."
  - p. 6: "In a broad sense the writing of Canadian history has always been concerned with the environment."
- **Innis and Lower (precursors)**
  - p. 6: "Certainly not Harold Innis for whom the fur trade, the cod fisheries and other staple products were the real stuff of history. Or even A. R. M. Lower whose work on the lumbering industry was central even to such a convinced nationalist."
  - Note 4, attached to this paragraph, cites Richard White, "American Environmental History," *PHR* 1985.
- **Innis, Lower and Creighton (precursors, with a difference)**
  - p. 6: "Innis, Lower and Creighton also struggled with the question of the impact of the North American environment on European women and men, their institutions and traditions."
  - pp. 6–7: "for this earlier generation of historians the exploitation of the environment was accepted as natural and necessary, even though excesses might be deplored."
  - p. 7: "More recent environmental historians have a different focus. They are no longer much concerned with explaining the impact of the environment on man; it is the impact of man on nature that is at the centre of their work."
- **Turner and Webb (US precursors)**
  - p. 6: "F.J. Turner and Walter Prescott Webb remained important, if declining, influences into the 1950s."
- **Cronon (exemplar of the reciprocal approach)**
  - p. 7: "Works like William Cronon's Changes in the Land - which often appeals to Canadian sources to explain ecological change in early New England - and Bruce Hodgins and Jamie Benidickson's The Temagami Experience analyze this interaction."
  - Note 22 cites Cronon's *Changes in the Land*, p. 12, for "two human ways of living, two ways of belonging to an ecosystem" (p. 9). It also cites White, "Native Americans and the Environment" (1989).
- **Crosby (analytical framework for contact)**
  - p. 13: "As Alfred W. Crosbie [sic], Jr., demonstrated so brilliantly nearly two decades ago, in his Columbian Exchange The Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492, contact was even more significantly the interaction of different ecologies or environments."
- **Thesis**
  - p. 15: "Cabbages, at least as much as kings, led to the transformation of the new world."
- **Others cited:** Glacken, *Traces on the Rhodian Shore* (n. 37).
- **Absent:** Thoreau, Marsh, Leopold, Carson, Worster, Pyne, Merchant, Hays, Nash, Braudel, Annales.

### C2. Forkey, "Damning the Dam" (CHR 1998)

The clearest pre-2000 statement in a major Canadian journal that the staples historians are **not** the field's prototypes.

- **Cronon and White (define the field)**
  - p. 70: "As one of the field's most able practitioners notes, the historiography of environmental history is distinguished by three types of studies".
  - Note 5 is to Cronon, "Modes of Prophecy and Production," *JAH* 1990, and to White, *PHR* 1985.
- **Innis, Creighton and Lower (rejected as prototypes)**
  - p. 70: "Canadian historians have been slow to enter this new field. Although the environment was an ingredient in the work of Harold A. Innis, Donald Creighton, and Arthur R.M. Lower, their studies do not serve as prototypes for Canadian environmental history."
  - p. 70: "In their approach, the environment exists only as a scaffolding on which human history unfolds, and not as an actor in the process."
  - p. 71: "the staples model masks the essential role of social relationships and the dynamic between humans and nature."
- **Andrew H. Clark (preferred antecedent)**
  - p. 71: "More appropriate antecedents for Canadian environmental history may be found in the work of historical geographer Andrew H. Clark, who better demonstrated the interplay between people and their environment."
- **Gaffield and Gaffield, *Consuming Canada* (1995)**
  - n. 6: the volume "addresses the field in Canada, though unfortunately with limited success".

### C3. Loo, "Making a Modern Wilderness" (CHR Forum, 2001)

- **McKibben (argued against)**
  - p. 93: "Emotionally I'm with McKibben, convinced, like many of his readers, by his eloquent passion. But from my perspective as a historian ... I'm not so sure he's got it exactly right."
  - The ellipsis omits a parenthetical.
- **Janet Foster (Canadian precedent, used to mark a difference)**
  - p. 95: "As she points out, there was no Canadian equivalent to the American crusade for wildlife in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Canada had no Muirs, Audubons, or Roosevelts, no high-profile visionaries around whom a movement could coalesce."
- **Canadian difference: provinces**
  - p. 95: "Wildlife conservation was the work of the provinces, not the least because they had jurisdiction over natural resource management."
- **Leopold (historical actor, not historiographical influence)**
  - p. 104: "American hunter and forester Aldo Leopold was at the forefront of these efforts. His book Game Management (1933) brought together the work of decades".
  - p. 104: "In many ways, Leopold's environmentalism embodied the culture of modernity".
- **Others:** James C. Scott, *Weapons of the Weak*, is cited for rural resistance (n. 80).
- **Absent:** Innis, Cronon, Worster.

### C4. Wynn, "Thinking About Mountains, Valleys and Solitudes" (Acadiensis 2001)

- **Innis (genealogy of regional historical geography and economic history)**
  - p. 142: "each of these books engages in one way or another with Harold Innis's staples view of economic development (though this is not the only line of influence in their intellectual genealogies)".
- **Leopold (an ethic and a call, not a historiographical model)**
  - p. 144: "Decades have passed since the fierce fire fading in the eye of a dying wolf brought Aldo Leopold to frame a 'land ethic' ... Yet interest in such an ecological perspective on human affairs has been slow to take root."
  - p. 145: historical geographers should "respond to Leopold's call to recognize that 'many historical events, hitherto explained solely in terms of human enterprise, were actually biotic interactions between people and land'".
- **Canada versus US**
  - p. 145: "In the United States and elsewhere the vigorous new field of environmental history has grown from this conjuncture. In Canada, by contrast, the response has been faltering. Perhaps this owes something to the relative strength of historical geography in Canada in comparison with the United States".

### C5. MacEachern, "Voices Crying in the Wilderness" (Acadiensis 2002)

A short, quotable origin narrative written while the field was forming.

- **Innis, Lower, Creighton, Morton (foundational, then abandoned)**
  - p. 215: "In a sense, of course, Canadian history has long been associated with nature. Think Innis on beaver and cod; Lower on trees; Creighton on rivers; Morton on the frontier generally. These foundational historians recognized that nature was a constant contributing factor in what drew Europeans (or, for that matter, Paleoindians) to Canada, what determined their survival, what defined their settlement and what eventually shaped the national identity."
  - p. 215: "But by the 1960s, historians were treating nature as they had long treated Natives – as an opening chapter of the national narrative, worthy of an introductory mention but not to be spoken of again. We were content to pass off any matters of nature and the past to historical geography".
- **Canadian pioneers before the field was named**
  - p. 215: "John Wadland, Bruce Hodgins, H.V. Nelles and Janet Foster spring to mind – they were voices crying in the wilderness."
- **The US front rank**
  - pp. 215–216: "Environmental history prospered in the United States with works by Roderick Nash, William Cronon, Donald Worster, Carolyn Merchant, Richard White and Stephen Pyne in the front rank."
  - n. 4 gives a reading list, one book each:
    - Nash, *Wilderness*
    - Cronon, *Changes in the Land* and *Nature's Metropolis*
    - Worster, *Dust Bowl* and *Rivers of Empire*
    - Merchant, *Ecological Revolutions*
    - Pyne, *Fire in America*
    - "Richard White, The Organic Machine: The Remaking of the Columbia River (New York, 1995)". This is White's environmental work, not *The Middle Ground*.
- **Worster (definitional model)**
  - p. 215: the field's "core values ... take as its purview any or all of three realms: nature itself as it has existed through time, humans' uses of nature and humans' ideas about nature."
  - n. 3 is to Worster, "Doing Environmental History."
- **Non-American forebears (unnamed; the closest this anglophone literature comes to the Annales question)**
  - p. 216: "In the 1990s, international scholars grumbled about American hegemony in the field, and about the presumption of regarding a field with so many French and British forebears as of American origin. But the term 'environmental history' has won out internationally over earlier European names such as 'ecological history' and 'green history', showing how real the American influence has been."
- **How Canada took the field up**
  - p. 216: "By the early 1990s, more Canadianists were taking a renewed interest in the role of nature in Canada's past. This was in part due to the rise of environmental history in the United States. ... But many Canadianists knew little or nothing of the new field, and were drawn to environmental topics through the environmental movement of the era, or simply from a realization that there was good history to be written."
  - p. 216: "Canadians would best avoid a slavish adherence to an American model."
- **Innis and Lower as the reference point the field defines itself against**
  - p. 216: "Environmental history is distinct from earlier work by historians like Innis and Lower not because it promotes the study of nature over that of humans – it does not – but because it takes occasion to focus on nature itself".
- **Leopold (influence on Canadians explicitly doubted)**
  - p. 219, on Warecki: "Yet there is no evidence here, nor the next four times the ecologist is mentioned, that Leopold was being read by Canadians, let alone influencing them."
- **Nash (an over-used American foil)**
  - p. 220: "Warecki over-values classic American historiography and feels obliged to confront it. He cites such well-worn works as Roderick Nash, Wilderness and the American Mind".
- **Canadian difference**
  - p. 220: "since Crown lands are a provincial responsibility in Canada, environmentalism here has been more provincially directed, making it less visible to a national audience and less obvious in a national narrative".

### C6. Wynn, review of Forkey (Scientia Canadensis 2003)

- **Innis and the staples approach (proto-environmental claim rejected)**
  - p. 96: "Forkey is right to puncture (as he does, gently, in his first chapter) tired and oft-repeated claims that Canadian history, the story of settlers transforming a new land, is 'necessarily' environmental, and that the staples approach represents an indigenous form of proto-environmental history."
  - This is Wynn's report of Forkey 2003, pp. 6–13 (not read).
- **Cronon (model)**
  - p. 95: Forkey's first chapter "draws inspiration from William Cronon's 1983 study of New England, entitled Changes in the Land".
- **Canada versus US**
  - p. 96: "The field is undeveloped in this country, compared with the United States".

### C7. Bocking, essay review (Scientia Canadensis 2003)

Minor for origins. Bocking defines the field's essence (pp. 85–86) as accounts of "nature itself as a significant historical actor, shaping the choices available to humans: such a combination is the essence of environmental history."

### C8. Wynn, "'Shall We Linger Along Ambitionless?'" (BC Studies 2004)

The fullest pre-2005 Canadian account of the field's origins and of why Canada lagged.

- **Mackintosh and Innis (most important interwar influences on BC scholarship)**
  - p. 15: "W.A. Mackintosh and H.A. Innis, who developed a distinctively Canadian perspective on colonial economic history, were probably the most important influences on BC scholarship during these years. Their approach, commonly known as the staples thesis, or staples theory, emphasized the importance of resource-based industries to the development of new world societies."
  - p. 15: Innis in 1932 thought BC's development "a story of 'struggle against mountains, rocks and forest.'"
- **Lower (*North American Assault*)**
  - p. 16: Carrothers's forestry chapter "was included in A.R.M. Lower's history entitled The North American Assault on the Canadian Forest".
- **US framing**
  - pp. 31–32 present environmental history as American: "By most accounts the environmental movement was catalytic in the emergence, in the United States, of a new field of scholarly inquiry known as environmental history."
  - Notes on this page cite:
    - n. 60: "In 1972 Roderick Nash attempted to give pedagogic shape to the field"
    - n. 61: Nash, *Wilderness*; Hays, *Conservation and the Gospel of Efficiency*
    - n. 62: White's *PHR* 1985 essay "is a superb historiography essay tracing the early development of the field"
    - n. 63: biographies "from Thoreau and Muir to Leopold and George Perkins Marsh"
    - n. 64: Nash and Worster, *Nature's Economy*
- **Hays (substantive framework for environmentalism)**
  - pp. 28–29: "For Samuel Hays, perhaps the first to provide a book-length account of the development of the environmental movement in the United States, postwar environmentalism stood in sharp contrast to earlier conservationist thought."
- **Thoreau (via Schrepfer)**
  - p. 30: environmentalists "turned away from an essentially mystical search for salvation in nature (exemplified by Thoreau and, especially, John Muir)".
- **Canada lagged**
  - p. 32: "Measured against American scholarship in this field, Canadian environmental history is underdeveloped."
  - p. 33: "History, geography, and political economy have each, in different ways, deprived the field of opportunities for growth in Canada."
- **The "always environmental" claim rebutted**
  - p. 33: "At one level, Canadians watching the oft-remarked expansion of environmental history in the United States seemed to wonder what all the fuss was about. After all, they observed, 'the writing of Canadian history has always been concerned with the environment.'"
  - This is quoted from Cook (C1), n. 68.
  - p. 33: "Had not Arthur Lower ... recognized that the North American past was 'largely the story of man's struggle with nature'? Indeed, but this missed much of the point."
- **Innis (staples ≠ environmental history)**
  - p. 33: "Although nature had a place in histories that adopted the staples approach - Innis began his books on the fishery and the fur trade with extended discussions of the cod and the beaver - these works focused on human uses of the environment."
  - p. 33: "these staple products, along with minerals and even the land itself, remained commodities for human exploitation. ... there was little concern in this work for the reciprocity and mutuality of relations between people and nature that proponents of environmental history regard as central to their enterprise."
- **Historical geography filled the niche; Innis and Clark**
  - p. 34: "In Canada, by contrast, historical geographers seemed to fill the niche that environmentally minded historians to the south colonized so successfully."
  - p. 34: "The dedication in the first volume of the Historical Atlas of Canada rightly traced the main lines of the field's intellectual pedigree at that juncture back to Harold Innis and the Canadian-American historical geographer Andrew Clark, for whom the environment was essentially an inert and rather featureless stage".
- **Crosby (framework for introduced species)**
  - p. 41: "In North America, attention has focused particularly on Crosby's claims that introduced diseases were 'the shock troops' of imperial conquest".
- **Richard White (his environmental monograph as a model)**
  - p. 43 n.: "Richard White's splendid study of Island County, Washington, also comes to mind as an example of the kind of work that might be done at an 'island scale' in British Columbia".
  - The note cites *Land Use, Environment and Social Change* (1980).
- **The "Innisian tradition" in political economy (less environmental than Innis)**
  - p. 44 n. 92: "A significant strand of this work seeks to connect itself to the 'Innisian tradition,' although it is perhaps more interested in capital than the staple and is even less environmental in its emphases than is the original work of Innis and others."
- **Cronon (*Nature's Metropolis* as a model)**
  - p. 45: Mackie's *Island Timber* is "(reminiscent of William Cronon's treatment, in Nature's Metropolis, of the way in which the forests of Wisconsin were consumed by the voracious mills of Chicago)".
  - OCR read "Natures Metropolis"; the apostrophe was restored.
- **Cronon ("Trouble with Wilderness"), Loo and James C. Scott**
  - pp. 56–57 n. 120 cite "The Trouble with Wilderness".
  - p. 38: Loo's article "echoes anthropologist James C. Scott's anxiety".

### E1. Campbell, *Shaped by the West Wind* (2005), Introduction

- **Nash, Worster, Cronon, Pyne (models)**
  - pp. 8–9: "Many of the best-known works in the field are in fact histories of environmental thought. Roderick Nash's Wilderness and the American Mind (1967), Donald Worster's Nature's Economy ... and Max Oelschlaeger's The Idea of Wilderness (1991)".
  - p. 9: "as in William Cronon's Nature's Metropolis".
  - p. 9: "Stephen Pyne focused on the creation of meanings for a single physical phenomenon in How the Canyon Became Grand (1998)".
- **Turner, Innis, Creighton (old national framings superseded)**
  - p. 10: "For a century, North American historians wrote from the outside looking in, envisaging landscape in grand, national, almost abstract terms. Frederick Jackson Turner, for example, pronounced the American frontier as the formative influence".
  - p. 10: "for Harold Innis, it was the technologies used to penetrate the continent and to export natural resources or 'staples'; for Donald Creighton, it was the dynamics of a commercial empire based in the St. Lawrence valley."
  - pp. 10–11: Morton's critique and J.M.S. Careless's "limited identities" dissolved the national framework.
- **Clark and Wynn (historical geography)**
  - p. 14: "Much of the environmental history in Canada has come from historical geographers. This is particularly evident in Atlantic Canada, and the work of scholars from Andrew Hill Clark to Graeme Wynn."

### E2. Castonguay, "Faire du Québec un objet de l'histoire environnementale" (Globe 2006)

Proof pages •n•. English-version folios are given as "Eng. p."

- **Quebec historians do not claim the label**
  - •3•: "les historiens qui ont fait de la société québécoise leur principal objet d'études ne se réclament pas explicitement de l'histoire environnementale."
- **Cronon, Merchant, Worster (a "debate founding the field")**
  - •2• n. 2: "voir un débat fondateur du champ : William CRONON, « Modes of Prophecy and Production ... » ; Carolyn MERCHANT, « Gender and Environmental History » ... ; Donald WORSTER, « Transformation of the Earth ... »" (all *JAH* 1990).
- **European proto-history; Le Roy Ladurie (nearest Annales-adjacent reference)**
  - •4• n. 8: "nous pouvons apprécier la diversité des approches et des thèmes d'une proto-histoire environnementale européenne. Voir Emmanuel LEROY LADURIE, Histoire du climat depuis l'an mil".
  - Also listed: Deléage, Pitte, Delort, Corvol, de Planhol, Guillerme, Corbin. **Neither Braudel nor Febvre is named.**
- **Staples (distant origin, but no interaction)**
  - Eng. p. 27: "The authors of recent reviews see the distant origins of Canadian environmental history in the geographic considerations of the first professional Canadian historians, even though they are careful to point out the shortcomings."
  - The reviews cited are Forkey 2003, pp. 6–13 and Evenden 2004, pp. 10–11.
  - •6•: "Cette approche ne fait en somme aucune place aux interactions environnement-société."
- **Cronon (*Nature's Metropolis* parallels Quebec historical geography)**
  - •8• n. 17: "Ceux familiers avec la littérature en histoire environnementale verront une parenté étroite avec une œuvre phare du champ : William CRONON, Nature's Metropolis."
  - The parallel is to the Courville/Robert/Séguin Laurentian project.
- **Quebec scholars refuse the field's novelty**

## 3. Quebec scholarship (summary)

- The Quebec retrospectives (E2, E3, E4, R5) say francophone historians largely **did not adopt the label** until the 2000s. Their precursor lines run through:
  - historical geography: Courville, Robert, Séguin, the *Atlas historique du Québec*, Hatvany;
  - social and economic history of resources: Hardy and Séguin, *Forêt et société en Mauricie* (1984);
  - early declared environmental historians working with Hays's conservation thesis: Gillis and Roach; Girard 1990.
- **French and Annales influences appear only here**, and weakly:
  - Le Roy Ladurie as European "proto-histoire environnementale" (E2) and as climate history (E3, E9, R5);
  - Febvre's *La terre et l'évolution humaine* as a personal formation (E4, by a self-declared non-environmental historian);
  - Braudel for capitalism and world-economy (E4), and as the lineage of seigneurial studies (R5 p. 164);
  - Furst's own caution that the Annales filiation is "souvent affirmée a posteriori" (R5 p. 157).
- Cronon's *Nature's Metropolis* is read in Quebec as kin to the Laurentian historical-geography project (E2 n. 17).

## 5. Judgement

This section is the analyst's interpretation of the evidence above, not a finding of the sources.

**Front and centre framework figures, supported by the Canadian evidence:**

1. **William Cronon.** He is the most-cited outside scholar (16 of 26 text entries) across all three periods, in English and French. Canadians use him for:
   - the definition of the field (the *Changes in the Land* dialectic; "Modes of Prophecy");
   - exemplary method (*Changes in the Land*, *Nature's Metropolis*);
   - the critique of wilderness that Canadians use to separate themselves from US wilderness history (Loo, Castonguay).

   He is also the documented bridge back to Innis: *Nature's Metropolis* is read as staples and metropolitan-hinterland history (R2, R8). R7 reports Cronon himself saying so, but that is a blog report of an oral remark.
2. **Donald Worster.** His "three levels" or "three realms" model is the working definition in MacEachern 2002 and 2009, Devor Hall 2021 and Furst 2020. Castonguay cites the 1990 *JAH* roundtable as the field's "débat fondateur". He wrote one of the two framing essays of the main Canadian teaching reader (E7).
3. **Alfred Crosby** is a strong third for the framework role. He is used analytically for contact and biological exchange in the founding Canadian essay (Cook 1990) and after it (Wynn 2004, MacEachern 2009).

**Canadian precursor that belongs front and centre for an atlas teaching Canada: Harold Innis.** This is well evidenced, but with a precise shape.

- Innis is the figure that Canadian environmental history's **origin story is told around and against**. He is named in 18 texts.
- Repeatedly, and from the start, he is denied prototype or "proto-environmental" status, by Forkey 1998, Wynn 2003 and 2004, Castonguay 2006, MacDowell 2012, and Coates and Wynn 2019.
- He is also claimed as Canada's distinctive legacy and international contribution, by Loo 2014, Macfarlane 2024 and CHESS 2024.

The defensible edge is therefore Innis to Environment as **"precursor (contested): staples as resource/commodity history, which environmental history defined itself against and later re-absorbed."** It should not be "founder."

**Precursors at roster level:**

- **Lower** (forest; *North American Assault*; often paired with Innis).
- **Creighton** (Laurentian thesis).
- **W.L. Morton**, the organising figure of Cook 1990, also in Wynn's pedigree.
- **Andrew H. Clark** and Canadian historical geography. The texts repeatedly name these as the *real* institutional antecedent; Forkey calls Clark "more appropriate antecedents". This is not on the editor's list but is better supported than several names that are.
- **George Perkins Marsh** is a credible "earlier roots" precursor in Canadian accounts, but almost entirely through Wynn's personal route and MacEachern's entry point. Wynn himself flags that Marsh's reputation was "substantially an American one."

**Roster-level contributors, not front and centre:**

- **Richard White.** In Canada he functions mainly as the **historiographer of the field** (the 1985 *PHR* survey, cited in 5 texts). His environmental monographs are barely used: *Organic Machine* appears once in a reading list; *Land Use ... Island County* once as a model. *Roots of Dependency* was not found. *The Middle Ground* enters Canadian environmental-history writing once, in 2020, and not as environmental history. The heavy Canadian citation of *The Middle Ground* belongs to fur-trade and Indigenous history, not to this family.
- **Stephen Pyne** (fire; he also wrote on Quebec fire).
- **Roderick Nash** (an origin text in retrospect, but used in Canada partly as a foil).
- **Samuel Hays** (conservation thesis, worked through by Canadian forest-conservation historians).
- **Carolyn Merchant** (thin).

**Not supported as framework figures in Canadian historiography:**

- **Thoreau, Leopold, Carson.** They appear as historical actors or as the environmentalist context of the 1960s and 1970s, not as models for how Canadian environmental history is written. MacEachern 2002 explicitly faults a Canadian author for assuming Leopold's influence. If included at all, they belong as context or "actor" nodes outside the person-entry canon.

**Braudel and the Annales:**

- There is no support in the anglophone Canadian origin narratives.
- There is modest, mostly retrospective support in Quebec. Furst 2020 calls the Annales filiation "souvent affirmée a posteriori" and puts seigneurial studies (Dechêne et al.) "dans la lignée de l'approche développée par Fernand Braudel". Robert 2006 credits Febvre's *La terre et l'évolution humaine* as a formation.
- This gives the existing **Febvre** entry a small, Quebec-specific link to the Environment family, through Robert 2006 (one text, explicitly from outside the field).
- A Braudel to Environment edge would need a "claimed lineage (retrospective, Quebec)" label. It should not be front and centre.

**Canadian authors of the field, for roster or person-entry discussion:**

- **Graeme Wynn** (the field's principal narrator and organiser; series editor).
- **Ramsay Cook** (the 1990 essay that, per MacDowell, "sparked" the field).
- **Tina Loo** (state, high modernism, critique of wilderness).
- **Stéphane Castonguay** (Quebec).
- **Alan MacEachern** (field-builder; NiCHE).
- **Janet Foster** (1978, framed against Nash).
- **Neil Forkey** (the earliest CHR statement against the staples prototype).

## 6. Recommended follow-ups

- Read Evenden and Wynn, "54:40 or Fight" (2009), especially pp. 215–46 and pp. 217–19. Every later Canadian ordering essay cites it, so it should settle the Innis and historical-geography questions in the field's own canonical form.
- Read Wynn, *Canada and Arctic North America* (2007), introduction; Evenden, *Fish versus Power* (2004), pp. 10–11; and Forkey, *Shaping the Upper Canadian Frontier* (2003), pp. 6–13.
- Read Gaffield and Gaffield, *Consuming Canada* (1995), introduction. It is the only pre-2000 Canadian reader, and it would add a second in-frame (1920–2000) source alongside Cook 1990 and Forkey 1998.
- Read Loo, *States of Nature*, endnotes to the introduction, to check whether Innis or the staples literature is cited behind the p. 1 passage.
