## 1. Component 1: the Dobb–Sweezy exchange, *Science & Society* 14:2 (Spring 1950): 134–167

**Read status: read in full**, from a JSTOR-licensed scan ("JSTOR Michigan; licensed version"), stable URL https://www.jstor.org/stable/40400000. The copy is hosted at https://classes.matthewjbrown.net/teaching-files/marx/dobb-sweezy-debate.pdf (2.95 MB); it is the atlas's existing `dobb_sweezy_transition` URL. The embedded text layer is clipped at the right margin, so all 34 pages were OCR'd locally; printed pages 134–167 were confirmed from the running heads. This extends the atlas's earlier reading (Sweezy pp. 134–137; Dobb pp. 157–167) to Sweezy's whole article.

### 1a. Sweezy, "A Critique" (pp. 134–157), section by section

- **Occasion** (p. 134): "We live in the period of transition from capitalism to socialism; and this fact lends particular interest to studies of earlier transitions from one social system to another." Sweezy confines himself to the third of Dobb's *Studies* "devoted to the decline of feudalism and the rise of capitalism".
- **Definition** (pp. 134–135): Dobb's feudalism is "virtually identical with what we usually mean by serfdom" (Sweezy quoting *Studies*, p. 35). Sweezy calls this definition "defective in not identifying a system of production", since serfdom has been "associated with different forms of economic organization".
- **Western European feudalism as "production for use"** (p. 136): "The crucial feature of feudalism in this sense is that it is a system of production for use … There is, in other words, none of the pressure which exists under capitalism for continual improvements in methods of production." Feudal warfare "has no tendency to transform it"; surplus population "exercises no creative or revolutionizing influence" (pp. 136–137). Conclusion: "a system with a very strong bias in favor of maintaining given methods and relations of production" (p. 137).
- **The core charge** (p. 141): "Dobb's theory of the internal causation of the breakdown of feudalism could still be rescued if it could be shown that the rise of the towns was a process internal to the feudal system … Since trade can in no sense be regarded as a form of feudal economy, it follows that Dobb could hardly argue that the rise of urban life was a consequence of internal feudal causes … having neglected to analyse the laws and tendencies of western European feudalism, he mistakes for immanent trends certain historical developments which in fact can only be explained as arising from causes external to the system."
- **Trade as "root cause", Pirenne** (p. 142):
  - "It seems to me that Dobb has not succeeded in shaking that part of the commonly accepted theory which holds that the root cause of the decline of feudalism was the growth of trade."
  - The conflict is "between production for the market and production for use".
  - Pirenne's theory of reopened Mediterranean and northern trade routes is "quite convincing. But clearly one does not have to accept Pirenne's theory in order to agree that the growth of trade was the decisive factor" (n. 10).
  - **Credit to Dobb:** "no theory which fails to take into account the factors which Dobb stresses—especially the growing extravagance of the ruling class and the flight of the serfs from the land—can be regarded as correct … the following notes and suggestions owe much to Dobb even where they depart from his views".
- **The second serfdom** (pp. 141, 147): "Near the centers of trade, the effect on feudal economy is strongly disintegrating; further away the effect tends to be just the opposite" (p. 141, n. 9). The decline of western feudalism was "due to the inability of the ruling class to maintain control over, and hence to overexploit, society's labor power" (p. 147).
- **The intervening period** (pp. 147–150): feudalism in western Europe "was already moribund, if not actually dead, before capitalism was born … I shall simply call the system which prevailed in western Europe during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries 'pre-capitalist commodity production'" (p. 150).
- **The rise of capitalism** (pp. 153–157). Sweezy reads Marx's "really revolutionary way" (Capital III) against Dobb's emphasis on small producers rising from the ranks (pp. 153–155). He closes by regretting "that Dobb's reiterated statements about the importance of the realization phase may serve to divert the attention of some readers from his excellent treatment of the essential problems of the period of original accumulation" (p. 157).

### 1b. Dobb, "Reply" (pp. 157–167): what it adds beyond the atlas's existing note

- **Tone** (p. 157): "I personally welcome his contribution … as a distinguished challenge … With a good deal of what he says I feel no disagreement … But in one or two places a more fundamental difference over method and analysis seems to emerge".
- **Relations of production versus exchange** (p. 158). Sweezy's "system of production" seems to include "the relations between the producer and his market … these relations of exchange (by contrast with relations of production) are the focus of attention in Sweezy's interpretation … If this is so, then I think we have a fundamental issue between us. The definition which I was using in my Studies was advisedly in terms of the relations of production characteristic of feudalism: namely, the relations between the direct producer and his overlord."
- **Against "western European feudalism" as a genus** (p. 159): "the desire to represent 'western European feudalism' as a distinctive genus and to endow it alone with the title of 'feudal' is a product of bourgeois historians".
- **Internal contradictions** (p. 159): to deny feudalism any internal tendency to change "would be, surely, to make it an exception to the general Marxist law of development that economic society is moved by its own internal contradictions".
- **Interaction, not either/or** (p. 160): "He seems to see it as a question of either internal conflict or external forces. This strikes me as much too simplified, even mechanical, a presentation. I see it as an interaction of the two; although with primary emphasis, it is true, upon the internal contradictions … What I am asserting is that trade exercised its influence to the extent that it accentuated the internal conflicts within the old mode of production."
- **Evidence against the trade correlation** (p. 161): labour services disappeared earliest "in the backward north and west of England" and survived longest "in the more advanced south-east"; in eastern Europe "the correlation was, not between nearness to markets and feudal disintegration … but between nearness to markets and strengthening of serfdom (cf. my Studies, p. 38-42)". **This is the passage Brenner's 1976 argument generalises** (cf. Brenner 1976, p. 53; `evidence-brenner.md` §1). The inference is mine, not a reader's.
- **The English Revolution and the absolutist state** (p. 163). He rejects the view that capitalism was established before the sixteenth century, because then the seventeenth-century civil war "cannot … have been the bourgeois revolution". He holds "that the ruling class was still feudal and that the state was still the political instrument of its rule". He notes that this "has occupied a good deal of discussion among Marxist historians in England in the last few years".
- **Small producers and the revolution** (p. 165). Rising gentry and "kulaks" "were a most important driving force in the bourgeois revolution of the seventeenth century, providing in particular the sinews of Cromwell's New Model Army". Merchant capital, "far from always playing a progressive role, was often to be found allied with feudal reaction". **This is the position Brenner's *Merchants and Revolution* (1993) later revisits** (`evidence-brenner.md` §7a; the book itself is unread).
- **Signed** (p. 167): "MAURICE DOBB, Trinity College, Cambridge, England".

## 3. Component 2: the Brenner debate and "political Marxism", deepened

The Brenner debate itself (1976–85) is documented in `evidence-brenner.md` §§1–5, re-sourced under the copyright rule. This section adds the history of the label and of the school, and the critiques. All texts below are open and were read on 2026-10-18.

### 3a. The label: from Bois's charge (1978) to a self-description

1. **Bois (1978), the coinage, mediated.**
   - Wikipedia, "Political Marxism", n. 3, quotes the 1985 reprint pp. 115–16: "Brenner's Marxism is 'political Marxism'—in reaction to the wave of economistic tendencies in contemporary historiography … It amounts to a voluntarist vision of history in which the class struggle is divorced from all other objective contingencies and, in the first place, from such laws of development as may be peculiar to a specific mode of production."
   - Callinicos (1990, below) quotes the second sentence in almost the same words.
   - **Two independent open witnesses agree.**
2. **Wood's adoption, as reported by a critic (1990).** Alex Callinicos, "The Limits of 'Political Marxism'", NLR I/184 (Nov.–Dec. 1990), pp. 110–115. The body text is open on the NLR site with page markers; the footnotes are behind the paywall.
   - "This is by no means the first time that Wood has used Brenner's work to distinguish her alternative reading of historical materialism from Cohen's. Indeed, at one point she adopted for this reading the label given to Brenner's work by one of his Marxist critics, Guy Bois, namely 'political Marxism'" (p. 111).
   - "Wood rejects the charge of voluntarism, but takes Marx himself to say that 'capitalism is unique in its drive to revolutionize the productive forces, while other modes of production have tended to conserve existing forces'" (p. 111, citing Wood p. 70 n. 47).
   - The Wood text where she adopted the label is cited in his n. 3–4, which are not visible.
3. **Wood's adoption, as reported by Brenner (2016).** Robert Brenner, "Ellen Meiksins Wood (1942–2016)", *Against the Current* 181 (March–April 2016), abridged from a talk at a Verso conference (Marxists Internet Archive; read in full).
   - "Bois crafted the term 'political Marxism' to criticize my own work and that of co-thinkers like Ellen in the name of his own version of economic determinism."
   - "Ellen responded by accepting Bois's challenge. If Bois wants to characterize our approach as 'political Marxism,' she said, that's his prerogative. What is actually at stake is recognizing the systematically different ways in which 'the economic' and 'the political' interconnect in pre-capitalist and capitalist societies respectively".
   - **Brenner's paraphrase of Wood, not her words.** He places her response in connection with "The Separation of the Economic and Political in Capitalism" (NLR, May–June 1981).
4. **A reader on the 1981 essay.** Ken Hirschkop, "A political Marxist: Ellen Meiksins Wood, 1942–2016", *Radical Philosophy* 197 (May/June 2016), pp. 67–69 (read in full): the NLR article on the separation of the economic from the political contained "Ellen's robust defence of 'political Marxism'".
5. **What I could read of the 1981 essay.** The Verso repost (25 February 2016, which links to NLR for the rest) gives the opening sections and notes. The part read contains **no** occurrence of "Bois" or "political Marxism". Its acknowledgement thanks "Perry Anderson, Robin Blackburn, Robert Brenner, Ralph Miliband, Neal Wood … and my students at York University, Toronto, especially Frances Abele and George Comninel" (n. *). It cites Brenner's NLR essay "for a discussion of how capitalist relations of production uniquely demand the revolutionary transformation of productive forces" (n. 14; the repost misprints the issue number as "304").
6. **Conclusion on "self-description".** That Wood accepted "political Marxism" for her own position is attested by three readers: Callinicos (1990), Brenner (2016) and Hirschkop (2016). The passage where she does so was **not read in a primary text**. The candidates are the full 1981 NLR essay and the 1995 reprint in *Democracy Against Capitalism*, whose Cambridge Core openings (introduction, chs. 1, 2 and 4) were read and do not contain the phrase. The entry should say "accepted the label, by the accounts of Callinicos (1990) and Brenner (2016)", not quote her.
7. **Later usage.** Verso's 2016 headnotes (read) say Wood, "alongside Robert Brenner, inaugurated the 'Political Marxist' approach to history". marx200.org (read) says the approach was "strongly shaped by the political scientist George Comninel and the historian Ellen Meiksins Wood". Harman (2004) names "Ellen Meiksins Wood, George Comninel, Benno Teschke" as those who "carried Brenner's argument to the extreme".

### 3b. Wood's own account of the lineage (primary, open)

Ellen Meiksins Wood, "Eurocentric Anti-Eurocentrism", *Against the Current* 92 (May–June 2001) (Marxists Internet Archive; read in full; no page numbers, so cited by section).
- **Marx.** In "the so-called primitive accumulation" Marx "did decisively break with the old paradigm … a transformation of social property relations—the expropriation of direct producers, specifically in England—was the real 'primitive accumulation.'"
- **Component 1, the transition debate.** "Marx's insights were elaborated by later Marxist historians, especially in the famous Transition Debate which began in 1950 in Science and Society. Here, the main issue was whether the transition from feudalism to capitalism was brought about by external factors—in particular, the growth of trade (as in the 'commercialization model')—or by internal factors, a development in social property relations. In that debate, historians such as Maurice Dobb and R.H. Hilton challenged the commercialization model."
- **Her criticism of that debate.** "This was an important challenge to the commercialization model of capitalist development, but it still shared significant assumptions with that old model … they were still assuming too much of what needed to be explained."
- **Component 2, Brenner.** "Robert Brenner built on the foundation created by these Marxist historians and especially their emphasis on the class relations between lords and peasants. But he clearly felt that his predecessors were still conceding too much to the old model … he set out to explain the emergence of a new and historically unprecedented social form." His explanation "had to do with the emergence of what he calls 'market dependence'".
- **Industrialisation (component 3 link).** "Like other Marxist historians such as E.P. Thompson, Brenner understands industrialization … [as] the end product of those specific economic imperatives which resulted from very distinctive social property relations. The so-called Industrial Revolution was the outcome of an economy already structured by capitalist social property relations".
- **Divergence (component 4 link).** "We need, for instance, to explain the dynamics of highly commercialized societies that did not become capitalist … China, for instance, was for a long time far ahead of Europe in general, not least in technology".
- **Targets.** J. M. Blaut's "protocapitalism"; Frank's "Asian-dominated world".
- **This is the clearest open statement, by the school's leading exponent, that components 1–2 form one line, and that the line bears on 3 and 4.**

### 3c. Critiques of political Marxism (open texts)

1. **Seccombe (1983)**, NLR I/137, free opening: Brenner "displays a tendency to deny the explanatory power of demographic phenomena in asserting the primacy of class struggle dynamics"; Bois's criticism "is, in my view, very much to the point. It could be extended far beyond Brenner's work to the entire legacy of classical Marxism". A Marxist-feminist demographic critique.
2. **Harman (1989)**, *International Socialism* 2:45, read in part: Brenner "take[s] up Dobb's argument, but simplif[ies] it", "a very narrow view of class struggle". Comninel and Rigby use Brenner "to attack any notion that the development of the forces of production explain the development of the relations of production".
3. **Callinicos (1990)**, NLR I/184, pp. 111–115 (open body):
   - Brenner "has undoubtedly provided a valuable corrective to those accounts of the transition to capitalism which, from Pirenne and Sweezy to Braudel and Wallerstein, have accorded prime importance to the expansion of the world market" (p. 111).
   - But Brenner's "exclusive focus on agrarian capitalism has encouraged … some wildly one-sided readings". Comninel's *Rethinking the French Revolution*, "based, incidentally, on a dissertation supervised by Wood", is "the most lamentable example" (p. 112).
   - Merchant capitalism, "systemic slavery" (Blackburn) and proto-industrialisation were "transitional forms" (p. 112).
   - "Wood's 'political Marxism' is little more than a sociology of domination" (p. 114).
   - "despite their disagreements, Brenner and Bois have greatly advanced our understanding of the form taken by the similar contradiction responsible for the late-medieval crisis of feudalism" (p. 113).
   - **A critique of Wood by name; it also links component 2 to component 5 (slavery).**
4. **McAuley (2001)**, "A Comment on Capitalist Origins", *Against the Current* 94 (Sept.–Oct. 2001), read in full.
   - Names "the Brenner-Wood thesis", "since she admittedly draws on Robert Brenner's findings and conclusions".
   - Puts "Maurice Dobb and Robert Brenner in the first group" (internal class relations), against "Samir Amin, Oliver Cox, C.L.R. James, Eric Williams, and Walter Rodney in the second" (trade and colonialism).
   - Reports J. M. Blaut's critique (*Eight Eurocentric Historians*, 2000) that the thesis treats colonialism as "a marginal process" and that capitalism arrives "complete and entire, as though it were a god descending from Olympus" (Blaut, p. 60, as McAuley quotes him).
   - Quotes *The Origin of Capitalism* with pages (pp. 7–8, 43, 46–47, 76, 82, 102): **mediated quotations of Wood's book.**
   - **Links component 2 to components 5 (Williams) and to the atlas's `dependency`/`anticolonial` entries.**
5. **Wood's replies** (both read in full, Marxists Internet Archive). "A Response to Critics on Capitalist Origins", ATC (March 2002), and "A Response to Christopher McAuley's 'On Capitalist Origins'", ATC (Nov. 2002).
   - "I don't even remotely recognize my argument, or Brenner's, in McAuley's repetition of James Blaut's accusation that people like us have a 'mystical' notion of capitalism".
   - Capitalism as "a specific social system with certain basic principles of motion and 'rules for reproduction' (to use Brenner's term)".
   - "Of course slave plantations in the British colonies played an important part in the development of capitalism (quite a while after the emergence of capitalism in the English countryside)".
   - "The contrast that interests me – and the one that Brenner has so clearly laid out – isn't between Europe and the rest of the world but between one specific European case (Brenner might say two: the English and the Dutch) against the rest of Europe".
6. **Harman–Brenner (2004 transcript)**, read in full: "Ellen Wood and her followers, for whom god visited England and inaugurated capitalist relations of production there and then abandoned the world … This is a wholly inadequate perspective. It is not Bob's."

No world-systems scholar's direct reply to Brenner or Wood was found open. Blaut's critique is known only through McAuley.

### 4b. Her own texts read (open)
1. **"The Separation of the Economic and the Political in Capitalism", NLR I/127 (May–June 1981).** Opening sections and notes read in Verso's authorised repost (25 February 2016); no journal pages. Cambridge Core opening of the 1995 reprint (*Democracy Against Capitalism*, ch. 1) also read.
   - Programme: Marxism should not perpetuate "the rigid conceptual separation of the 'economic' and the 'political' which has served capitalist ideology so well" (1995 opening).
   - Target: G. A. Cohen's technological determinism. "Precisely these criticisms of political economy have recently been used in an important book by G. A. Cohen to support an argument against a social interpretation of materialism". "what Cohen's technological determinism does is to repeat the error of the political economists: he generalizes the particular historical experience of capitalism", citing Brenner (n. 14).
   - **Edge use:** critique of Cohen. G. A. Cohen is a contributor on the `karl` entry, with a strand on productive-force primacy.
2. **The Pristine Culture of Capitalism (Verso, 1991).** Publisher's authorised extract read (Verso blog, 14 January 2016; no page numbers; from the book's opening argument).
   - Target: "the Nairn-Anderson theses", which explain British decline by "the precocity of English capitalism and its development in the shadow of tenacious antiquities".
   - Thompson: she sides with "arguments put forward by E.P. Thompson in the original debate", and his "persuasive charge that Nairn-Anderson operated with an abstractly idealized model of a 'Bourgeois Revolution' drawn - somewhat tautologically - from the experience of Other Countries".
   - Thesis: "Britain - or rather England - was the world's first capitalist society"; the Continental "hallmarks" of modernity "did not signal the maturity of 'bourgeois' or capitalist forces but on the contrary reflected the continuing strength of pre-capitalist social property relations … The principal case is France"; "Is Britain, then, a peculiar capitalism or is it peculiarly capitalist?"; "Britain may even be the most thoroughly capitalist culture in Europe."
   - **Edge use:** influence from E. P. Thompson (an atlas entry), as her explicit endorsement of his critique. Also a critique of Perry Anderson and Tom Nairn, neither of whom is an entry.
3. **The Origin of Capitalism (Monthly Review Press, 1999; expanded, Verso 2002).** **Unread.** Known through:
   - the OpenLibrary jacket description (read): it "challenges most existing accounts of capitalism's origins, arguing that they fail to recognize its distinctive attributes as a social system by making its emergence seem natural and inevitable"; "Only with a proper understanding of capitalism's beginning … can we imagine the possibility of it ending";
   - McAuley's quotations with pages (mediated). For example: "The purpose of this exercise is both scholarly and political. The naturalization of capitalism, which denies its specificity … limits our understanding of the past … Thinking about future alternatives to capitalism requires us to explore alternative conceptions of its past" (pp. 7–8, as McAuley quotes it);
   - Wood's own 2002 reply, which quotes the book on improving landlords (read).
4. **"Eurocentric Anti-Eurocentrism" (ATC 2001)** and the **two 2002 replies**: read in full (§3b, §3c).

**description:**
"A political theorist and historian, at York University in Toronto from 1967, who became the principal exponent of what Guy Bois, criticising Robert Brenner, had called 'political Marxism'. By the accounts of Alex Callinicos (1990) and Brenner (2016), she accepted the label while rejecting Bois's charge of voluntarism. 'The Separation of the Economic and the Political in Capitalism' (1981; revised for Democracy Against Capitalism, 1995) argued against G. A. Cohen's technological determinism that Marxism must not perpetuate 'the rigid conceptual separation of the "economic" and the "political"', since capitalism alone draws that line. The Pristine Culture of Capitalism (1991) turned the Nairn–Anderson theses of a backward Britain around: 'Britain - or rather England - was the world's first capitalist society', and the Continental 'modern' state signalled 'the continuing strength of pre-capitalist social property relations'. She sided there with E. P. Thompson's 'persuasive charge' against an idealised model of bourgeois revolution. In The Origin of Capitalism (1999; 2002) and 'Eurocentric Anti-Eurocentrism' (2001) she traced a line from Marx's 'so-called primitive accumulation' through the transition debate, in which Dobb and Hilton 'challenged the commercialization model', to Brenner, who explained 'the emergence of what he calls "market dependence"'. Against anti-Eurocentric histories she held that the confusion of commerce with capitalism was itself Eurocentric. Critics called her position 'little more than a sociology of domination' (Callinicos), faulted 'the Brenner-Wood thesis' for making colonialism marginal (McAuley, after Blaut), and took it to be a caricature of Brenner's (Harman). She answered that slave plantations mattered 'quite a while after the emergence of capitalism in the English countryside'. She also wrote that Christopher Hill's work 'inspired' Brenner's social interpretation of the English Revolution."

## 10. Summary

**Component 1 (the transition debate)** now has read evidence beyond the atlas strand: the whole Sweezy–Dobb exchange (1950, pp. 134–167). Three points are new:
- Sweezy's positive model: "production for use", trade as "the root cause" of feudalism's decline, and "pre-capitalist commodity production".
- Sweezy's explicit credit to Dobb.
- Dobb's argument that the seventeenth-century ruling class "was still feudal", with rising gentry and "kulaks" as "a most important driving force in the bourgeois revolution".

The line from Dobb–Sweezy to Brenner is stated in open texts by Hilton (1978; 1985), Wood (2001) and Harman (1989; 2004).

**Component 2 (the Brenner debate and political Marxism):**
- "Political Marxism" began as Bois's charge (1978; two open witnesses). Wood accepted it by the accounts of Callinicos (1990), Brenner (2016) and Hirschkop (2016); her own words were not read.
- Wood's 2001 essay is a primary, open statement of the whole lineage, Marx → transition debate → Brenner, and ties it to industrialisation and China (components 3–4).
- Critics: Seccombe (1983), Harman (1989; 2004), Callinicos (1990) and McAuley (2001, after Blaut). McAuley's critique ties component 2 to slavery and colonialism (component 5).
