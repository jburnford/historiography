### Peter Gay, interview excerpted from September 1997

https://cliospsyche.org/interviews/the-psychoanalytically-informed-historian-peter-gay

**Complete available interview read**, including its biographical introduction and closing interviewer credit. Interview by David Felix and Paul H. Elovitz with supplemental questions by Bob Lentz; the site explicitly calls it an excerpt from September 1997, not necessarily the complete printed interview. Local `/tmp/historiography-gay-interview.html` and `.txt`.

Gay distinguishes his historical practice from reductionist psychohistory and insists on external circumstances as well as inner life. His French Revolution example includes state bankruptcy, royal prestige and estate conflict; these are his methodological illustration, not a newly audited causal history. He recalls Freud reading around Franz Neumann, Neumann’s wife and Herbert Marcuse in 1950/1951; this is a named intellectual resource and participant recollection, not independently checked correspondence. Do not guess Neumann’s wife’s name from this passage. He dates his formal training to the mid-1970s and denies practicing as an analyst.

He discusses The Bourgeois Experience, Weimar Culture (1968), Freud for Historians (1985), Anna Freud’s defenses, Lacey Baldwin Smith’s Treason in Tudor England (1986), the Georges’ Wilson/House study, Dodds’s The Greeks and the Irrational (1951), John Demos’s Entertaining Satan and Maynard Solomon’s Mozart (1995). These are primary reception statements, not original-book readings. He values Demos’s contextual integration but wants psychology weighted more heavily. His clinical superiority claims, generalized diagnosis of Tudor people, predictions about postmodernism and claims of uniformly favorable Dodds reception remain attributed, not adopted.

**Edition trap:** Gay calls the Georges’ book 1964; that is a later edition. Search metadata identifies Alexander L. George and Juliette L. George; original 1956 requires a catalogue/title-page check before assigning precise first-publication metadata. Do not erase Juliette. Gay’s account of sparse reaction to Freud for Historians is his retrospective view, not proof that no reviews existed.

### Freud, The Origin and Development of Psychoanalysis (1910)

Public PDF: https://cdn-cms.f-static.com/uploads/1259807/normal_5b844b790f13a.pdf

Local `/tmp/historiography-freud-clark.pdf`, 39 pages, and `.txt`. It reproduces the York Classics in the History of Psychology transcription, with Raymond E. Fancher’s 1998 introduction/commentary and Dennis Nilsson’s 2006 ebook packaging. Original: American Journal of Psychology 21, pp. 181–218; lectures at Clark in September 1909; English translation by Harry W. Chase, revised by Freud. Official Freud edition metadata independently identifies original 1910: https://www.freudedition.net/en/ueber-psychoanalyse. The edition’s English-text link failed in web retrieval; York index https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Freud/Origin/index.htm returned 502.

**Complete Lectures I–II read**, PDF pp. 6–15, including their notes 1–10. Freud credits Breuer and recounts the Anna O. case; he distinguishes hypnoidal-state theory from his later conflict/resistance account, credits Charcot and Bernheim, and opposes the Janet view as he represents it. His clinical narration is historical authorial evidence, not a modern efficacy finding or verification of cure. His comparisons with monuments and a disruptive audience are illustrations, not independent case data. The Charing Cross etymology reported in note 9 is not adopted. Nor should his representation of Janet stand for Janet’s complete theory.

Lecture II explicitly warns against retroactively forcing Breuer’s hypnotically obtained case under the repression account. Freud’s later theory should therefore not be projected without qualification into every earlier case. The published Studies on Hysteria (1895; second edition 1909) is named, but that book has not been read here. Breuer treated Pappenheim; Freud did not.

**Completed additional reading:** Lectures III–V and notes (PDF pp. 16–30), so all five lectures pp. 6–30 are now read. Clinical, universal and normative claims remain attributed. **Still incomplete/unread:** Fancher’s introduction pp. 3–5 was partly read through a truncated output; do not claim it complete. Schapiro examines a specific application with sparse historical evidence, not psychoanalysis as a whole. ### Lloyd deMause, Foundations of Psychohistory, preface

https://psychohistory.com/books/foundations-of-psychohistory/preface/

**Complete preface read**, with roman page markers ii–v. Local `/tmp/historiography-demause-preface.html`, `.txt` (includes navigation), and `-body.txt` (14,062 characters, article only). Book year 1982 subsequently checked in the opened Google Books catalogue, https://books.google.com/books/about/Foundations_of_Psychohistory.html?id=fDlSAQAAIAAJ. This preface defines deMause’s program, not all psychohistory.

DeMause advocates introspective discovery followed by testing against historical material, a psychogenic theory of childrearing and group fantasy, and methodological individualism. His explicit reductionism differs from Gay’s position. He invokes Freud, Dilthey and Binion; opposes Durkheim/Marx and relativism as he characterizes them; and quotes critics Etheredge, Stone, Stannard and Hennock. Those critics’ originals remain unread. He distinguishes his aims from William Langer’s call to use psychoanalysis in history. His claims about being the only new twentieth-century social science, universal historical laws, groups, violence, maturity and clinical states are **attributed programmatic claims**, not established historical or clinical findings. His claim to avoid Whiggishness does not settle whether his sequence is teleological.
