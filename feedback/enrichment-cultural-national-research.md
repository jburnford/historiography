## Sources and precisely bounded readings

### burckhardt_renaissance_primary

Jacob Burckhardt, The Civilisation of the Renaissance in Italy (1860), S. G. C. Middlemore translation, selected chapters.

https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2074/2074-h/2074-h.htm

Complete translator preface, I.1, II.1 and V.6 read in the Gutenberg transcription: printed pp. iii–iv, 3–7, 129–133 and 395–401. Preface identifies translation from third German edition with Geiger additions and Valbusa material; do not treat it as an unaltered 1860 witness. Endnotes and original German not collated. Supports political conditions, individuality, interpretive categories and the upper-class women equality argument criticized by Kelly. Claims of civilizational superiority, gender character and medieval uniformity remain Burckhardt’s contestable assertions.

### huizinga_waning_primary

Johan Huizinga, The Waning of the Middle Ages (Dutch 1919; adapted F. Hopman English 1924), selected passages.

https://www.globalgreyebooks.com/online-ebooks/johan-huizinga_waning-of-the-middle-ages_complete-text.html

Complete April 1924 preface read; opening selections of chapters I (first 9500 characters), XIX and XXIII (first 5500 each) consulted. A clipped XIX passage was reread. Preface identifies author-directed adaptation, reduction and consolidation, not a complete translation of the Dutch editions. Cultural endings, formalized emotions, uneven survival of images/documents and overlapping medieval/classical forms are arguments, not established universal descriptions. Full chapters, original Dutch, omitted references and cited originals remain unread.

### huizinga_ludens_primary

Johan Huizinga, Homo Ludens (Dutch 1938), English edition 1949, 1980 reprint, chapter I pp. 1–9.

https://commons.princeton.edu/eng574-s23/wp-content/uploads/sites/348/2023/04/Huizinga_Homo-Ludens_Play-as-a-Cultural-Phenomenon.pdf

Title/copyright and chapter I pp. 1–9 read (PDF indices 0–10); clipped pp. 7–8 were repeated. Scan is a 29-page excerpt, not the whole book; preface absent. Copyright identifies English 1949 and 1980 reprint; DBNL title metadata independently identifies original Dutch 1938. Meaning, absorption, voluntary activity, social forms and fluid relations with seriousness are conceptual claims; animal behavior and civilizational rankings are not independently verified. Original Dutch and later chapters not collated.

### lamprecht_history_primary

Karl Lamprecht, What Is History? Five Lectures on the Modern Science of History, trans. E. A. Andrews (Macmillan, 1905), selected passages.

https://archive.org/details/whatishistoryfiv00lampuoft

Title/copyright, complete preface vii–viii, opening pp. 3–8 and pp. 14–22 read from archive OCR; neighboring passages partly read. Preface dates German addresses to autumn 1904 and credits translation/revision collaborators and psychological resources. Herder is explicitly acknowledged; Ranke and disciples are assessed with distinctions; Burckhardt is not equated with Lamprecht. His claim for universally dominant social-psychological forces and ranked cultural development remains a contested program, not a scientific finding. Full lectures, original German and cited interlocutors uncollated.

### michelet_preface_primary

Jules Michelet, Preface to History of France (1869), trans. Edward K. Kaplan in On History (Open Book Publishers, 2013), pp. 139–147.

https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/46/77/97/2019467797/2019467797.pdf

Title/copyright/contents checked; Kaplan’s complete introduction pp. 135–138 and Michelet pp. 139–147 read, including notes 3–4. Original French and remaining preface unread. Distinguish Kaplan’s retrospective interpretation from Michelet’s self-presentation. Vico is explicitly called a master; Mill is quoted against innate fixed differences; Thierry is admired and criticized. National organic unity, priority claims, gender metaphors and religion judgments are attributed. Publisher PDF URL returned empty content; LOC copy retrieved successfully. No full History of France or Revolution reading. Gossman’s foreword note 1 (PDF index 13) separately confirms the History of France original sequence, 1833–1867 in seventeen volumes, before the 1869 nineteen-volume edition.

### macaulay_history_primary

Thomas Babington Macaulay, The History of England from the Accession of James II, volume I, chapter I opening.

https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1468/1468-h/1468-h.htm

Complete first five programmatic paragraphs and selected following early-history discussion read in Gutenberg transcription; a clipped fourth paragraph was repeated. Describes intended constitutional, commercial and imperial progress together with crimes, failures, Ireland and everyday manners. It states the author’s project, not proof of its achievement. Exact print witness and full volume uncollated. Cambridge reprint metadata refers to an 1849 edition; no first-publication date is inferred from that reprint. Racial and civilizational hierarchies remain attributed.

### macaulay_minute_primary

Thomas Babington Macaulay, Minute on Indian Education, 2 February 1835, in H. Sharp, Selections from Educational Records I (1920; reprint 1965), pp. 107–117.

https://english.washington.edu/sites/english/files/documents/ewp/teaching_resources/minute_on_indian_education_1835_by_thomas_babington_macaulay.pdf

2018 printout of Columbia classroom transcription: complete numbered sections 1–15 and 31–36 read, with partial section 16 and boundary text of 30; middle sections unread. Final date, Bentinck concurrence and source credit checked. Numbering is modern classroom apparatus, not original. Supports the allocation-of-funds argument, preference for English, imperial intermediaries and devaluation of existing literatures/history. These are Macaulay’s claims; his assertions of agreement and superiority are not verified facts. This minute is not itself evidence of uniform implementation or reception.

### bancroft_prefaces_primary

https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2001.05.0326

Complete first-edition preface dated 16 June 1834 and third-edition preface dated 1 May 1838 read from Tufts transcription, which identifies Boston, Little, Brown, 1859 witness. Bancroft combines comparison of contemporary testimony with claims that colonial institutions contain national liberty’s beginnings; he solicits libraries, sources and criticism. These are stated methods and national interpretations, not proof that he represents all inhabitants or implements impartiality. Main chapters and original printings remain unread.
