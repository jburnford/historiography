## Mary Ritter Beard

Read the complete preface to *Woman as Force in History* (1946), signed summer 1945, in Andy Blunden’s Marxists Internet Archive transcription: <https://www.marxists.org/archive/beard/woman-force/preface.htm>. The transcription adds an article to the title; established bibliographic title remains unchanged. It challenges the tradition of women as a universally subject sex and sets out legal, economic and other inquiries, particularly common law versus equity and criticism of equality as a sufficient guide. These are Beard’s historical arguments, not an independently verified account of legal rights or proof that agency abolishes subordination.

Opening portions of chapters I and X were consulted. Chapter I treats women’s participation across political regimes; do not present all agency as liberatory. Its wartime press claim about Gertrud Scholtz-Klink’s death is not adopted as established fact. Chapter X opens with broad claims about medieval work and uses manuals and other documentary forms; these claims have not been independently tested. Neither complete chapter nor the original printing was collated. Local files: `/tmp/historiography-beard-preface.html`, `-ch01.html/.txt`, `-ch10.html/.txt`. An initial UTF-8 decode failed on chapter X; parsing bytes with BeautifulSoup succeeded. Search found no named Power/Clark/Pinchbeck/Spruill/Salmon resource in chapter X; absence there is not a whole-book conclusion.

Read Anke Voss-Hubbard, “No Documents—No History”: Mary Ritter Beard and the Early History of Women’s Archives, *American Archivist* 58/1 (1995), 16–30, completely: <https://american-archivist.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/aarc/58/1/article-p16.pdf>. Browser access failed and the first curl returned 502; an escalated retry succeeded. All pages, including output gaps at 21–22 and 26–27, were read. Local PDF/text: `/tmp/historiography-beard-archives.pdf/.txt`.

The article distinguishes Schwimmer’s feminist-pacifist proposal, Beard’s broader WCWA mission, internal disagreement, funding failures and later institutional collecting. Pages 20–21 discuss Sue Bailey Thurman, Mary McLeod Bethune and NCNW cooperation, with no Black directors and minimal resources for committee fieldwork. This is a specific institutional criticism, not a claim of Black women’s absence from archival work or a direct critique of the later 1946 book. Voss-Hubbard’s cited Collier-Thomas essay and original correspondence remain mediated. Pages 24–30 reconstruct Grierson’s collecting, donor relations, catalogue/access decisions and uneven teaching/administrative support at Smith. Formation in 1942 differs from the 1946 Sophia Smith name. Grierson’s generous attribution to Beard does not license sole founding. Beard supported preservation of some people’s papers despite disagreement with their arguments.

The article also documents Charles/Mary coauthorship of *The Rise of American Civilization* (1927), *America in Midpassage* (1939) and other works. The exact division of authorship remains undetermined; women’s-history work is not folded into Charles’s constitutional interpretation.

## Julia Cherry Spruill

Read Julie Richter’s complete H-SAWH review, March 1999, from original H-Net HTML: <https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=2870>. Browser returned 403; curl succeeded. Local file `/tmp/historiography-richter-spruill.html`. This is primary evidence of Richter’s reception and mediated evidence of Spruill’s text and Scott’s introductions.

Richter quotes the original preface, p. xvii in the reissue, explaining the move from attitudes toward women to empirical groundwork on everyday life and social institutions. She describes households, work, public activity, law, urban/rural differences and status. Her quotation from p. 208 differentiates books women might have read and prescribed conduct from established reading or behavior. No original book passage or prefatory printing has been independently collated.

The review explicitly says Black and Native women are mentioned but do not become sustained questions about life and work. Preserve that distinction. It documents Colonial Williamsburg’s use, particularly for nonelite white women and status. Richter’s hoped-for use of footnotes in her own slavery project is future intent, not completed use. Her description of friendship between Beard and Spruill does not prove a transmission; the “force” phrase is Richter’s borrowing.

Scott’s 1972 and revised 1998 introductions are documented by the review. Smithsonian Libraries metadata checks *Unheard Voices* (1993), including Spruill’s “Conjugal felicity” and domestic discord: <https://www.si.edu/object/unheard-voices-first-historians-southern-women-edited-anne-firor-scott%3Asiris_sil_446960>. The collection’s introduction/texts remain unread. AHR 44/4/923 and JAH DOI 10.2307/1892508 are early-review leads not read here. Do not infer a Schlesinger mentorship or broad later-school influence from an unconsulted review.

## Lucy Maynard Salmon

Read title/copyright leaves, complete original preface vii–xiv, second-edition preface xv and chapters I–II pp. 1–15 of *Domestic Service*: <https://www.gutenberg.org/files/73921/73921-h/73921-h.htm>. Original 1897; second edition 1901 adds the European chapter. Local HTML/text `/tmp/historiography-salmon-domestic.html/.txt`. The original preface begins before the second “PREFACE” text occurrence; searches must not mistake the second edition for the original.

The 1889/1890 questionnaire distribution uses Vassar classes/alumnae, associations and clubs. Salmon reports 1,025 employer returns, including twenty after tabulation, covering 2,545 workers; 719 employee returns and about 200 miscellaneous returns. She records privacy concerns, fears of agitation, question burden, inability to answer and workers’ reluctance to sign an unclear document. The Massachusetts bureau collates tables in 1890. These procedures and her own representativeness claims do not constitute modern probability sampling; no calculations were independently audited.

Wright assists with schedules and advance census information; Wadlin and Pidgin receive acknowledgments for bureau assistance. Underhill reads manuscript and proofs and prepares the index. The wider respondent and college networks remain explicitly credited. Other acknowledged helpers (Dewey, Talbot, Mary Roberts Smith, Backus and others) remain in the primary source; this roster is selective, not a census of every acknowledgment.

Chapters I–II challenge the reduction of domestic employment to private complaints and relate it to industrial specialization and changing household production. Salmon’s strong historical generalizations, class/national assumptions and judgments of useful labor remain arguments to assess, not universal historical facts. The work includes proposed remedies but differentiates them from evidence; do not say it offers none.

Read title/copyright and complete Apologia v–xiv of *Progress in the Household* (1906): <https://www.gutenberg.org/files/64287/64287-h/64287-h.htm>. Local `/tmp/historiography-salmon-progress.html/.txt`. It explains the Vassar teaching assignment, delayed census publication and hoped-for audiences, and invokes Moses Coit Tyler’s teaching about judging a work by its intended purpose. Salmon’s guesses about economists’ and historians’ reactions are a self-account, not named contemporary reviews. Other essays remain unread.

The Vassar institutional biography was reread with its source list: <https://vcencyclopedia.vassar.edu/faculty/prominent-faculty/lucy-maynard-salmon/>. It documents critical teaching, student independence, everyday material evidence, institutional conflict and later newspaper books. Do not adopt its retrospective “new social history” as formal membership or its bibliography’s 1987 typo for *Domestic Service*. Its contemporary-review location, Box 49 Folder 8–8A, is a concrete access lead, not a consulted review. Later newspaper books and original teaching papers remain unread.

Vassar’s New York Heritage collection description identifies Adelaide Underhill’s student/librarian partnership and documentary collection: <https://nyheritage.org/collections/lucy-maynard-salmon-and-adelaide-underhill-collection>. Individual letters and other collection objects are not newly read; interpretive claims about intentional preservation are not independently established.
